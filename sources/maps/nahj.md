# Source map: Nahj al-Balagha (Qutbuddin, Brill 2024)

Extract: `sources/extracts/nahj.md`. Page numbers are the PDF `[p. N]` markers. Quotes are exact from the extract. The edition has no Arabic-free reading copy, so Arabic lines interleave with English on some pages (for example pp. 477 and 479); ignore them. No Arabic is reproduced here.

## 1. Edition notes

**Numbering (verified in the Detailed Contents, pp. 71–107, and the body):**
- Format is `chapter.text[.paragraph]`. Chapter 1 is Orations (sermons, addresses, prayers). Chapter 2 is Letters. Chapter 3 is Sayings. Long texts are subdivided: `1.191` is the whole Hammam oration, `1.219.1` is its first paragraph, `2.53.7` is paragraph 7 of the Testament of Ashtar.
- Ranges:
  - Orations: 1.1–1.232 (Radi's original), plus "Additional Orations" 1.233–1.238.
  - Letters: 2.1–2.78, plus "Additional Letter" 2.79.
  - Sayings: 3.1–3.429, plus "Additional Sayings" 3.430–3.446. (p. 834 gives the count as 17 later-added sayings.)
- Her numbering is her own. She follows manuscript "Sequence 1" for orations. The main text of Radi (the compiler) is kept apart from the additions.
- Radi's own comments are marked "Radi:" in the text.
- Footnotes give context, dating and sources. Appendix of Sources (about pp. 870 ff.) lists parallel citations per text number.

**Concordance with the common (Subhi Salih / Abduh / Faydh al-Islam) numbering:**
- **The edition contains NO concordance table.** Grep for "concordance", "Subhi" and "Faydh" finds only bibliography entries and a note (a footnote on previous scholarship, near line 887 of the extract) pointing to external concordances. Her section on previous editions (pp. 63–64) cites Abduh and Ibn Abi'l-Hadid by their section numbers only to flag two textual variants.
- **Anchors that can be verified inside the edition:**
  - Her 3.174 equals Abduh §190 and Ibn Abi'l-Hadid §185 (pp. 63–64 n. 41–43). Her 3.237 equals Abduh §252 (n. 43). So Abduh's sayings numbering runs about 15–16 ahead of hers in this range.
  - The lecturer's "432" is her 3.403 by content (see section 3). That is a gap of 29, so the offset grows through the Sayings.
- **From memory only, not in the edition [VERIFY against Subhi]:**
  - Letters seem to coincide with Subhi: her 2.31 (Testament to Hasan), 2.45 (Ibn Hunayf) and 2.53 (Ashtar) match the usual numbers.
  - Orations coincide with Subhi only in the early range, for example 1.1, 1.3 (Shiqshiqiyya), 1.28 and 1.45. They then drift by about -2 to -3 in the 190s and 220s. The Qasi'a is her 1.190 and Hammam is 1.191.
  - **Do not convert numbers by arithmetic.** Cite her number, and add the Subhi number only after checking a second edition.

## 2. Theme map

Format: **number, page** (PDF). Paraphrase, then a short quote where striking.

### Knowing God and the limits of describing Him
- **1.1.1, p. 122** (the first sermon). "The first part of religion is knowledge of God." A chain follows: knowledge, belief, oneness, sincerity, negating attributes. It ends in the claim that describing God is dividing Him. "God is a being but not by coming into being." "He is with all things but not by association. He is other than all things but not by detachment."
- **1.1.2, p. 124.** God creates "without long mulling, prior experience, generated movement, or stirring aspiration."
- **1.49, p. 188.** "Praise God, who is concealed in unseen mysteries but confirmed by clear signs." The eye that does not see Him cannot deny Him, and "the heart that acknowledges him cannot comprehend him."
- **1.82.1, p. 230.** God is first and last. "Hearts cannot comprehend his nature."
- **1.177, p. 418–420** (reply to Dhi'lib: "have you seen your Lord?"). "Eyes do not see him through physical observation, but hearts perceive him with true belief." "He is near to all things without touching them, yet far from them without being apart."
- **1.184.1, p. 438.** Radi's heading calls it an oration "that brings together principles of knowledge like no other". "Those who assign him a form have not acknowledged his oneness."
- **3.437, p. 836.** Declaring God's oneness "means not imagining him".

### The friends of God (awliya') and their inner vision
- **3.403, p. 826.** The key text (see section 3). God's special devotees "perceive the world's reality when others are deceived by her appearance" and "occupy themselves with what is coming when others occupy themselves with the present". A footnote says this text is also attributed to Jesus in Abu Nu'aym's Hilyat.
- **3.133, p. 736–738** (to Kumayl). Hearts are vessels. Earth is never without those who uphold God's proof. "Knowledge has led them to real perception, enabling them to touch the spirit of certainty." They are "God's vicegerents on his earth". "Ah! Would that I could see them!"
- **1.219.1, p. 522–524.** God speaks to chosen servants whose minds He engages, so "they draw back the veil for the living, until those who heed them see what most cannot see and hear what most cannot hear."
- **1.217, p. 516.** A pious man "resuscitated his intellect and killed his sentient soul". "Lightning shone in a brilliant flash that illuminated the road for him."
- **1.185, p. 444.** Praise of a host "whose names are recognized in the heavens but remain unknown on earth".
- **3.350, p. 806.** "A true believer looks at the world with eyes that reflect on it."

### The world (dunya) and detachment
- **1.28, p. 164.** "The world has shown its back and proclaimed its farewell." Today is training, tomorrow the race.
- **1.79, p. 218.** The world "instructs those who view her with discernment and blinds those who look at her with longing."
- **1.100.1, p. 274.** "Look at the world with the eyes of those who have little interest in her."
- **1.108.1, p. 294.** A warning against the world, who "deceives, harms, changes, ceases, wanes, perishes, and consumes".
- **1.223, p. 530.** A residence "encircled by trials and renowned for deceit", where earlier generations fell silent.
- **3.120, p. 730–732** (to a man blaming the world). The world is "a house of truth for all who stay true to her". "She is a mosque for God's loved ones."
- **3.221, p. 760.** The world is worth less than "the bones of a pig in the hand of a leper."
- **3.409, p. 828.** Renunciation is two Quranic injunctions: do not weep over loss, do not exult over gain.
- **2.45.1–5, pp. 626–630** (to Ibn Hunayf). Ali's own austerity: "his two shabby garments and his two pieces of bread". The world is dismissed ("Get away from me!").

### Self-knowledge and the nafs
- **1.220.1, p. 524.** "Your ignorance of yourself is extreme." It asks the human to feel compassion for his own soul as he does for others.
- **1.155.2, p. 372.** "Fear God and look to the direction of your own soul." The self surveils you and your limbs spy on you.
- **1.236, p. 542.** Gather provisions "by the self that is living for the self that will soon be dead". The believer bridles the "sentient soul".
- **1.191, p. 480** (Hammam). "If their ego bucks against doing something it dislikes, they do not give it free rein."
- **3.192, p. 752.** "Whoever takes himself to task profits, whoever neglects his soul loses."
- **3.65, p. 710.** A leader should teach himself before others.
- **1.147, p. 352.** "Each person shall meet what he flees as he flees."
- There is no saying here matching "he who knows himself knows his Lord" (grep for the wording finds nothing). Do not attribute it to this edition.

### Remembrance
- **1.219.1, pp. 522–524.** "God has made his remembrance the burnish for your hearts" (the sermon on Quran 24:37). With it "you can hear after your earlier deafness, see after your earlier night-blindness". This is the Barzakh sermon (see death below).
- **1.222–224, pp. 530–532** (prayers). 1.224: "your remembrance gives them comfort" when loneliness makes them fearful.
- **1.191, p. 480.** "They spend the night thanking God and the morning praising him."
- **1.155, p. 370** (opening). "Praise God, who made praise the key to his remembrance" (heading in the contents, p. 87).
- **1.107, p. 292.** Heading: "Worship rites and God's remembrance".
- **1.80.9, p. 224.** The sage "whose tongue remembrance spurs on".

### Night vigil
- **1.191, p. 478** (Hammam). "In the night they stand in worship and recite sections of the Quran." They weep, find "the cure for their illness" in the verses, and lay forehead, palms, knees and toes on the ground.
- **1.94.4, p. 266.** The Companions "spent the night in prostration and prayer".
- **1.80.9, p. 224.** "whose snatches of sleep in the watches of night are interrupted by prayer."
- **1.118, p. 312.** Departed brothers "pale from wakeful nights".
- **1.111.2, p. 302.** Fear "has made their nights wakeful and filled their days with thirst".
- **1.158.3, p. 378.** Of Jesus: "his lamp at night the moon".

### Fear and hope
- **3.73, p. 714.** "Place your hopes in no one save your Lord. Fear nothing save your sins."
- **1.158.2, p. 380.** "Every fear you feel is real except your fear of God, which is a sham." It rebukes hope in God that does not show in action.
- **3.26, p. 700.** Faith's four pillars. The branches of forbearance are longing, fear, rejection of worldliness and patient expectation.
- **1.191, p. 478.** "yearning for God's reward and fearing his punishment". Paradise and Hell are "before their eyes".
- **1.45, p. 186.** God is "never doubtful of his forgiveness".
- **3.222, p. 760.** Three kinds of worship: of merchants (desire for reward), of slaves (fear), of the free (thanks).
- **3.285, p. 788.** "As long as I can pray for forgiveness, I don't fear punishment for past sins."

### Love
Love of God is thinly expressed here as a theme. The best passages are:
- **1.224, p. 532.** "God! You are the greatest comforter for those who love you." Their hearts "long for you".
- **3.120, p. 732.** The world is "a mosque for God's loved ones".
- **1.191, p. 478.** The pious "yearning" for the verse and the reward.
- **3.222, p. 760.** Worship of thanks (above) as the highest kind.
- **3.102, p. 724.** "Were a mountain to love me, it would crumble!" This concerns the cost of loving the Ahl al-Bayt.
- **[QUERY]** The lecturer says Nahj is "full of love of God" (L11 ¶60). A keyword search finds few explicit passages. Ask which texts he means.

### Death and the barzakh
- **1.219.1, pp. 522–524.** The only passage with the word: the remembrancers behave "as though they have already witnessed what is beyond the grave. You would think they were privy to the mysteries of the Barzakh." Her footnote gives the Barzakh as the "intermediary station" before the final end.
- **1.109, p. 298.** The Angel of Death. "How can we who are incapable of describing a creature like us aspire to describe our creator?"
- **1.218, p. 516.** On horrors of death (Quran 102).
- **3.119, p. 730.** At the cemetery: "O people of desolate abodes, ruined quarters, and dark tombs!" The dead, if allowed to answer, would say "The best provision is piety."
- **3.112, p. 728.** At a funeral: "We behave as though death were decreed for everyone other than ourselves."
- **1.147, p. 352.** "death is the place toward which the soul is driven".
- **1.80.9, p. 224.** "You too must cross the bridge of death."

### The people of taqwa (Hammam, al-Muttaqin)
See section 4. Key lines:
- **1.191, p. 478.** "Paradise is before their eyes—they see it as clearly as though they were already enjoying its blessings."
- **1.191, p. 478.** "Their hearts are at peace, both in times of tribulation, and in times of prosperity."
- **1.191, p. 480.** "They sleep vigilant and awake in joy."
- **1.191, p. 480.** Their detachment "is not out of arrogance or grandiosity".

### Knowledge and the heart
- **3.99, p. 722.** "There is a piece of flesh attached to the jugular vein that is the human's most wondrous organ—the heart." It lists what lifts and sinks it.
- **3.189, p. 752.** "Every vessel constricts as it fills up, except the vessel of knowledge, which expands."
- **3.133, p. 736.** "these hearts are like vessels, and the best are those that best receive and store."
- **3.82, p. 716.** "The highest form of knowledge manifests in limbs and appendages."
- **3.323, p. 800.** Knowledge is of two kinds, innate and acquired. The acquired gives no benefit without the innate.
- **3.249, p. 770.** "Faith begins as a shining spot in the heart."
- **3.349, p. 806.** Knowledge is linked with action.
- **3.177, p. 748.** "a heart turns blind when coerced."
- **3.403, p. 826.** Mentions the Book recognised through the devotees and the devotees through the Book.

### The Imams and the Ahl al-Bayt as guides
- **1.2.2, p. 132.** "They are the embodiments of God's mystery, the foundations of his creed, the vessels of his knowledge." "No one from this community may be compared to Muhammad's family."
- **1.106.5, p. 292.** "We are the tree of prophecy... mines of knowledge and springs of wisdom."
- **1.117, p. 310.** "We—the people of his Prophet's house—possess the keys to wisdom and the light of command."
- **1.235, p. 542.** "They are the life of knowledge and the death of ignorance."
- **1.147, p. 352.** Final testament: hold fast to God and Muhammad, the "two pillars", the "two lamps".
- **3.133, p. 738.** Earth never lacks one who upholds God's proof.
- **3.100, p. 724.** "We are the saddle-pad in the middle."
- **1.230, p. 538.** "It is we, the Prophet's family, who are the princes of language."
- **1.190.12, pp. 474–476** (Qasi'a). Ali's closeness to the Prophet: hearing Satan's cry at revelation; "You see what I see and hear what I hear".

### Good character
- **3.33, p. 704** (to Hasan). "The noblest lineage is good character." Beware befriending a fool, a miser, a wanton or a liar.
- **3.103, p. 724.** "no companion like a beautiful character".
- **3.26, p. 700.** The four pillars of faith: forbearance, conviction, justice, struggle against evil.
- **3.73, p. 714.** Five traits, including being unashamed to say "I don't know" and to learn.
- **1.191, p. 480** (Hammam). "They forgive those who oppress them, give to those who refuse them, and foster those who cut them off."
- **2.27, p. 574.** To Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr: "Lower your wing over them".
- **2.31, p. 588.** The Testament to Hasan, lengthy counsel.

## 4. Hammam sermon (Sermon of the Pious)

- **Present: yes, as 1.191, pp. 476–480** (English on pp. 476, 478, 480; Arabic on 477 and 479). Heading in the contents is "Oration to Hammam, describing the truly pious" (Detailed Contents, p. 90). Radi's ending is on p. 480.
- **Frame (p. 476).** Hammam, "devoted to worship", asked Ali to describe the pious "such that I see them before my eyes!" Ali hesitated, then gave a short answer from Quran 16:128 ("God is with those who are pious and do good"). Hammam persisted, and Ali delivered the oration.
- **Structure.**
  - p. 476: God does not need obedience.
  - p. 478: the pious in the day and night; virtues of speech, dress, gait, gaze and hearing; the night vigil; fear of the Fire; refusing praise ("I know myself better than you know me").
  - p. 478–480: a list of hallmarks.
  - p. 480: the day-to-day character and conduct with people.
- **Ending (p. 480, Radi's note).** Hammam "fell as though struck by a thunderbolt and died then and there". Ali said "By God, I feared this effect on him!" and, when challenged, said every lifespan has an appointed time and that the challenger's tongue was Satan's.
- **Context.** Footnote 2 (p. 476) cites her two articles on this text: "Piety and Virtue in Early Islam: Two Sermons by Imam Ali" and "The Ultimate Response by Hammam to Ali's Sermon on Piety". It also notes a source reading Hammam's request as about Ali's true followers (shi'ah). This helps with the book's point about the path.
- **Use in the book.** The best single Nahj text for the irfan themes: night prayer, fear and hope, no pride in praise, and the pious who see Paradise and Hell as present. It also has the line of the soul not lingering without the appointed term. A very strong candidate for the chapters on setting out and on the five disciplines.
