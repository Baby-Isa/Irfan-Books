 Who was Rabi’a?

 Rabi'a was a freedwoman of the Al-Atiq, a tribe of
  Qays b. Adi, from which she was known as al-
  Adawiyya or al-Qaysiyya, and since she was born in
  Basra she was also known as al-Basriyya.

 Rabi’a was born probably about 95 or 99 A.H. in the
  poorest of homes (according to Attar) in Basra, where
  she spent the greater part of her life.

 Her father already had three daughters, and so she
  was called Rabi'a (= the fourth).
Rabi’a
 We can find material on her only in the Memoir of the Saints of Attar
   who lived more than four hundred years after Rabi'a. Much of what he
   tells of her must be regarded as purely legendary.

 Rabi´a al-Adawiyya is an Islamic saint. She is believed to have lived
   from 717 to 801 AD in what is now Iraq. While a poor orphan she was
   captured by a slave trader who proceeded to sell her into slavery. As a
   slave she was kept busy with her household duties until night, but once
   night time released from her chores she devoted herself to prayer,
   going without sleep to do so. One night her master caught sight of her
   absorbed in prayer; he was astonished to see a light miraculously
   appear over her head which illuminated the entire house. Terrified he
   went back to his room, where he sat in wonder till daybreak. At dawn
   he approached Rabi´a, told her what he had seen, and gave her her
   freedom.

 When she became renowned, she received numerous offers of
   marriage.
Rabi’a
   As an early mystic emerging in Basra her mysticism had common features with that of
    Basra mystics, namely the austere asceticism known from Hasan al-Basri and others.
    During an illness, a friend asked her if she desired anything. "...[H]ow can you ask me such
    a question as 'What do I desire?' I swear by the glory of God that for twelve years I have
    desired fresh dates, and you know that in Basra dates are plentiful, and I have not yet
    tasted them. I am a servant (of God), and what has a servant to do with desire?" It was her
    custom to pray all night, sleep briefly just before dawn, and then rise again just as dawn
    "tinged the sky with gold."
   However, her mystical path became quite distinguished from early Basra school which was
    renowned for its fearful attitude. She taught that repentance was a gift from God because
    no one could repent unless God had already accepted him and given him this gift of
    repentance. She taught that sinners must fear the punishment they deserved for their sins,
    but she also offered such sinners far more hope of Paradise than most other ascetics did.
    For herself, however, she held to a higher ideal, worshipping God neither from fear of Hell
    nor for hope of Paradise, for she saw such self-interest as unworthy of God's servants;
    emotions like fear and hope were like veils -- i.e., hindrances to the vision of God Himself.
    The story is told that once a number of Sufis saw her hurrying on her way with water in one
    hand and a burning torch in the other. When they asked her to explain, she said: "I want to
    through fire in Paradise and to pour water on to Hell, so that these two veils disappear and
    it becomes clear who worships God out of love, not out of fear of Hell or hope for Paradise."
    Rabi’a
   Margaret Smith tells us that Rabi'a began her ascetic life in a small desert cell
    near Basra, where she lost herself in prayer and went straight to God for
    teaching. As far as is known, she never studied under any master or spiritual
    director.
    She was one of the first of the Sufis to teach that Love alone was the guide on
    the mystic path. As her holiness became more widely known, numerous
    individuals beat a path to her door seeking her spiritual direction.

   The path of Rabi’a in her mystical understanding, which stood out very brilliantly,
    was the path of zealous love for God. She is generally regarded as the person
    who introduced the element of selfless love into the austere teachings of the
    early ascetics and gave mysticism the hue of true mysticism.

   through him Hellenistic Philosophical ideas penetrated into
   Sufism.

Tirmidhi’s main contribution to the mystical theory is probably his
   “Seal of the Saints”, in which he developed the terminology of
   sainthood that has been used since that time. Thus the leader
   of the Sufi hierarchy was qutb, “pole” or “pivot”, or ghauth,
   “help”.

According to him the saints govern the universe. Certain groups of
  three, seven, forty or three hundred saints are entrusted with
  various duties in maintaining the world order.

Like the Prophets whose seal is Muhammad (sa), the saints also
   have their seal.
   Mysticism of gnosis (ma’rifa)

The degree of sainthood as sketched by Tirmidhi are
  related to the degrees of illumination and gnosis
  reached by the Sufi- it is not a “hierarchy of love.”

His emphasis on gnosis, ma’rifa, prepares the way for
  later theosophical speculation.

Abu Yazid (Bayezid) Bistami (d. 252/874)

His famous shat’h (‫ )شطح‬which has fascinated many mystics after him was
   “subhani ma a’adhama sha’ni ” “Praise be to Me, how great is My
   Majesty!”

Bayezid practised mortification and emptying himself of himself, until he had
   reached, at least for a moment, the world of absolute unity, where as he
   said, lover, beloved, and love are one; and where he himself is the wine,
   the wine-drinker, and the cupbearer.

Junayd (d. 288/910), the leader of Baghdad school, was his student, although
   believed that Bayezid did not reach the final goal of the seeker.

Hallaj, his associate, believed that “poor Abu Yazid” had arrived only at the
   threshold of the divine.

His name occurs in poetry more than that of any other mystic, with the
   exception of Mansur al-Hallaj.

Like many other mystics he came from Iran

He emphasised on the state of sobriety ( sahw) as contrasted to Bayezid’s

   and annihilates man completely in God
        al-Junayd al-Baghdadi (d.
                298/920)
Junayd , however, considered the “second sobriety” the highest
  and the preferable state. According to him, after the ecstatic
  intoxication man becomes once more aware of himself in the
  “life in God,” when all his attributes, transformed and
  spiritualised, are restored to him.

According to Junayd, fana, ‘annihilation’ is not the ultimate goal,
  but baqa, ‘remaining’, a new life in God.

He was critical of mystics like Hallaj who spoke openly about
  mystical experience and developed instead a language of
  allusions (isharat) to speak about the subject that makes it
  unintelligible for the outsiders.

At the age of 12 the ‘Great Shaykh’ narrates what took place:


“One day I fell seriously ill and went into a coma, so
serious that everyone thought me dead. And then I
saw (in a vision) some horrible-looking people who
were seeking to hurt me. I noticed a person, full of
grace and strength, breathing out a wonderful
perfume which defended me against them and drove
them away. “Who are you?” I asked, and this being
replied: “I am the Sura Yâ’ Sīn, I am your protector.”
Then I regained consciousness to find my father,
may God have mercy on him, at my bedside in tears;
he had just finished reciting Sura Yâ’sīn.”
                           INSPIRATIONS

    Ibn ‘Arabi claimed that he wrote through inspiration. He
    authored many great books which according to him originated
    from divine inspiration. The mystical experiences he went
    through in life, were his inspirations to write, often attempting to
    explain those experiences.

Examples:

   ‘al-Futūhat al-Makkīyya’ (The Meccan Illuminations):
    It is an encyclopedic work founded on the Qur’an and
    discussion of Islamic wisdom viewed from the perspective of
    the stages of the mystic path.

   ‘Fusūs al-Hikâm’ (The Bezels of Wisdom): This he says
    he received directly from the Prophet Mohammed (pbuh), who
    appeared to him in Damascus in a dream in the year 1129 (AH
    627).
