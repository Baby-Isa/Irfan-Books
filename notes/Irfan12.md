# Lecture 12: Early Sufi Dispositions II: Muhasibi, the Mysticism of Renunciation (Zuhd), and the Mysticism of Love (Rabiʿa, Dhul-Nun)

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan12.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
Continuing the survey of early Sufi "dispositions" (fear, love, renunciation, maʿrifa) from the previous week, the lecturer adds al-Harith al-Muhasibi as a bridge figure, then treats the mysticism of renunciation (Ibrahim b. Adham, Fudayl b. Iyad, Shaqiq al-Balkhi) and the mysticism of love (Maruf al-Karkhi, Rabiʿa al-Adawiyya, Dhul-Nun al-Misri). The recurring argument is that early mysticism developed its technical vocabulary and its stages in the 2nd and 3rd centuries AH, that many of these figures were linked to the Imams, and that the Imams stand on a categorically higher level than the mystics (¶37). Love, in the lecturer's view, is "the heart of mysticism" and was missing in the earlier fear and asceticism schools (¶56). The "gnostic" disposition (maʿrifa) is deferred to next week (¶65). (¶3, ¶9, ¶38, ¶65)

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Recap: dispositions of the Sufis (¶3)
- Different Sufis have different dominant inclinations: overwhelming fear, overwhelming love, powerful renunciation of duniya, or pursuit of maʿrifa (knowledge of God) above all. (¶3)
- Fear was illustrated last time by Hasan al-Basri. (¶3)
- Caveat: "fear" or "love" mysticism does not mean exclusive possession of that trait, only that they "gave more preference to this concept rather than others". Some Sufis were not outwardly zahid at all but were zahid "in their hearts". (¶9)

### 2.2 Al-Harith al-Muhasibi and muhasaba (¶3–8)
- Important because he influenced later mysticism, above all Ghazali, who lived about three centuries after him and read his writings. (¶3, ¶5)
- Born in Basra (date given as "781 AH", see section 7); probably influenced by Hasan al-Basri's teaching, since Basra-born. (¶4)
- His name is derived from muhasaba: the constant analysis of "even the most secret motions of the soul and the heart". This is still a living practice: counting and calculating one's actions, feelings and attitudes nightly, weekly or periodically. (¶4)
- He taught a relentless fight against the lower nature: not just the ascetic's struggle against the flesh but "subtle psychological analysis". He wrote early books on mystical psychology. His subtle analysis of riyaʾ (ostentation) became "fundamental and indispensable" to early and later Sufis. (¶5)
- His influence is visible on the Malamatiyya ("people of blame"). (¶5)
- Extreme consequence of the anti-riyaʾ stress: some Sufis deliberately incurred blame, e.g. pious people drinking wine publicly, or pretending to drink while drinking water, so that people would call them fasiq, fajir, kafir. This bred contempt for Sufis, who were accused of ignoring the Sharia. (¶6–7)
- Later Sufis "corrected" this: not wanting praise yet seeking blame is still acting for the sake of people. (¶7–8)
- Shaytan's subtlety: "Whatever you do, Shaitan finds a way", e.g. some did blameworthy acts to show other Sufis that they were beyond riyaʾ. (¶8)
- Much of the technical language of mysticism was developed in the 2nd and 3rd centuries, starting with or before muhasaba. (¶8)

### 2.3 Mysticism of zuhd: the figures (¶9–11)
- Examples: Ibrahim b. Adham (d. 162 AH), Fudayl b. Iyad, Shaqiq al-Balkhi. All somehow related to the Imams. (¶9–10)
- Ibrahim b. Adham was a contemporary of Imam Sadiq and Imam Kazim. He was a prince in Balkh; the ascetics came from Khorasan, "the land of asceticism". (¶9–10)
- Junayd of Baghdad (4th century, a "turning point" and "architect" of mystical understanding and jargon, honoured by Shia and Sunni mystics alike) called Ibrahim "the key of mystical sciences". (¶10–11)
- Ibrahim learned from Imam Sadiq while young, left his princely life with no provisions, "seeking the pleasure of God", and became proverbial for poverty, absence of possessions and tawakkul. (¶11)
- Parallel drawn with the Buddha: a princely background, then desert or jungle, preaching, illumination. "Usually they correspond between Abrahim Adham and Buddha." (¶11–12)
- A book "Ismaul Shayaat Imam Sadiq" is mentioned in connection with Shaqiq; some say it was written by Shaqiq and attributed to Imam Sadiq. Deferred ("we'll come to that later"). (¶10)

### 2.4 Ibrahim's three levels of zuhd and the Imams' teaching (¶12–18)
- He is credited with the first classification of the stages of zuhd. Three levels: (¶12)
  1. Renouncing the world's pleasures. (¶12)
  2. Renouncing the happy feeling of having achieved renunciation, since "Alhamdulillah, I don't love anything" still diverts from God. Imposters began to talk about zuhd; real Sufis demanded complete attention to the Lord. (¶12–13)
  3. The world is so unimportant that one does not speak of zuhd at all. (¶14, ¶16)
- Amir al-Muminin in Nahj al-Balagha: "the best of zuhd or renunciation is to conceal your zuhd", by appearing to live lavishly without actually having it. (¶13)
- Story of Sufyan al-Thawri and Imam Sadiq (see section 5). (¶14)
- Argument on level three, using Imam Ali's saying that this world, or rulership, is worth less than a pig's bone in the hand of a leper/sick man: "You renounce something which is valuable, isn't it?" If the world is like that bone, "would you talk about renouncing it at all?" Zuhd of duniya is therefore "out of place" at the highest level; one doesn't pay it attention. (¶15–16)
- Another reported classification of zuhd in three types: (¶16–18)
  - compulsory (turning from the haram);
  - meritorious or "veritable" (turning from even the halal for something higher);
  - zuhd of safety (turning from the ambiguous or doubtful: when the heart is uneasy, "just turn away from it"). (¶16–17)
- Ibrahim reported hadith from the Prophet, Imam Sadiq, Sufyan al-Thawri, Malik b. Dinar and others. A musnad (all hadith reported through a chain from the Prophet) was compiled by Muhammad b. Ishaq b. Yahya (d. 395 AH), only 49 pages, so he was a muhaddith. (¶18)

### 2.5 Fudayl b. Iyad (¶19–23)
- A "more mythical" figure than Ibrahim, and with a more interesting story. Died 187 AH; contemporary of Imams Kazim and Reza, with many stories from them. Born in Samarkand, lived in Kufa, died in Mecca, hence the stacked nisbas: Samarqandi, Kufi, Makki. Known for great fear and abundant weeping. (¶19)
- Conversion: a young highwayman, he climbed a wall to reach his beloved girl and heard Quran recited: "Alam yaʾni lil-ladhina amanu an takhshaʿa qulubuhum..." (Q 57:16, as quoted). He answered "yes, it is time, my Lord", withdrew to the desert and lived austerely. The lecturer notes the story says the verse speaks of "is it not time", and he answered that it was. (¶19–21)
- His was a tawba nasuh. He reported abundantly from Imam Sadiq. (¶21)
- Reliability across sects: al-Kafi reports his hadith, and Bukhari also; Dhahabi says there is scholarly consensus on his reliability and calls him Shaykh al-Islam. Sufyan al-Thawri, Sufyan b. Uyayna and al-Shafiʿi reported from him. The lecturer wants to find his tomb in Mecca. The "high hierarchy" could be a reporter in the most authentic books of both sects. (¶21–22)
- Wahid al-Behbahani (figurehead of the Usuli movement) is mentioned as regarding himself Shia because of a hadith from Imam Sadiq in al-Kafi, apparently via Fudayl. The link is unclear (see section 7). (¶21)
- Qushayri (Risala): "when he died, sadness was taken away from the world", which the lecturer calls exaggeration but a nice way of praising someone's fearful, sad heart. (¶23)

### 2.6 Shaqiq al-Balkhi and Hatim al-Asamm (¶23–29)
- From Balkh; killed in battle in Gilan (north Iran). An exemplar of asceticism. He reported hadith from Ahl al-Bayt, without naming them because, in his town, naming people who reported from Ahl al-Bayt was forbidden or abandoned. He also reported from Ibrahim b. Adham and Sufyan al-Thawri. (¶23–24)
- Hatim al-Asamm reported from him; he is nicknamed "Luqman of this ummah". The lecturer is "handpicking" a few mystics because treating all would need several sessions. (¶24)
- Story of Hatim "the deaf" (see section 5). (¶25)
- Shia scholars rely on Shaqiq's sayings even though Sunnis regard him as Sunni. Observation: mystics often did not reveal their madhhab, and "no one even knew what type of mazhab they were following". (¶26)
- His famous classification of religious scholars, cited from Munyat al-Murid of Shahid al-Thani (¶26–28). As the transcript records it:
  - Type 1: knows God but not God's command (a mystic who does not know jurisprudence). (¶26–27)
  - Type 2: a servant whose heart is illuminated with divine knowledge and who has emerged into the vision of God's Jalal and Kibriya; does not study jurisprudence. (¶27)
  - Type 3: knows the commands (halal, haram, minute details of ahkam) but does not know God. Compared to the criticism of the Jews. (¶27)
  - The lecturer then says "the third type" are those who know both; they sit "on a borderline between this world and the next". (¶28) (See section 7: the typology as transcribed is muddled.)
  - Their dual mode: turned to God they know nothing of this world; turned to this world they are like ordinary people, talking to and teaching them. (¶28)
- Tied to a Prophetic hadith: "Ask from the scholars (ulema), mix with the wise people (huqamah) and sit with the great". Shaqiq's reading: ulema are those who know the command of God; huqamah are the mystics near God; "the great" are those who know both. (¶28–29)
- Practical point: "you never go to a mystic and ask them about a fatwa on moon sighting"; knowing halal and haram "doesn't give life to your heart". (¶29)

### 2.7 Shaqiq's story of Imam Kazim (¶30–37)
- Context: many pretenders to Sufism lived off others as a means of livelihood. Reported by Ibn al-Jawzi, "a Sunni scholar". (¶30)
- Full narration, see section 5. Lecturer's moral: "you will see how the Mystic actually separated themselves in level from people like immah", completely different in calibre of understanding and behaviour. (¶37)
- The lecturer notes he had earlier skipped translating some of this (slides) as "not very important". (¶30)

### 2.8 Mysticism of love: definitions and the three figures (¶38–42)
- Imam Sadiq's definition, repeated by later mystics: "love is a divine fire that devolves man completely" (sic, transcript's wording; see section 7). The lecturer says nearly every mysticism book on God repeats it. (¶38)
- Three figures with overwhelming love: Maruf al-Karkhi (Iraq, d. end of 2nd century), Rabiʿa al-Adawiyya (Basra) and Dhul-Nun al-Misri (Egypt). (¶38–39)
- Maruf: among the first to speak of divine love; love cannot be learned; it "is a divine gift and not an acquisition". His student Sari al-Saqati was first to discuss the mystical states (ahwal). (¶39)
- Lecturer's question to the class: how is love put in the heart? His answer: it is not merely put like knowledge; God "shows the face of himself" to people, "which completely infatuates them and takes them out of their mind". It is shown only to those who deserve it. (¶39–40)
- Analogy: two people fall in love through seeing something beautiful in each other (admitted to be "very simple and probably elusive"). If God shows that face, the love "cannot be removed by anyone". Hence the Imams always asked for it. This love differs from physical love. (¶41)
- It must be experienced, not discussed. These people "are not mad"; they are not stretching verses. Capacity varies: the mystics were limited and annihilated by what they saw, whereas the Prophet, though shown the best face, "could still live like other people". (¶42–43)

### 2.9 Women and mysticism (¶43–47)
- Mystics have written much about women, and there were many women mystics. (¶43)
- Q&A (see section 6): Lady Amin as a modern example. (¶45)
- Qaysari (student of Ibn ʿArabi), in commentary on Fusus al-Hikam: the female "observed in her reality" is identical with the male; divest of sex and look at the soul. The lecturer registers personal doubt: soul-level differences may exist ("they think differently... this is the beauty of the creation"). (¶46–47)
- Title of "saints" (awliyaʾ Allah) given to women equally from the beginning of Islamic mystical history, unlike Christianity. Islam has no priesthood or priestly caste, so nothing prevented a woman reaching the highest rank. "And that's true." (¶47)

### 2.10 Rabiʿa al-Adawiyya: life (¶47–55)
- Of the Qays tribe; born in Basra, hence al-Basriyya. (¶47)
- Whenever love is discussed, "the first person who is discussed of course is not men. It is Rabia", perhaps due to her female nature. (¶48)
- Born c. 95 or 99 AH (the lecturer's figures) in poverty; named Rabiʿa ("the fourth") as the fourth daughter, according to Attar (Tadhkirat al-Awliyaʾ). Nowadays first-born girls also get the name. (¶48–49)
- Attar lived more than 400 years later; much of his account is "purely legendary", though mystics "have made a myth" of her. (¶49)
- Orphan, captured and sold into slavery; prayed at night; her master saw a light over her head illuminating the house, was terrified and freed her. The lecturer compares this with hadith about Fatima al-Zahraʾ, whose light filled the house and neighbourhood, which some call Shia exaggeration, yet mystics report the same of Rabiʿa. (¶49–51)
- Declined many marriage offers. Early Basran austerity: she had desired fresh dates for 12 years and never tasted them: "I am a servant of God and what has a servant to do with desires." The lecturer is unsure of its historicity, and says she later moved from austerity to the mysticism of love. (¶51–52)
- Her nights: prayer, a brief sleep before dawn, rising at dawn. (¶52)
- Distinctive from the fearful Basra school: repentance is a gift from God (no one can repent unless God has already accepted him); sinners must still fear punishment, but her higher ideal was worship neither from fear of hell nor hope of paradise, since such self-interest is unworthy and "fear and hope were like veils". The lecturer notes hope and fear are normally good, but she reached a stage where even these bar one from Allah. (¶53–54)
- Torch and water story (see section 5). (¶54)
- Per "Michael Smith" (see section 7): she began in a desert cell near Basra, "went straight to God for teaching", had no master or silsila, unlike those with chains back to the prophets. One of the first to teach that love alone guides the path. Visitors sought her counsel. (¶55)
- Judged the person who "introduced the element of selfless love into the austere teaching of the earliest ascetic". Lecturer: love is the "thesis and the heart of mysticism"; in other mystics (not the Imams) "love was missing. It was fear, it was asceticism... hardship... hereafter." (¶55–56)

### 2.11 Rabiʿa's poems and prayers (¶56–61)
- Her Arabic is beautiful but loses sense in English. (¶56–57)
- Poem: "I have made thee the companion of my heart but my body is available for those who seek its company": the body is friendly to people, the Beloved is the guest of her soul, God. (¶57)
- The "two ways of loving you" poem: selfish love, where she remembers him because she wants to; and love worthy of him, where "you lift the veil", "it's not me who remembers you, it's you who comes to me". Also: none like thee, "have pity on the sinner who comes to thee". (¶57–58)
- Prayer: if I worship for fear of hell, burn me in hell; if for hope of paradise, exclude me; but if for you yourself, do not withhold your eternal beauty (lecturer paraphrases and restates). (¶59)
- Prayer: give my worldly share to your enemies and my otherworldly share to your friends, "since you are more than enough for me". Lecturer: "I don't want anything from you." (¶59–60)
- Link to Imam Ali's three kinds of worshippers: the slave (fear), the merchant (hope of paradise), and the free (worship because He deserves it). Rabiʿa realises the third. (¶60)
- Legend vs sayings: stories grew from reverence, but sayings, poems and prayers "are not legends anymore". She died in her early to mid-80s, "continually united with her beloved": "my beloved is always with me". (¶60–61)
- Translations cited: Andrew Harvey and Eric Hanson (transcribed "Eric Hunt"), Perfume of the Deserts; Charles Upton, Doorkeeper of the Hearts. Slides to be sent. (¶61–62)

### 2.12 Dhul-Nun al-Misri (¶62–65)
- Contemporary of Rabiʿa but much younger; transmitted from Malik b. Anas; imprisoned during the Muʿtazilite persecution for believing the Quran uncreated; accused of being a philosopher and alchemist; many miracles ascribed (legend or reality unknown). Egypt was then a cradle of philosophical ideas. (¶62)
- Probably influenced by Neoplatonism (Nicholson's view). (¶63–64)
- Represents the School of Love. His saying: "publicly I call thee my Lord, but in solitude I call thee my beloved", i.e. Ya Rabb in public, mahbub in solitude. (¶63)
- First to formulate a theory of maʿrifa (intuitive knowledge) as against ʿilm (discursive knowledge). The Quran does not use this distinction; mystics distinguish knowledge from within the heart from knowledge from outside. Called by some "the first philosopher among the Sufis". His poems are more developed than Rabiʿa's "rudimentary" ones. (¶63–64)
- The sign of the lover of God is following the Prophet, God's friend, in morals, deeds, orders and customs: "very orthodox of course". (¶64)
- Exchange: asked to be shown the greatest name of God, "show me the smallest": the lecturer's gloss is that all of Allah's names are the greatest (isme aʿzam), though we do not realise it. (¶65)
- Next week: mysticism of gnosis. (¶65)

## 5. Stories, anecdotes and analogies
- (¶14) Sufyan al-Thawri sees Imam Sadiq in a luxurious kingly robe and objects; the Imam reveals a harsh garment underneath: conceal your zuhd. Told fully, briefly.
- (¶19–21) Fudayl the highwayman hears Q 57:16 while climbing a wall to his beloved and repents. Told fully.
- (¶25) Hatim "the deaf": a woman passes wind while asking him a question; he pretends to be deaf so she is spared embarrassment. Told fully.
- (¶31–37) Shaqiq meets a young man in Sufi wool at Qadisiyya (149 AH) and plans to rebuke him as a parasite; the young man says "keep away from lots of suspicions". At Waqisa he is praying in tears and recites Q 20:82. The jug falls into a well and water rises to return it. At Mecca he prays and weeps all night, then enters a grand house, where Shaqiq is told he is Musa b. Jaʿfar. Told fully.
- (¶11–12) Ibrahim b. Adham compared to the Buddha. Analogy.
- (¶41) Falling in love between two people as an analogy for God showing His "face". Analogy.
- (¶50–51) Rabiʿa's master sees light over her head and frees her; paralleled with Fatima's light. Told fully.
- (¶52) Rabiʿa's twelve years of not tasting dates. Brief.
- (¶54) Rabiʿa with water and a torch to put out hell and burn paradise, to remove the two veils. Told fully.
- (¶63) Dhul-Nun's "Lord in public, beloved in solitude." Alluded to.
- (¶65) "Show me the greatest name... show me the smallest." Alluded to, incomplete.

## 7. Problems in the transcript
- (¶4) "born in Basra in 781 AH": likely CE (c. 165 AH); similarly (¶49) "717 to 801 AH" for Rabiʿa is likely CE. (¶48) gives "95 or 99 AH". Note the inconsistency.
- (¶3) "you will see Ghazali in his book Al Harith al Muhasibi": garbled; probably "you will see al-Muhasibi frequently in Ghazali's books".
- (¶5) "included Ghazali": likely "influenced Ghazali".
- (¶6) "as you have in the Quran, they did not observe the content that they should": garbled.
- (¶8) "Master of Moderate medieval mysticism": uncertain wording. "It is he who gave Sufism highly developed technical language" probably refers to Muhasibi but "he" is unclear.
- (¶10) "Ismaul Shayaat": unclear title.
- (¶14–15) "ruler ship of you", "bone of a pig in hands of a leopard" (¶15, then "sick man"): probably leper; garbled.
- (¶16) "In another hades he is reported": the subject of "he" is unclear (Ibrahim or another).
- (¶19) "bring all these kitties": kunya/nisba.
- (¶20) "Alam ya'ni lil lazeena": the Arabic as transcribed may be mis-heard; to be verified against Q 57:16 text. "It is not the time" in ¶20 contradicts the surrounding story and is presumably a mis-hearing.
- (¶21–22) Al-Behbahani's hadith in al-Kafi and "Kulaini and Bukhari report this hades in al-Kafi": confused (Bukhari is not in al-Kafi); the sentence needs a check. "now Al-Waheed al-Behbahani" sits oddly with Fudayl.
- (¶22) "Sufiyan Thawri, Sufyan ibn Uyaynah and As-Shafi": likely al-Shafiʿi.
- (¶23) "He said and when he died...": speaker unclear; "Al-Qushayri... 4th and 5th century" matches his lifetime roughly.
- (¶26–28) The three types of scholars: type 1 and 2 as transcribed are very similar; later "the third type" is used for the combined type, suggesting a fourth. The text is likely missing or garbled.
- (¶28) "huqamah" probably hukamaʾ.
- (¶30) "I did not translate it": refers to slide material not in the transcript. "at a test" probably "in a text/report". (¶31) "he was white colour not white": garbled. (¶32) "suspicions are seen": probably "some suspicion is a sin" (cf. Q 49:12; [VERIFY]).

- (¶38) "divine fire that devolves man completely": probably "devours".
- (¶41) "faith" for "face" in "Show us the faith of you" and "face and faith"; likely mis-hearing of "face" (wajh); (¶48, 54) "faith of God" likewise.
- (¶45) "Lady Amin... 5-6 years ago... belongs to the 2nd century after Hijra": inconsistent; Banu Amin died in 1983 CE.
