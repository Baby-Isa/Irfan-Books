# Lecture 08: Beginning the Journey (Suluk): Repentance, Character and the Five Disciplines

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan08.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture moves from theory and the mystic's description of the journey to practical advice on how to travel it. The journey (suluk) is inward: God is already nearer than the jugular vein, so travel means removing the veils from the heart (¶3–4, 9). The first step is repentance, open even to the worst sinners and the old, but it must be paced and grounded (¶10–28). The lecture then gives a concrete rite of repentance for Dhu'l-Qa'dah (¶29–33), makes good conduct toward people a precondition (¶39–42), and turns to the "Hadith of Miraj" and the Unwan al-Basri hadith. From these it develops the first disciplines on the list of five: silence and hunger, the latter treated as a path to wisdom and watchfulness (muraqaba) (¶43–88). Night vigil is promised for the next session, so the lecture is unfinished (¶89). The central caution is balance: each discipline is to be practised in moderation, never in a way that rushes the self.

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Recap and the vocabulary of the journey (¶3–4)
- Series so far: theoretical irfan, then "some aspects of practical irfan in the sense that how the mystic describes the journey". Now "practical advice from the mystics how this journey should be carried out" (¶3).
- Suluk is translated "wayfaring". The Arabic root means "to take the path". Behaviour is also called suluk "because it is how you move around as a personality" (¶3).
- The one who does suluk is the salik, the wayfarer. The real goal of mysticism is knowledge (¶3).
- "We are not traveling from one place to another place. We are actually finding ourselves": the journey is inward, not outward (¶4).
- It concentrates one capability that God has given us. Through it we find "what's going on around us". Many things are happening in this room that we do not see. The purpose is to see them, to see God's presence and to see our own selves (¶4).
- This returns to the opening teaching of the course: whoever knows his soul knows his Lord (¶4).

### 2.2 Najm al-Din Kubra: the two journeys (¶5–9)
- Shaykh Najm al-Din Kubra: a mystic of the 7th Islamic century and founder of the Kubrawiyya order (¶5).
- Name explained: people saw him as a "tremendous reminder of Allah", after the verse about the "greatest calamity" (Q 79:34, as cited by the lecturer). Kubra is grammatically feminine; for men one would say akbar (¶5).
- He opens: "O slave of Allah, know that you are a wayfarer... seeking your Lord and ultimately one day you would meet him" (¶5).
- Lecturer's remark: Najm al-Din presents "whoever hopes to meet God should know the time of meeting will come" as a tradition. The lecturer says "I don't know why there is a mistake", since this is a Quran verse (Q 18:110, "whoever hopes to meet his Lord..."). He wonders if Najm al-Din "somehow confused" it (¶6).
- Paraphrase: whoever does not hope to meet God will meet him anyway, "in an embarrassing situation" (¶6).
- God has set two journeys for the children of Adam: qahri (involuntary) and ikhtiyari (voluntary) (¶6).
- The involuntary journey is death, barzakh and Qiyama, "out of your control". The voluntary journey is the shortcut, the mystical path, suluk (¶7).
- Objection raised: why rush, if we will arrive anyway? The answer is given in a garbled passage (¶8). What survives:
  - The involuntary journey is "longer and harder".
  - There is "more merit" in the voluntary one.
  - The mention of those who "have already completed the journey and already are living beside Allah" (¶8) is unclear.
- No movement in space is involved. God is "closer to a person than his own jugular vein... here we are not going to go anywhere" (¶9).
- "Journey" means "the removal of the veil that obstruct the vision of the heart" so that divine light can shine in the heart. This is "the same journey for which man has been created": to see the glory and beauty of God. We will see it one day, but "it can take the veils from our heart" (¶9).
- All the techniques and methods the Sufis improvised across religions serve to "sharpen the heart and to take the veil" (¶9).
- Veils are of two kinds, dark and light (shining), as mentioned earlier. There are different practices at each stage for each veil (¶10).

### 2.3 First step: tawba (repentance) (¶10–12)
- First, wake up from slumber. Then "the first and foremost... step is to repent" (¶10).
- Repentance here is not formal. It means repenting from casualness towards the Lord, from taking everything for granted, from wasting life, and from heedlessness of God's presence. These are things "we are not going to be punished for" in the ordinary sense (¶10).
- Analogy: "If you want a better job, you need a higher qualification." A better station needs a better state of mind and behaviour, so you leave the old behind (¶11).
- Question: can a sinner qualify? Yes. Repentance "covers all types of bad attitudes towards Allah" (¶11–12).
- Examples: Bishr al-Hafi and Fudayl b. Iyad. They were "real mischievous people", but "something struck a chord" in the soul. They repented wholeheartedly and became mystics (¶12).

### 2.4 Q&A thread: relapse, despair and age (¶13–24)
- Relapse after repentance: "They might do it" again, but one must leave the ordinary things behind to reach the higher station. Repentance is continuous (¶13–15).
- Mujahida: "a fight with one's own selves". At every stage "the self would come back and take revenge... hits the person in the face in a way that for some day they are dizzy" (¶15).
- Despair (qunut): Q 39:53 ("O my servants who have wronged themselves, do not despair of God's mercy", as cited). Despair is common among sinful believers. Their numbers are great, and they say "there is no way for us to go back" (¶15–16).
- Instinct tells us God forgives, "even if the prophet wouldn't have told us" (¶16).
- When aiming at higher stations, ordinary believers say "this is not for us, this is for people of higher caliber". The lecturer says this is "exactly what the verse is addressing" (¶16).
- He reads "wronged themselves" as including those who "have wasted lots of capacities in their souls". They despair of moving on the path. Do not say "me, such a waste, such an idle person" (¶17–18).
- A person may say "he might forgive me, I might go to Jannah but to move on this path is impossible for me": "This we have to put aside" (¶18).
- Age: this is also despair of God's mercy (¶19–20). It does get harder with age. Analogy: becoming a boxer or wrestler young is easy, but with age "energy, power, capacities have diminished". "It is difficult... but it is not impossible". Only our ability falters, not God's mercy (¶22).
- Audience: the path is not linear; a few steps can take you very far or many steps not far. The lecturer agrees: correct instruction is crucial (¶23–24).

### 2.5 Story: the warning against rushing (¶24–27)
- The son of "Ayatollah Amjad" of Tehran (a friend who had just visited and left that day) was heavy-hearted, disappointed and full of doubts. He was advised to recite Ziyarat Ashura (¶24).
- Ziyarat Ashura is effective with its "hundred laanat and hundred salam". Reciting without them is "good", but the stated effects are for the full form (¶25).
- Result: after a long daily practice his face showed light and people noticed (¶25).
- Ayatollah "Bahauddin" (a close associate of the father, deceased a few years ago) visited and told the father to make the son stop (¶25–26).
- Reason: "you need to have basis. You are rushing... and then suddenly you might fall". Progress should be linear and gradual. Compare fasting suddenly for three months, gaining spiritual energy, and then feeling "abandoned by your own soul" (¶26).
- Lecturer's moral: be aware of "the dangers of rushing things forward"; keep to "the pace that our Aimmah have advised" (¶27).
- Return to mercy: Q 6:54 (as cited): believers who come to the Prophet are greeted with salamun alaykum, and "your Lord has prescribed for himself mercy". These are "ordinary believers who do lots of things... still believers like us", including those who err in ignorance (¶27).
- It can also be read at a deeper level, for believers moving from ordinary belief to deeper belief. When they wake they see their past as "pathetic" and change course (¶28).

### 2.6 Tawba rite for Dhu'l-Qa'dah (¶29–38)
- Today was the first of Dhu'l-Qa'dah, "the high time" to start suluk (¶29).
- The Prophet came to the people on a Sunday of Dhu'l-Qa'dah. The lecturer says it is reported in many hadith books, in Mafatih al-Jinan, and in the Muraqabat of Mirza Maliki (a mystic and a teacher of Imam Khomeini) (¶29).
- Others say it may be done any time, but the high time is a Sunday in Dhu'l-Qa'dah (¶30).
- The rite as the lecturer gives it:
  - Ghusl with the intention of ghusl of tawba, then wudu.
  - Four rakaʿat, in twos ("every mustahab salaat is done in twos"). In each rakʿa: one Fatiha, three Ikhlas, one Nas (¶30–31).
  - Then istighfar 70 times. The exact phrase "is not mentioned in the riwayat" (¶30).
  - Then la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah, then a du'a (as transcribed, ¶32) asking forgiveness for oneself and for all believers, men and women.
- Reward: angels call from heaven that all sins, including those of descendants and parents, are forgiven. "Start whatever you want to do because you are completely clear." There is "a long list" of such announcements (¶33).
- Q&A: the istighfar and the du'a come after the prayer (¶35–36). There is no particular hour, only a Sunday (¶37–38). The lecturer forgot to bring the book al-Muraqabat, and it is in Arabic anyway (¶38–39).

### 2.7 Good character towards people (¶39–42)
- After tawba, "the first and foremost and most important condition is to behave yourself with regards to people" (¶39).
- Reason: "there is a system of justice working underlying every action in this world... very, very strong". A wrong done to anyone, even an animal, or inadvertently, blocks the rise through stations until the relationship is put right (¶39–40).
- The first condition is "husn al-khuluq wa seerah", character and conduct (¶40).
- The Prophet and the Imams, though of the highest station, were such that "everyone loved to sit with them". A mystic, "however high they go", must stay friendly with ordinary people (¶40–41).
- Hadith (both Sunni and Shia, per the lecturer): "the beauty of character is half of deen". Without that half, how can one move deeper into faith? (¶41).
- Joke (¶42): do husbands with good character have the whole faith and wives half? The lecturer says they are "in two different areas".

### 2.8 The Hadith of Miraj: love, muraqaba and the five disciplines (¶43–51)
- The Hadith of Miraj was translated by Abbas Jaafar, who allowed its use in class and on the web (¶43).
- Students should use judgment: some parts can be practised, some cannot (¶43). The lecturer shows excerpts on PowerPoint (¶44).
- God's love "encompasses those who love for my sake", even sinners, because they are God's creatures and God still hopes in them (¶44). They love the faithful more than the sinners, "the more they find my light in someone" (¶45).
- "There's no signpost or boundary or limit to my love" (¶45). Such people "never ask for the need from others" and relate to people for God's sake, not out of need (¶45).
- The qualities listed in ¶46 include: guarding the heart from temptation, being mindful that "I'm aware of his acts and I'm watching him", and taking hunger as his life.
  - The lecturer calls the watching clause the key point. This is muraqaba: being watchful of God's presence.
  - He links it to the mystics' oft-repeated saying "worship God as you see him, and if you do not see him know that he is seeing you" (¶46).
- Imam Khomeini is said to have advised his politicians that this world is the place of God's manifestation, where "Allah is present, he was really seeing". This is muraqaba (¶47).
- Audience: how is muraqaba different from taqwa? Answer: taqwa is refraining from disobedience even without feeling God's presence. When taqwa becomes "very, very thick" it yields constant muraqaba, which restrains idle thinking (¶48–49).
- Second discipline: guarding the heart and mind from idle imagination. "It is usually the chain of thoughts which drags us into sin": imagine something, want it, imagine how to get it, improvise ways, even if haram (¶50).
- Silence and hunger are prerequisites for muraqaba and for the power to block imagination (¶51).
- When the heart is clean, imagination is clean. Hence the Infallibles (Masumeen) have no "vicious thoughts"; even their talk and dreams are masum (¶51).

### 2.9 Dreams of the Prophets as revelation (¶51–54)
- In dream "we are taken into a world which we deserve". Prophets' dreams contain only the pure, so they are revelation (¶51–52).
- Quranic examples (as cited by the lecturer):
  - Q 17:60: the vision shown as a tribulation for the people and the accursed tree. The lecturer glosses it as the dream of monkeys jumping on his pulpit (the transcript says "pockets") (¶52).
  - Q 48:27: the truthful vision of entering the Sacred Mosque. This concerned the Treaty of Hudaybiyya year, the 6th year (the transcript says 8th). The people doubted when they were stopped. The Quran says it will happen next year (¶52–53).
  - Q 37:102: Ibrahim sees in a dream that he is slaughtering his son. This sufficed as revelation (¶53).
- Reason: "these currents of vile imagination... wouldn't attack them" (¶54).

### 2.10 The five practices and the principle of balance (¶55–58)
- Five things: silence, hunger, night vigil, seclusion, continuous remembrance of God (¶55).
- Al-Kashani said these in a Persian line: they "make perfect things imperfect of the world" (¶55). The line is as paraphrased by the lecturer.
- Caution: nobody is asked to keep silence all life. The Prophet and Amir al-Mu'minin spoke a great deal (¶55).
- Digression: a million Sunni hadith, much forged. Ali Shariati on Abu Hurayra: the Prophet, living 63 years, could not have spoken that much (¶55–56).
- Hunger: fasting is not allowed on two consecutive days (the wasal fast), so balance (¶56).
- Night vigil: the Quran mentions half, a third, two thirds, but God "knows you cannot do this all the time" (¶57).
- Seclusion and constant remembrance are impossible with family, society and duties, unless one is a Hindu guru, lives in a hole or well, or is a Christian monk in a cell. "We are not advised to do that" (¶57–58).
- These five are common to mystics across all religions (Hindu, Buddhist, Christian, Jewish, Muslim). The key question is "where is the balance" for each (¶58).

### 2.11 Silence (¶59–64)
- From the Hadith of Miraj: adopt silence. The most flourishing hearts are those of the silent virtuous, and the most barren those of the idle talkers (¶59).
- Meaning: do not talk of what does not concern you or benefit you and others. The tongue and vocal cords exist for a purpose (¶59).
- Knowledge of religion is also vital. Without it, mystical experiences, which come at first "in adversity... without our control", can lead to madness, presumption or arrogance (¶60–61).
- Example: a woman who sees much of the barzakh world, on the verge of madness, with no knowledge to explain it. "This is why usually these people are called schizophrenic" (¶61).
- Knowledge "doesn't come through mystical experience. You have to learn that" (¶61–62).
- Audience: Shaykh Rajab Ali was a learned man who controlled his nafs. Answer: knowledge was given through relationship with scholars. Sometimes experiences come with knowledge and sometimes without, and without it people go mad (¶63–64).

### 2.12 Hunger (¶65–73)
- In the Hadith of Miraj the Prophet asks "what is the benefit of hunger?" and is told "wisdom" (¶65).
- Audience: a hadith of Imam Jafar al-Sadiq that knowledge is best achieved on an empty stomach. The lecturer: "wisdom and knowledge doesn't come with filled stomach. But not always hunger will give wisdom" (¶66–67).
- Audience: shedding the body at will. The lecturer says the soul leaves in sleep every night, so it is "not against the physical nature of human beings". The mystic merely brings it under control (¶68–69).
- Analogy: digestion. He saw a Qadiri Sufi swallow a very large stone with no teeth and bring it back at will, showing control of the stomach (¶69–70).
- Hadith of Miraj on hunger: it brings wisdom, protection of the heart, closeness, constant sadness (the heart's grief at separation from God), truthful speech and indifference to hardship or ease (¶71).
- Hunger is paralleled with prostration as a state of closeness (¶71).
- Practical meaning: "you never should be unease" after eating. A heavy stomach makes the mind dizzy, and "you cannot remember God" or pray well (¶72–73).
- A mu'min stops eating when he could still eat 40 more morsels. Another benefit: if invited elsewhere one can take another morsel (¶73).

### 2.13 Silence and fasting in the Quran (¶74–76)
- Hadith of Miraj: the essence of worship is silence. Some tariqas prescribe days of silence and fasting (¶74).
- Quranic precedent: Maryam's fast of silence (Q 19:26, as cited). It was a custom of Bani Israil; one signed to people that one was fasting from speech, for a day at least (¶74–75).
- Chain: fasting gives wisdom, wisdom gives maʿrifa, maʿrifa gives yaqin. Whoever has certitude no longer cares whether days are easy or hard (¶75).
- When a servant stays hungry and guards his tongue, "I teach him wisdom" (¶75).
- The lecturer says "what is the relation between these we don't know but mystics have experienced" (¶76).
- The wisdom will be a witness against an unbeliever at the Hashr. For a believer it is light, healing and mercy. "The first thing that I will show him will be his own thoughts... his own faults before he gets involved with the faults of others" (¶76–77). The passage is partly garbled.

### 2.14 Imam Sadiq's advice to Unwan al-Basri (¶77–88)
- Unwan al-Basri, an old Sunni man, wanted knowledge from Imam Jafar. He was first rejected and told to go to Malik b. Anas and other scholars of Medina. He prayed that God "soften the heart of Jafar", and then the Imam received him warmly (¶77–78).
- Advice on eating:
  - Do not eat what you have no appetite for.
  - Do not eat unless hungry; we eat from habit (breakfast, lunch, dinner) (¶78).
  - Eat only halal, beginning with Allah's name.
  - Prophet's saying: no vessel is worse filled than the stomach. One third for food, one third for drink, one third for air (¶79).
- Audience: dislike of vegetables, children's eating (¶80–81). Lecturer: children are choosy and mothers must tell them. For adults, a banana or biryani is fine "as long as you take these points into consideration" (¶81). Food is to be discussed next week (¶81).
- Audience: Imam Ali's hard bread. Lecturer: "he's in level 1000, we just want to start level one". He told people not to imitate him (¶82–83).
- Nutrition: a trained body and soul can extract the best from poor food. Imam Ali's analogy: a desert tree is stronger than a riverside tree (¶84–85).
- Audience: meat hardens the heart? Lecturer: so eat meat every other day, not twice a day. Leaving it altogether has "spiritual difficulties" too (¶86–87).
- Advice on conduct (husn al-sira):
  - Silence: if told "say one word and I'll give you ten", do not reply (¶87).
  - When insulted, do not be angry. Reply "if you are right, may Allah forgive me", and "if you are wrong, may He forgive you". The example is a businessman called a "robber" (¶88).
  - To harshness, respond with good counsel and prayer for the person (¶88).
- Night vigil is deferred to "Friday or Sunday" (¶89).
