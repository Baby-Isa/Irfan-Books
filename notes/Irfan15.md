# Lecture 15: The Stations of the Mystics in Ibn Sina's Isharat (Namat 9)

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan15.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
With only two sessions left, the lecturer drops the history of the tariqas (judged "not very much related to irfan", teaching nothing "about God") and reads, slowly, the ninth chapter of Ibn Sina's *Al-Isharat wa'l-Tanbihat* on the stations of the mystics (¶3–5). The lecture follows the text as a ladder: two preparatory stages (iradah, riyadah), then three "ranks" of three steps each, ending in wusool or fana (arrival). That arrival is the end of the first of the four journeys and only the beginning of the second (¶18–19, ¶59). The lecturer explains each term through everyday analogies: thunder behind clouds, medicine dosage and operations, a framed mirror, familiarity with a host's house. He insists that annihilation is the loss of self-perception, not of the soul (¶55–57). It fits the series as the concrete, stage-by-stage account of the first journey (sayr), recalled from the earlier teaching on four journeys (¶59).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Change of plan; why Ibn Sina (¶3–5)
- Only two sessions left, so the continuation of the tariqas is dropped. It is "just the history of how different tariqas were developed", with the Ni'matullahi tariqa and "Shah" having "his own views and all these issues, which is not actually teaching us anything about God" (¶3).
- A handout is taken from *Al-Isharat wa'l-Tanbihat* ("admonitions, warnings and caution") by Ibn Sina. The lecturer forgot to copy the cover page. The book is "one of the most valuable works on philosophy" and has four volumes (¶3).
- Amazingly, at its end Ibn Sina wrote a chapter "as a mystic". The lecturer asks how a non-mystic could explain all these stages (¶4).
- Some mystics claim Ibn Sina wrote it while under the training of the businessman Abu Saeed, in "Riyadh", doing 40 nights or days of training (¶4). The transcript is garbled here (see §7).
- The chapter is the "ninth chapter", on mystical stations. Any book on mysticism will allude to it (¶4).
- He explains "very nicely" how mystics progress. The first journey is annihilation or "arrival" (¶5).
- The English text is confusing on its own and needs reflection, the Arabic and a commentary. So the lecturer goes slowly through this session and the next (¶5).

### 2.2 First preparation: Iradah (¶6–8)
- The text begins at page 85, chapter 7, a "remark concerning the first preparation". The first stage is willingness in movement towards the truth, "iradah". One who has it is a "mureed" (¶5–6).
- The willingness is not universal: of billions of people, "very few" have it (¶7).
- Ibn Sina gives two ways it comes to the heart. (a) By demonstrative certainty (al-burhani yaqini). The intellect concludes that prosperity and felicity require moving towards Allah. The lecturer calls this "very difficult" (¶7). (b) By tranquillity of the soul through confirmation of faith (¶7).
- The usual route is the second. A person long practising faith and good deeds gradually finds the thought "this world is without value. I have to leave this and go towards Allah". Allah puts the willingness in the heart (¶8).
- Signs: wanting to wake before dawn, to worship and supplicate, and eagerness for worship. The lecturer cites "many hadith" that if God wants khair for someone he makes worship pleasant for them (¶8).
- Ibn Sina shows how iradah is formed "because we don't find this iradah in many people" (¶8).

### 2.3 Second preparation: Riyadah (¶9–17)
- Chapter eight: iradah and riyadah are both "preparatory stages". "We are not starting mystical experience yet" (¶9).
- Souls are "very much engrossed in the material life" and unfamiliar with that other world, so they need training. The analogy is an athlete, who "cannot go just like this" and needs lots of training (¶9).
- Riyadah comes from the training of animals, such as a cow for ploughing or a horse for hunting. The word implies that "you are like animals that should be trained" (¶10).
- The lecturer rejects the rendering "self-mortification". It is "just some training of the soul to become familiar with that other world", the malakut (¶10).
- Riyadah has three aims (¶11).
  - **First:** remove from the path whatever is other than the truth (haqq). The lecturer calls this "one of the most difficult" parts: emptying the soul of other needs. Here ascetic attitudes come in (¶11).
  - He distinguishes the ascetic from the mystic. Ibn Sina says asceticism "has no value" in itself. Some choose it to gain more: "they abstain from the minimum, to gain the maximum" (¶11–12).
  - Refraining from pleasure so as to enjoy the pleasures of Jannah is "the path of an ascetic" and earns its own reward, but "not the path of a mystic". The mystic wants asceticism only because the world diverts him (¶12).
  - Later, when the soul is ready and the world no longer diverts, there is no need to abstain from its pleasures. It is needed "at the beginning, when they are training this soul" (¶12–13).
  - **Second:** render the commanding soul (nafs al-ammara) obedient to the tranquil soul (nafs al-mutmainna), so that the imagination and estimation (waham, khayal) are drawn to what is proper (¶13–15).
  - The lecturer says the usual nafs is "ammara or muztariddah", agitated and "always going up and down" (¶13–14).
  - Imagination "works in different areas", we want many things, and the nafs orders us to realise them. That is "what causes us to do wrong things" (¶14).
  - Riyadah subdues this to a nafs "completely reliant on Allah and completely tranquil" in the face of life's varied areas (¶14–15).
  - **Third:** refine the soul to receive admonition and knowledge. Unrefined, it "cannot receive that type of knowledge which is given to the mystics" (¶15). Ibn Sina's phrase is rendered "render the innermost thought sensitive to attention", meaning sensitivity to knowledge from the other world (¶15).
- Q&A (¶16–17): answered "we are a channel or canal towards Allah", and riyadah refines the channel so we can receive knowledge (¶17).
- Summary: these stages do not make a mystic. Iradah requires being a very good person. Riyadah requires abstaining from evil and the trivial world to purify yourself, and only then can one "set on the first stage" (¶18).

### 2.4 How many stations? (¶18–19)
- Different people count the stations differently: 9 or 12 ascensions, 100 stations, 1000 (divided into tens), even 7000. Some say there are none: "always just one step. You just decide to go to that and you go" (¶18–19).
- Ibn Sina's classification is "very nice": three ranks, each with its own steps. The lecturer says the progression is "very beautiful, very difficult to actually explain" (¶19).

### 2.5 First rank (a): waqt and the stolen glance (¶19–31)
- Chapter 9, "first step in conjunction" (wusool, "arriving at Allah"). The first step is the moment, waqt (plural awqat). The Sufi's first experience is not long knowledge but moments of light shining and stopping (¶20).
- Everyone has had some of these, and they "do not depend on your knowledge and purification". They come from Allah as an instant sense of closeness, lasting a second or a minute (¶20–21).
- For those with iradah and riyadah they come more clearly and frequently. The image is "thunder light from behind the clouds" (¶21).
- Ibn Sina's phrase is "pleasurable stolen looks", which the lecturer calls "Qhalsa". It is like glancing at something and pulling your eyes back. In Farsi it is used of those who suddenly fall into ecstatic sensation (¶21–22).
- Why only glimpses: the soul must be prepared gradually. Seen directly, lightning "your eyes will burn". "Light of the truth ... blinds inexperienced eyes". You cannot gaze at the sun (¶22–23).
- Allah's mercy "very smoothly brings peace to your heart, so that your heart would expand" (¶22).
- Ordinary people do not notice such moments. The trained person knows they are coming. Before they come and after they go he is "in agitation, in unrest" (¶23).
- On "every moment is surrounded by two ecstasies" the lecturer says "ecstasy" is a poor translation. He cites Ayatullah Hassanzadeh, who prefers a term that "originally means hope" (the transcript is garbled; see §7). Before the moment the mystic is eager and "senseless" until it comes (¶24–25).
- After it goes he is "even sadder", having tasted the pleasure. This is "one of the most excruciating stations": they have "no control whatsoever" over it (¶25).
- Each flash confirms faith and increases eagerness, and patience is needed (¶25).
- Mystics in all stations "complain about the separation". The heart cannot take more at the start. The analogy: an operation expands the heart a little, then one must wait before another. "You cannot have several operations all at once" (¶26).
- With continued exercise the overwhelming moments grow more frequent (¶27).
- Q&A (¶29): the sadness is "like sadness or be separated from something which is very valuable". It continues "until the very end". It is reported of Imam Ali (as) that he was always sad. At that high station "we don't know what it is for", but at the beginning the cause is known.
- Q&A (¶31): dreams and iradah are out of control, and so is this wajdah (finding) as it comes and goes. It is "just the first step" and "not yet very challenging" because the heart "cannot take it at the beginning".

### 2.6 Second step: tawaqqul (¶32–33, 36)
- Chapter on the "second step": seeing the truth in everything. The lecturer names it "Tawaqqul" (see §7), and says conjunction becomes a fixed habit (¶32).
- At first the awqat come only during riyadah: a 40-day special worship, fasting, praying, night worship, dhikr. Later they come even while talking to someone, because the heart is now very close to that source (¶32–33).
- Ibn Sina: whenever he catches a glimpse of a thing he returns from it to the "side of sanctity" and is overcome by a fainting. He "almost sees the truth in everything" (¶33, ¶36).

### 2.7 Third step: istifaad (¶34, 36–38)
- Istifaad "means somehow to jump from your place". Lights now excite you so strongly that someone beside you can tell you are "under the spell of some sort of supernatural training or teaching" (¶36–37).
- The dosage analogy: Allah "is taking you smoothly by increasing the dosage of that light so that your heart can take it", in "very measured portions" (¶37). The first and second steps are mild. The third is stronger and can affect the body (¶37).
- Ibn Sina: veils are lifted and he "ceases to be calm". The novice is "provoked out of his stability". With prolonged exercise he will no longer be provoked by each lifting of a veil (¶38).

### 2.8 Second rank: inqilab (¶38–43)
- Recap of rank one: light at moments, then stronger lights that connect everything to the light, then dosage so high that it moved the heart (¶38–39).
- Second rank: after the moving, tranquillity. He is not moved by the "high voltage electricity" any more (¶39).
- "Inqilab" means "turning upside down". The mystic becomes "a medium" with that world and gradually familiar. His moment is converted into tranquillity and the stolen looks become familiar (¶39–40).
- He gains "stable knowledge of the truth" as a continuous companion in which he delights. If he turns away he does so "with loss and regret", since he must do business and go to the world (¶41).
- Hence some never tire of worship. Owais al-Qarani (discussed before) waited all day for night. One night he did ruku until morning, the next night sajdah. Asked if the long night tired him, he said it was short and "I would like the whole life, that'd be one night" passed in one prostration to Allah (¶41–42).
- Our bodies tire after 15 minutes of sajdah because we are unfamiliar. "The body is always subservient to the soul": if the soul wants, the body does not tire (¶42–43).

### 2.9 Taghalgul (¶43–46)
- The next step is taghalgul. The analogy is water poured on a tree, going down through the roots. The knowledge penetrates the whole soul and becomes "some sort of life" (¶44).
- The fifth step: less outward manifestation of the inner experience. "He will be present while being absent and stationary while marching on" (¶44, ¶46).
- A poem is quoted (not identified, see §4): "an absent person who's present" (¶44).
- Such a person keeps company with people bodily while the soul clings to the beings of the malakut al-aala (¶45). He "cannot pay attention to things" because he is absent from them (¶45).

### 2.10 Mashiya (¶46–47)
- Until now the lights came without control. In the sixth stage "he has it whenever he wants": "mashiya", will (¶46).
- The lecturer says he "has become a complete mystic" and adds "there are steps beyond this as well" (¶46).
- The second rank runs from inqilab to mashiya. The analogy: first you are familiar with people but can visit only when invited. Later it is like your own house, where you go "whenever you wish" (¶47–48).

### 2.11 Third rank: tatahareeq (¶48–51)
- The first step of the third rank is tatahareeq (moving up more towards the Lord), the "seventh step", with conjunction "without even a wish" (¶48).
- He does not need a wish: when he notices one thing he notices another, meaning he sees God's power behind everything (¶48, ¶50).
- The lecturer cites the Quran: "Afalaa yanzuroona ilalibili kaifa khuliqat" (88:17), do they not look at the camels (¶48–49). He applies it to seeing God with every created thing (¶49).
- He cites Imam Ali (as): "I never have seen anything unless except that I saw God before and after it". He adds that this is "not that very high quality of Imam Ali" but something these people have (¶49).
- He lives among the ignorant but is "not ignorant anymore". His heart becomes "a reflection of Allah". The lecturer warns that one might then say "I am God", because he sees God so fully in himself (¶50–51).

### 2.12 Taraddud (¶52–54)
- Hesitation (taraddud) is not doubt. It means becoming a replica of the truth while remaining aware of oneself (¶52).
- The innermost thought, probably qalb, becomes a polished mirror facing the truth. Lofty pleasures pour in (¶52).
- He is pleased with himself for the traces of truth. The lecturer says it is not arrogance but wonder at "the power that Allah has rested in your heart" (¶53–54). He cites "the heart of the momin is the Haram of Allah" (¶53).
- He oscillates: "going to himself going to god" (¶54). The lecturer calls it a pitfall that must be left behind (¶55).

### 2.13 Wusool and fana (¶55–60)
- The ninth station is wusool (arriving), which "the mystics call annihilation". He no longer looks at himself. The soul does not perish: "if the soul goes away there's no reflection of Allah" (¶55).
- The mirror analogy. In the morning you see only yourself, not the mirror. Put a lovely frame on it and you notice the frame and the mirror. Take the frame away and you see only yourself again (¶56). Breaking the mirror would mean no reflection (¶56–57).
- The lecturer draws on Alexander Knysh's explanation of fana: there is "no self-perception anymore", not "no soul" (¶57).
- Ibn Sina: he notices only the "sign of sanctity" (translated, probably "Janab al-Quds", which refers to Allah). If he notices himself, it is as a mirror and not "as it is ornamented with the pleasure" (¶57–58).
- This is the end of the first of the four journeys discussed earlier. It is "just the beginning". The second journey, "journeying through haqq with haqq", is "more pleasurable and more difficult" (¶59).
- Allah shows him his asma and sifat and how things are created by them (¶60).
- Hafiz is cited: the only veil between you and God is yourself (¶60).

## 7. Problems in the transcript
- (¶4) "a chapter unnecessary" is probably "a chapter on a mystic" or "on mysticism". "businessman Abu Saeed" and "Riyadh" are garbled; Riyadh may be riyadah, so the claim might be that Ibn Sina underwent training under Abu Saʿid.
- (¶4) "Namat" for the chapter is not used in the transcript, so "ninth chapter" is the lecturer's wording.
- (¶5) "first journey ... in annihilation or we call it arrival" is unclear.
- (¶7) "So this iradah is certainly older" is probably a mis-hearing.
- (¶11) "has no value" is ambiguous in context.
- (¶13, ¶15) "obedient to the transgressor" is probably "tranquil soul". The ending "proper to the .." is missing.
- (¶24) "wajhdun ilayh", "hope", "wajh which actually passes you" are garbled; wajd is probably meant.
- (¶25) "senseless" probably means "restless" or "sad".
- (¶26) the last sentences about operation and "align that to operate it" are garbled.
- (¶32) "Tawaqqul" is probably not "tawakkul" (trust in God); the standard second step is probably a different term (QUERY: check the Arabic).
- (¶34, ¶36) "Istifaad" probably istifaza.
- (¶44, ¶46) "crap up to this point" is probably "recap".
- (¶48) "Tatahareeq" is uncertain. "conjunction with a .." is truncated.
- (¶50) "ignorant ... written one of them" is garbled.
- (¶54) "reluctant" is probably "hesitant", and "how big in my soul" is garbled.
- (¶63) Q 80:40 as quoted does not clearly match the point (80:40 speaks of faces covered with dust); the lecturer may have meant 75:22–23. The reciter's wording needs checking.
- (¶16, 28, 30, 35, 62, 64, 66, 68) Questions are inaudible, so the Q&A is reconstructed from answers.
- The step numbers differ: ¶44 calls taghalgul the "fifth step", ¶48 calls tatahareeq the "seventh", ¶55 calls wusool the "ninth". So the count is nine steps in three ranks, but ¶39 says "the fourth step" for inqilab.
- (¶21) "pleasurable stolen looks" is also rendered "pleasurable, older look" in ¶23.
