# Lecture 02: Who the ʿĀrifs Are, and the Terms for Irfan

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan02.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture opens by recalling that Islamic irfan teaches people to see, hear and understand what is normally unseen, unheard and not understood (¶3). It then uses a string of Quranic verses to show that the Quran itself distinguishes a special class of believers, the knowers, from ordinary believers and unbelievers (¶7–19). Next it sets out the vocabulary of the course: irfan/maʿrifa, Sufism, mysticism and "agnosticism" (¶20–28). Finally it distinguishes two kinds of knowledge, acquired knowledge and "knowledge by presence", and contrasts ʿilm, which defines and limits, with maʿrifa, which aims at arriving and at purification (¶29–38). The lecture builds on Lecture 1 (definition of irfan, the muqarrabun, the awliyāʾ) and prepares the later discussion of knowledge, love and the path.

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Recap and the verse about those who do not see, hear or understand (¶3–6)
- Last week: there are things in this world "which we do not see and hear" and do not understand; Islamic irfan or mysticism "is to teach us how to see, hear and understand these things" (¶3).
- The lecturer cites the verse about those who have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, ears they do not hear with: "like animals, and even worse" (¶3–4).
- Puzzle posed to the class: animals do have eyes and ears. They see while walking and run away at a sound. So what is the comparison with animals about? (¶4)
- Answer, carried over from last week: there is "a machine inside the human being" that must translate what we see and hear so as to "reach the reality" (¶4). What we see and hear is only "at the surface... superficial" (¶5). Animals lack this machine (¶5).
- The unbelievers (kuffar) "see and hear", and would laugh and say they see better and more artistically than believers. The Quran concedes that they see but says they do not understand (¶5).
- The lecturer promises that this "enigma of having and not having, listening and not listening, being and not being" will return and that the course will make students familiar with "the mysteries of mysticism of seeing, being and not being, having and not having" (¶5).
- You see and hear with your senses, which "only give you a sight of it". The soul or machine is there but is "dead and blind" and "not working". Mysticism's task is to ignite it (¶6).
- Mysticism "has a beginning and no end", against the common claim that it has an end. The beginning is when the machine starts to work, and then "you see the wonders of this world" (¶6–7).

### 2.2 Two groups of people: ʿurafāʾ and jahāla (¶7)
- Two groups: those whose soul works, and those whose soul does not work. The first are ʿurafāʾ (plural of ʿārif, "someone who knows"). The others are jahāla, "those who don't know", who lack the capability to know and understand (¶7).
- The lecturer sets aside prophets and imams, who are "very special urafaa", and asks about ordinary people with special understanding (¶8). He is also not speaking of the kuffar. Ordinary believers do have understanding, but some people have a special understanding mentioned in the Quran (¶8).

### 2.3 Muqarrabun and the four categories (Q 4:69) (¶8–11)
- The muqarrabun were mentioned last week as "those who are brought near". But Allah is not far away: "He is very near". So being brought near must mean that "some veil which was between ordinary people and Allah is taken away", and they "understand far better" (¶8–9).
- Last week the muqarrabun were said to be of four categories, which are in Surat al-Nisaʾ, "whoever obeys Allah and the Prophet is with those whom Allah has favoured" (¶9, citing 4:69).
- "What is favouring? Certainly it is not wealth or a strong body. It is something else" (¶10).
- The categories: prophets (nabiyyīn), the truthful (ṣiddīqīn), the witnesses (shuhadāʾ), and, at the end, the righteous (ṣāliḥīn). We cannot reach the first three levels (¶10).
- Ṣāliḥīn are "those who deserve or those who have the capacity to be given this favour". Allah chooses siddiqeen from them, and then nabi: "It is only from this saleheen that Allah would choose the other 3 categories." The lecturer cites the prophets as an example, saying that Allah made them prophets because they were of the ṣāliḥīn (¶10–11).
- Example: Lut. "We made him enter into our mercy... He was from saaliheen" (Q 21:75 as cited). He was in this category, "the special mercy" (¶11).
- The ṣāliḥīn are people who "understand better than us, who see and hear better than us" (¶11).

### 2.4 Further verses showing a special class of believers (¶11–19)
- **Hearts that tremble (Q 8:2, cited from Sūrat al-Anfāl)** (¶11–13). The lecturer jokes about who in the room trembles at Allah's name: "even if we have we are not going to reveal this secret." These are not ordinary believers (¶12). Trembling needs experience: to be scared by something you must "have some sort of idea or experience about it". So these believers know Allah, which is why his name makes their hearts tremble (¶12–13).
- **The humble in prayer (Q 23:1–2)** (¶13–14). "It is not me certainly and many of you might not be like this." Khāshiʿ means "someone who is broken", more than humility, an inner experience that brings one "down inside yourself" (¶13). They "break down" when they stand before Allah in salah because of "their experience that they have of Allah" (¶14).
- The lecturer then raises the central question: "Can Allah be experienced at all? This is what mysticism is all about. Can we have a way or a canal or a path to Allah?" (¶14)
- **Allah's own witness to tawheed (Q 3:18)** (¶14–16). Called "one of the great verses of the quran for only people who have high knowledge can understand" (¶14). Allah, the angels and "those endued with knowledge" witness that there is no god but He. Allah has knowledge of his own tawheed, and our knowledge of tawheed cannot be compared with it (¶14–15). "Ulul ʿilm" is "another expression of aarif" (¶15).
- Tawheed is "not as simple as just saying that there is no god but Allah." Deeper understanding cannot be done "by our intellect". One must "start and ignite that machine inside us, the soul", which is the only part able to grasp tawheed (¶15–16).
- Layers of meaning: each Quranic verse has 7 layers of depth, beneath each 70, beneath each of those 700 (cited as something "we have heard") (¶16). The same concept is understood differently at different levels (¶17).
- **Fear of Allah (Q 35:28)** (¶17). Only those who have knowledge fear Allah. We fear his punishment more than him. Some people fear him and not his punishments, with a fear of a kind "we cannot explain", "not fear of punishment" (¶17). "We cannot deny that" such people are mentioned in the Quran (¶17).
- **Ulū al-albāb** ("those who have inner intellect") are named as another expression (¶18).
- **Awliyāʾ Allāh (Q 10:62)**: no fear on them nor grief. Again "a group of people who are different from us". The question is how someone becomes a friend of Allah and "connect[s] to Allah very closely", depending on how wali is translated (¶18).
- **A good life (Q 16:97, Sūrat al-Nahl)**: whoever, male or female, does a righteous deed while a believer, will be given a "hayāt ṭayyiba". The lecturer links ʿamila ṣāliḥan to ṣāliḥīn, so whoever acts righteously becomes of the ṣāliḥīn (¶18–19).
- Ṭayyiba means "clean and pure from the filth of this world". They "live in this world but they do not live in the filth of this world", transcending "the worldly issues which other people are infected with". This pure life "is of course what mystics are after" (¶19).
- This explains why mystics accept "so much hardship, lots of training and purification to find a glimpse and experience of Allah". Love of Allah "has been put in their heart and that's why they are glad to seek" (¶19).
- The awliyāʾ were treated last week and are only touched on here (¶20).

### 2.5 Terminology (¶20–28)
- The lecturer says the foregoing was only an introduction and the course proper now starts with terminology (¶20).
- **Irfan** comes from maʿrifa, "knowledge and understanding". Irfan means possessing maʿrifa. The difference between maʿrifa and ʿilm will be discussed later, although "in religious terminology ilm and maarifah are used unanimously" (¶21). Maʿrifa is the knowledge of Allah, "knowledge of world matters" is ʿilm. One who has maʿrifa is an ʿārif, plural ʿurafāʾ (¶21).
- **Sufism** (¶22–24).
  - There are "many theories" for the name. The lecturer says "sufiyah" was first used by "Jami in 255AH" in the 3rd century, but the attribute goes back to the 2nd century. The first person called Sufi was Abu Hashim al-Kufi (¶22).
  - Some derive it from Latin "scientia" (knowledge), but the lecturer does not accept this. His preferred explanation is that these people wore rough woollen clothes called "suf" (¶22).
  - Sufism is "not very much favoured especially by Shia and Sunni scholars". Early mystics borrowed concepts and practices from other religions, including non-monotheist ones, and "some of them left their shariah all together" (¶23).
  - Hence "the term Sufi and sufiyah became a type of attribute which was not very much favoured by religious people". Many Shia books say bad things about it and report hadiths that the imams were against Sufis and so against mysticism (¶23).
  - But "we have to distinguish between the right and the wrong practise, right and wrong intention". Sufism is another term for ʿurafāʾ and "we can use or we can change it" (¶24).
- **Mysticism** (¶25–27).
  - Usually used by Christian ʿurafāʾ because it deals with "mystery". The lecturer says Christian faith is "shrouded with mysteries" (he names the idea of "takmeel") and Christians say all faith and understanding of God is a mystery (¶25).
  - Muslims use it for different reasons. Tawheed is a tidy concept everyone understands at some level, but "what more you go into that world of Allah the more wonders you see and the more mysterious it would become." So it is "a right term to use" (¶25).
  - "When you encounter that dignity and greatness of Allah you are mystified." Mysticism deals with "bewilderness and mystification" (¶26). "We become more bewildered" as one proceeds (¶28).
- **"Agnosticism"** (¶26–27). The lecturer says this term comes from ancient Greek and relates to knowledge, and that for precision it suits Islamic irfan, since irfan deals with maʿrifa of Allah and love of Allah (¶26–27).
- Maʿrifa and love go hand in hand. Some mystics emphasise maʿrifa, some love. "The more you find maarifah of Allah the more you find that love in your heart" (¶26). "You do not love him because you do not know him": we love people for known features, and "out of the blue you cannot fall in love" (¶27).

### 2.6 Two kinds of knowledge: ḥuṣūlī and ḥuḍūrī (¶29–35)
- Philosophers and mystics divide knowledge into two (¶29).
- **Ilm al-ḥuṣūlī ("ilmul husul")**: acquired knowledge, by reasoning or the senses. "Husul means to acquire." You get concepts, analyse them, and acquire "what corresponds to those concepts from outside" (¶29).
- **Ilm al-ḥuḍūrī (transcribed "knowledge with pleasant", "ilmul khuzu/khusu")**: knowledge that is "with you inside your soul". You do not get the concepts and "don't need any means to acquire it" (¶29–30).
- Mysticism "is defined as the urge to reach out to the infinite by ilmul khuzu" (¶30). The lecturer admits he is "rushing forward because these are very difficult concepts" (¶30).
- Examples. The first is one's own self: "the most important ilmul khuzu that we have", though many "don't know that our selves exist at all" (¶30). Next, "a very vague idea that we have for Allah inside us" (¶31). Hunger is felt by the soul, "it comes from within" (¶31). Anxiety is experienced from deep inside. There is a cause, "but that cause is not what that feeling is" (¶31). Pain is the same (¶32).
- Analogy: seeing an object produces an image in the mind, which is acquired because of the object. But the anxiety it causes "comes from somewhere inside my soul" (¶31).
- Presence knowledge is "totally grasped and there is no missing of it". Even if hunger is false, you feel something "very real" (¶32).
- You cannot concentrate and examine how a feeling "confuses" you. "For some people it's possible but for ordinary people it's not possible". Mystics are after "knowledge with present without development without any effort" (¶32).
- We have it for some basic feelings but not for what is outside us, which "has to be acquired" (¶33).
- The lecturer says a line of poetry from Imam Ali concerns the matter, with the wording "there is no masqook knowledge unless there is a masmoon, ie. inner knowledge", but he says he has "forgotten the poetry at the moment" (¶33).
- The transcript says "usually for us ilmul khuzu is outside us and ilmul khusu is what is inside us" (¶34). This seems garbled (see section 7).
- "You cannot know god by ilmul khusu... You just get the feeling and awareness." Looking at the sky you say "this should have a creator", but you cannot concentrate and find who he is "at this stage but inshallah by the end of this course" (¶34).
- The two types are "a theme of philosophy". Acquired knowledge is received by ʿaql, "theoretical intellect", "by which we reason, we understand and argue" (¶34–35). This is like philosophy and science: the subject matter differs but the reasoning is the same (¶35).
- The knowledge irfan deals with is not from analysis, argument or reasoning but "comes from human sight". It will be discussed later (¶35).

### 2.7 ʿIlm versus maʿrifa (¶36–38)
- Religious texts treat ʿilm and maʿrifa as the same, "but for us just to make a difference" (¶36).
- **ʿIlm** aims at understanding and is limited to "the realm of time and space". To understand you must limit and categorise. Every science begins by categorising and defining. "When you define something you limit it", hence Arabic ḥadd, with ḥudūd as limits. Example: "haiwan-e-natiq" (rational animal) (¶36).
- **Maʿrifa** "doesn't aim at understanding rather it aims at arriving", since it is presence knowledge. "You arrive at hunger. You don't try to understand hunger." It is "beyond time and space" and "aims at inside and self-purification" (¶37). The transcript says it "rewards limitations", which seems to be a mis-hearing (see section 7).
- Purification is "polishing the soul so that it starts working". The machine "is rough", and begins to work only after purification starts (¶37).
- Summary: "What mysticism is about? It is to arrive... and not to define god. Defining god is limited." The lecturer says Nahj al-Balagha has that whoever defines God has limited him. "We cannot define god. He only can arrive at him." Arriving "has a very long story", and the beginning of the "world of wonders" is when one arrives, to be continued next week (¶38).

## 7. Problems in the transcript
- (¶1) "Auzubillah Minashaitan Nirajeem" opening formula transcribed phonetically; ¶39 closing blessing also phonetic ("aale hittahereen").
- (¶29) "knowledge with pleasant" is almost certainly "knowledge by presence" (ḥuḍūrī). Spelled "khuzu" (¶29–30), "khusu" (¶32, 34), "khuzul" (¶37), "husul" (¶29). In ¶32–34 the term "ilmul khusu" is sometimes used where the sense is "confusion", and ¶34 seems to reverse the two terms.
- (¶32) "how it confuses" seems a garble; possibly "how it [the hunger] is made". ¶33 echoes "how this confusion is made". Meaning unclear.
- (¶22) "Jami in 255AH": the person and date are unclear. Jāmī the poet lived in the 9th century AH, so a different person or date is likely meant. "Sufism... first used... in the 3rd century" vs 255 AH is consistent with the 3rd century. "sifiyah" typo for sufiyah.
- (¶22) "Latin 'scientia'": a lecturer's claim that is uncertain; he rejects it himself.
- (¶25) "takmeel" is unclear, possibly "taslees/tathlīth" (Trinity) or "taʿmīl"; unresolved.
- (¶26–27) "agnosticism/agnostic" most probably is "gnosticism / gnostic" (Greek gnosis). The transcript's "agnosticsism" is likely a mis-hearing or slip. Used as the lecturer's English word for irfan, with no negation intended.
- (¶27) "which makes them more productive" is unclear; possibly "more passionate/attached".
- (¶28) "up to the pace of one who uses it" is garbled.
- (¶33) "masqook ... masmoon" garbled; probably an Arabic pair meaning "outer/inner" or similar; unidentified.
- (¶37) "it rewards limitations" and "You have to put them in relation with each other" are garbled; probably "it removes/transcends limitations".
- (¶38) "to arrive taqwa" is garbled; likely "to arrive at God". "He only can arrive at him" is unclear.
- (¶7) "jahala" and "ulul ilam" (¶15) spelled variantly. "immah" for aʾimma, "hades" for ḥadīth throughout.
- (¶3) The lecturer cites 7:179 as "they have heart..." (translating Q 7:179, consistent).
- (¶13) The text says "I mean it's more than humbleness" about khāshiʿ; mild comparison of terms.
- (¶5) "kuffar" is applied to anyone who "sees but does not understand"; the lecturer's own claim.
- (¶16) Verse "7 layers... 70... 700" is stated with "we have heard"; the source is not given.
- (¶23) Slight apparent tension: Sufism disliked because of borrowing, yet "Sufism is another term used for urafaa and we can use it or change it" (¶24).
- (¶34) "You can't do it at this stage" seems to mean "by acquired knowledge".
- (¶32) "I might be false hunger" is a garble for "it might be false hunger".
