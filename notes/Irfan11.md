# Lecture 11: The Origins of Islamic Mysticism: Alleged Outside Sources, the Early Mystics, and the Imams

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan11.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
This is the first lecture on the history of mysticism in Islam, covering how mystical thought and practice began and how it later became the tariqas or Sufi orders (¶3). The lecturer rejects the common Western-scholarly search for an outside source (Christian monasticism, Platonism and Neoplatonism, Hindu and Buddhist mysticism) and argues that Islamic mysticism comes from the Quran and Hadith themselves (¶3–4, ¶23–24). Outside influence is at most one of technique, not doctrine (¶6, ¶22–23). He then traces mysticism from the time of the Prophet (Ahl al-Suffa, Abu Dharr, Salman, Uways al-Qarani, the "eight ascetics"; ¶34–44). Next he uses Annemarie Schimmel to argue that the roots of Sufism lie in Imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq and the earlier Imams, so Sufi ideas were shaped by Shia sources (¶45–61). He closes with a typology of early mystics (fear, renunciation, love; ¶61–63) and begins profiling them: Hasan al-Basri and Rabiʿa for fear and love, Ibrahim Adham and tawakkul for renunciation (¶64–92).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Framing and the Western "outside source" thesis (¶3–4)
- Today's topic is the history of mysticism: how it began in early Islam, and how it developed into "tariqa or the different orders of Sufism" (¶3).
- Western books on mysticism look for an outside source because they "cannot believe that Islam as a religion of law and jurisprudence can produce mystics" (¶3).
- The idea is strongest among Western thinkers influenced by Christianity. Example: a prominent theologian said the God of Islam is a powerful "big Josh" waiting for people to make mistakes so as to punish them (¶3).
- Rhetorical question: how could that picture of God give rise to love and a mystical relationship between human and Creator? (¶4)
- The trend is changing. Fairer scholars such as Annemarie Schimmel "confess" that Islamic mysticism came out of the Quran and Hadith (¶4).
- The alleged sources are taken in turn: Christianity, Platonism, Hindu and Buddhist mysticism (¶4, ¶12, ¶21).

### 2.2 Alleged source 1: Christianity (¶5–11)
- Claim: Muslim practices resemble those of Christian monks, and the very word Sufi (harsh woolen, usually black, garments) is said to come from monasticism (¶5).
- Christian mysticism in the past was mostly fear, harsh asceticism and abstinence. The stress on love is newer (¶5).
- Lecturer's reply: no one claims the core of Islamic mysticism was Christian. Only methods are at issue, such as abstinence, recitation of the divine name and collective chanting (¶6).
- Celibacy was honoured in monasticism, but Muslim mystics have wives, so it "didn't go very well" with their practice (¶6).
- Quranic discussion: near the end of Surah Hadid, God places "kindness and mercy" in the hearts of those who truly follow Jesus (¶6–7).
  - Genuine followers of Isa would have that mercy (¶7).
  - Jesus' quality was mercy to everyone, even sinners. He sat in the houses of those drinking wine, which was unusual to the Jewish clergy (¶7).
- Hadith or saying of Jesus: asked by the clergy why he sits with sinners, he said "I am a doctor, I am a tabib and I look for patients" (¶8).
  - Lecturer's caution: we are not qualified to do the same, because when we sit with sinners we end up doing as they do.
  - One should only do this when sure of oneself and one's heart (¶8–9).
- The verse on rahbaniyya (monasticism): it comes from rahba, fear. Lecturer distinguishes mysticism of fear, of love, of abstinence and of knowledge, to be discussed later (¶9).
  - God did not prescribe rahbaniyya for them. He prescribed only seeking His pleasure (¶9–11).
  - They wanted more than ordinary worship, "left the world for the love of God" and isolated themselves, improvising a method God had not written for them (¶10–11).
  - Those who did it in faith were rewarded. "I'm telling you, this is not the way" (¶11).
- Conclusion: Muslim khanqah and zawiya are "completely different" from Christian monasticism. The claim of influence is "very difficult to prove" because practices, concepts and thought differ (¶11–12).

### 2.3 Alleged source 2: Plato and Neoplatonism (¶12–20)
- Plato is called a "mystic philosopher of Greece", before Jesus. He influenced many mystics, e.g. Ibn Arabi, and Ismaili thought is "completely based on Neoplatonic ideas" (¶12).
- Core ideas: God in the absolute is unknowable. When He creates, He manifests names and attributes, and there we come into contact with Him. Ibn Arabi says the same (¶12–13).
- Audience exchange: how can the essence be unknowable yet be "annihilated" into? (¶14)
  - Lecturer: annihilation means knowing there is nothing in the world but God. The attributes are "signals about that essence", but the essence is unreachable (¶15).
  - Quranic link: wajhullah, the "face of God". Whether in the worlds of mulk, malakut or jabarut, "you only see the face of Allah", and east and west belong to Him (¶15–16).
- The relation between essence and attributes is "one of the most difficult and excruciating questions". Many mystics have fallen into great errors over it (¶16).
- Ibn Arabi is popular because he has given the "most acceptable theory", the one that best fits mystical experience. Lecturer will not call it "absolutely true" (¶16–17).
- Plato's first creation is the "universal intellect", then the "universal soul" above it. Mystics easily compared this to hadith (¶19–20).
- The lecturer says Plato was "a monk" and a student of Dawud/Sulayman, so his knowledge came from prophetic sources. Paganism and idolatry are another matter (¶19).
- Main reply: Neoplatonism entered Islamic mysticism only in later centuries. The early mystics of the 2nd–4th centuries had none of it, except a few who misunderstood it, like "Ismaili philosophers" (¶20).
  - It came in when mystics wanted to systematise theoretical Irfan (irfan-e nazari) (¶20–21).

### 2.4 Alleged source 3: Hindu and Buddhist mysticism (¶21–23)
- The concept of fana (annihilation) is somewhat similar to nirvana. The difference between them is promised for later (¶21).
- Forceful argument: many early mystics came from Khurasan, then bordering China and India and including Afghanistan. People from Bukhara and Balkh were neighbours of Buddhists (¶21–22).
- Lecturer's categorisation: doctrinal influence versus technique.
  - Doctrinally the concepts are "so different" that influence is hard to think (¶22).
  - Techniques such as khalwa (seclusion), silence and long meditation may have been taken from Hindu or Buddhist practice, because they are absent from the hadith and the Imams (¶22).
- Julian Baldick (discussed earlier) holds that Buddhism may have influenced techniques but "certainly" not doctrine (¶23).

### 2.5 The Quran itself is mystical; Western scholarship's narrow training (¶23–33)
- Those who say the Quran is only a book of law and "powerful rulers sitting and trying to punish people" have not understood it (¶23).
- Examples: believers have the greatest love for God (Q 2:165, as cited) and "who should rely on God" (cited as 3:122; ¶23).
- Even in verses on law, the end of the verse carries "a mystical allusion", a "mystical signal" for why one must do it (¶24).
- Why Westerners miss this: Western scholarship grew through individual teachers specialised in one aspect (law, history), and students inherit only that aspect (¶24).
  - Anecdote of a famous theologian who said he learned all he knew about Islam from "Russell", who knew only tiny bits of jurisprudence and history (¶24–25).
  - Anecdote of a Persian-studies woman at Cambridge whose speciality was an obscure poet ("Mancharish") because "my teacher was expert in this". She did not like him (¶25–26).
- Pattern: Western researchers ask "where did it come from?" of everything Islamic (jurisprudence, mysticism, tafsir, even the Quran, hadith authentication and chains of transmission) and assume Romans, Jews or Christians, because "Arabs could not make" them (¶26–27).
- Because they deny prophethood ("an imposter"), they must find a source for the Quran (¶29).
  - Lecturer: even a hundred scholars given the Bible and other books could not produce such a work. Their analysis is correct on its own premises, since they do not accept the divine (¶29–31).
- Audience pushback: people around the Prophet were clever and hadith authentication was a Muslim improvisation (¶31).
  - Lecturer: then you bring "other people into the picture". The qiraʿat (recitations) show the system. Early text had no dots, so recitation depended on memory heard from the Prophet (e.g. a-fala taʿqilun vs ya'qilun). Hence the limited seven or ten recitation schools. This is "amazing and that's why they cannot believe" (¶32–33).

### 2.6 Mysticism in the time of the Prophet (¶34–44)
- Ahl al-Suffa: Meccan migrants without homes, lodged on a bench (suffa, "like sofa") around the Prophet's mosque. Some were very rich and left everything, so they were "good hearted people with strong faith" (¶34).
- Some say most of them were mystics "not all the time". Three companions are named by Sunnis and Shia: Abu Dharr, Salman, Uways al-Qarani (¶35, ¶38).
  - Abu Dharr: ascetic. He was deported and died alone in a far desert, fulfilling the Prophet's saying "May God have mercy on Abu Dharr, he lives alone, dies alone and will be raised alone" (¶35).
  - Salman al-Farsi: known for knowledge, not (as Abu Dharr was) for asceticism (¶35). Lecturer is unsure whether the reports are true: he reportedly lived 200, 250 or 300 years, and was about 250 when he met the Prophet (¶36).
    - Salman was a historian, learned from Buddhists, then became Christian under monks and scholars at "the peak of religion at that time". Hearing of the coming Prophet, he went to Medina, was captured and sold into slavery (a prince, son of a great Iranian clergyman). The Prophet found him working for a Jewish woman, bought and freed him, and Salman refused to leave (¶37–38).
    - After that "you don't hear anything about Salman's knowledge anymore". He gave up everything to the Prophet and later fought with Amir al-Muminin (¶38).
  - Uways al-Qarani: from Yemen, "a sign of divine grace". Love of the Prophet fell in his heart without hearing a verse (¶38–39).
    - His old mother would not let him leave Yemen. Once she allowed a visit on condition he return the same day, and he found the Prophet away (Tabuk "or somewhere") and went back (¶39–40).
    - The Prophet said "I am smelling the fragrance of love from Yemen" (¶40).
    - He later came to Medina after his mother died, but the Prophet had passed away. "See how Allah had made the story" (¶41).
    - He became a close disciple of Amir al-Muminin and fought in his battles, especially Siffin, "a great healer" (¶41).
    - Lecturer notes the story is in hagiographies such as Tazkirat al-Awliya, and he does not vouch for it (¶39).
- The "eight ascetics" (Zuhhad Thamaniya): Uways always at the top, then Amir b. Abd Qays, Harim b. Hayyan, Abu Muslim "al-Jarrar" and others (¶40–41).
  - Garbled remark: "four were with Ali", some with Muawiya, and Hasan al-Basri was ambiguous, to be treated later (¶42).
- Hadith (told loosely): the Prophet asks a companion how he is, and he says he sees hell and paradise and can tell who is in each. The Prophet hushes him ("never speak of such things") but says "I know that you are truthful" (¶42–43).
- Lecturer's lament: companions who became prominent politically were "harsh, cruel" and militaristic, which is all that is remembered. Many companions were "real mystics" who explained their experiences (¶43–44).

### 2.7 Annemarie Schimmel and Imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq (¶45–61)
- Schimmel (in her history of Islamic mysticism): the roots of Islamic mysticism go back to Imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq (¶45).
  - Lecturer: most mystics were Sunni "by name" because they grew up in Sunni environments. On inspection most were disciples or relatives of Imam Sadiq and other Imams (¶45–46).
  - The ideas they spread came from "refined concepts about Islam and the Quran" (¶46).
- Hadith of the four levels of the Quran, attributed to Imam Sadiq (Bihar al-Anwar, Misbah al-Shariʿa; ¶46–47).
  - Four levels: expression (ibara), allusion (ishara), subtle graces (lataʾif) and realities (haqaʾiq) (¶47).
  - Expression is for the common people (awamm), allusion for the elite (khawass), lataʾif for the saints (awliya), realities for the prophets (¶47–48).
  - Expression is a sentence with a meaning. Allusion points to something hidden. Lataʾif are "touches of grace" that God gives to a heart. Realities are what God actually means (¶47).
  - Schimmel: this hierarchy of interior knowledge sketches the structure later Sufis developed in stages (maqamat) and stations, each with different meaning for different people (¶48).
  - Example: tawakkul. For ordinary people it is saying "I rely on You"; for the elite it means "not having any independence whatsoever" (¶49).
- Schimmel: Imam Sadiq was the first to speak the "language of love". Lecturer says the Imams were not known to Western scholarship, and love is common in the language of the Imams (e.g. a hadith of Imam Baqir in al-Kafi; ¶49–50).
  - Misbah al-Shariʿa: love is a "divine fire that devours man completely" (¶50–51). Lecturer: this predates Rabiʿa al-Adawiyya (end of 2nd century; ¶50).
- Schimmel concludes that Shia and Sufi ideas were interdependent at earliest stages. Lecturer's gloss: the Sunni approach alone "couldn't give rise" to this understanding, so Sufism was "under complete influence of Shia force" (¶51).
- Audience Q (¶52–57): why, then, are there so many Sunni, and so few Shia, mystics?
  - Lecturer: Sunni mystics could not reject their inherited love of Abu Bakr and Umar, so they kept that and took the ideas that fit (¶53).
  - Shia were completely under the Imams and said only "this is what Jaʿfar al-Sadiq said", never speaking from themselves. Hence there are no prominent Shia mystic figures (¶56–57).
  - Analogy: when Salman met the Prophet "there was no Salman anymore". You hear nothing from Ali during the Prophet's life but a lot from Umar: "that Sun has gone and now there was moon shine" (¶56).
  - Sunnis repeated what they heard in their own language, and it grew into tariqas (¶57).
- Schimmel: the Imams' thoughts must have worked "beneath the surface" until they appeared in Dhu al-Nun al-Misri (Egypt), Bayazid al-Bistami (Iran) and al-Harith al-Muhasibi (Iraq) (¶58).
  - Lecturer: what they said was an "echo" of Imam Baqir and Imam Sadiq. Rabiʿa's poems cannot compare with Munajat al-Muhibbin of Imam Sajjad or the supplications of al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya, which Shia scholars recite when speaking of love, while Sunnis lack these (¶58–59).
- Correction of Schimmel: the four-level hadith is reported earlier, from Imam Sajjad and Imam Husayn, and then from Ali, so the layered understanding is traceable to the Prophet's time (¶59–60). Lecturer says he has quoted all the references elsewhere (¶59).
  - Nahj al-Balagha is full of love of God and attachment, where "there is no more you there at all" (¶60).
  - Other companions not linked to Ali do not speak this way, so it was "novel" to many Muslims (¶61).

### 2.8 Types of early mystical attitude (¶61–63)
- Even Prophets differ in attitude, some more fearful and some more hopeful (¶61).
- Hadith from Imam Sadiq: Yahya (Prophet John) feared God, and Isa had hope, though with mixed hope and fear. Yahya's fear overwhelmed his hope. Quranic stories of Yahya are said to show this (¶62).
- Some mystics talk of God and death in a frightening way. Others were austere and renounced the world. Others had love (¶62–63).
- Three categories: mystics of fear, mystics of renunciation, mystics of love. These are "the most outstanding feature" and not exclusive. Love ultimately became "the victorious side" of mysticism, which is strange for Western scholars who think of ascetic mysticism (¶63–64).

### 2.9 Mysticism of fear: Hasan al-Basri (¶64–72)
- Hasan al-Basri: one of the eight ascetics, died 110 AH. Amir al-Muminin died 40 AH, so he lived long and was already grown then. His position at the time was "ambivalent" (Muawiya, Uthman, Ali), "a very difficult personality to analyse" (¶64).
- Myths: prayed fajr with the wudu of isha for 50 years (not sleeping). Lecturer: renewing wudu is mustahabb anyway. This is a common hagiographic motif in Tazkirat al-Awliya (¶65).
- He was a muhaddith, taught tafsir and fiqh, and was a "man of danger" (¶66, partly garbled). Many of his hadith are mursal, omitting the companion.
  - When asked, he said that whenever he omits the companion, it is Ali, but the Umayyad era made the name dangerous to say. So his mursal hadith are famously from Ali, from the Prophet (¶66–68).
- Saying of Hasan: "I am not surprised at one who goes to hell; I am surprised at one who gets to paradise" (¶68, garbled). A man heard this and reported it to Imam Sajjad (¶69).
  - Imam Sajjad's answer: "this is the view of Hasan al-Basri but we have a different view". I am surprised at how someone manages to get through hell, given hope in God's mercy (¶70).
  - Lecturer: this fear is of hell and punishment, not love, and not khashya (¶70–72).

### 2.10 Mysticism of love: Rabiʿa al-Adawiyya (¶72–74)
- Rabiʿa walked with a torch in one hand and a bowl of water in the other, to burn paradise and put out hell "so that no one feels hell anymore and no one desires paradise and everyone worships God just for the sake of God" (¶72–73).
- Lecturer thinks this is likely "under the influence and inspiration" of Imam Ali's talks (¶72).
- Mysticism of love developed later through people like Rumi (transcribed "rather"). It is interesting that a woman started this, as men "probably" lacked this romantic idea of loving God, putting the Imams aside (¶73–74).

### 2.11 Mysticism of renunciation: Khurasan and Ibrahim Adham (¶74–79)
- Renunciation comes mainly from Khurasan (northeast Iran, Mashhad now). Was it Buddhist influence? Hard to tell, as the attitude is also in the Quran within limits (¶74–75).
- Khurasan is also the hotspot of Sunni traditions (Muslim, Bukhari, Ibn Maja). Traditionists had to travel to Khurasan to be prominent (¶76).
- Three renunciants were influenced by Imam Sadiq and Imam Kazim. Tonight only Ibrahim Adham (the others next session; ¶77).
  - Died 162 AH, 14 years after Imam Sadiq (148). He was born and raised in Balkh. Junayd of Baghdad called him the "key of mystical sciences" (¶77).
  - He learned from Imam Sadiq young, with hadith from the Imam (¶78).
  - Like the Buddha, he was a prince who renounced his princely life and became proverbial for poverty, abstinence and tawakkul (¶78).
  - Tawakkul training: a mystic goes into the desert for 40 days without food or provision (¶78).

### 2.12 Tawakkul: a practical discussion (¶79–92)
- Can we practise this? Yes, differently, but "we have to do something" (¶79).
- Anecdote (¶80–81): when the war began, the lecturer, a talaba in Qum, attended a voluntary military-training camp. Tulab were dropped in the desert for 24 hours with no food or water and had to find the way back. They asked how to make wudu, were told tayammum, and a funny fiqh argument began over voluntary lack of water.
- Debate: if God gave you hands and a brain, should you do your part first? Tawakkul begins when you have done your part and then leave the rest to Him (¶82–83).
  - The mystic going into the desert is not "sitting at home" but acting in tawakkul (¶86).
  - Example: Imam Khomeini fighting America, which ordinary people say won't succeed, "a very simple example" (¶86).
- Some mystics did the desert trial several times. Lecturer says "no one should do it now" or they lose their life. Life itself gives enough opportunities (¶87, ¶89–90).
- Reference to Jesus tempted by Satan in the desert (the test of throwing himself down), which is not advised in Shia practice, though many did (¶88–89).
- Closing story: on the warfronts, young men spoke of God so that the lecturer, as a clergyman, felt "miserable" and "nothing". Imam Khomeini said this world is rahmah. Western technology came on one side and the youngsters had nothing. The BBC calls them "silly" and suicidal, so these are "two different worldviews" (¶91–92).
