# Lecture 10: Night Vigil, Seclusion and Remembrance (the last three of the five pillars of suluk), and the Opening of Sufi Tariqas

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan10.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture completes the discussion of suluk (wayfaring) through Kashani's five-part summary: silence, hunger, night vigil, seclusion and continuous remembrance (¶6). Silence and hunger were covered earlier; here the lecturer treats night vigil (¶7–40), seclusion (¶41–54) and remembrance (¶54–65), then briefly notes the stations of the path (¶66) and opens the topic of Sufi tariqas, with a discussion of the word tariqa in the Quran (¶67–73). The running argument is that the Quran itself underlies these practices, that ehsan leads to God-given knowledge and wisdom (¶11–12), and that Islam is therefore not merely a "legal religion" but "a very mystical religion actually" (¶40). The lecture also opens with course logistics (assessment) (¶3–5).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Assessment logistics (¶3–5)
- Essay 60%, written exam 40%; the essay should be analytical (¶3). Sample exam questions will be given; topics are on handouts, other topics allowed if confirmed with the lecturer; selective reading is limited because English-language scholarship on Islamic mysticism is very large (¶4). Deadline: two weeks before the course ends, so towards end of January, with the last session around 7 February (¶4–5). (Logistics only.)

### 2.2 Recap: Kashani's five elements (¶6–7)
- Practical side of mysticism is suluk, with many levels and dimensions. A Persian saying from Kashani includes "all main elements and concepts" common to mystics of the world regardless of creed: silence, hunger, night vigils, seclusion and continuous remembrance of God (¶6).
- Night vigil appears in Christian, Buddhist and Islamic mysticism (¶7).
- Contrast: there is no abundant Quranic evidence for silence or hunger, but there is "ample evidence" for night vigil, which leads to mystical experience (¶7).

### 2.3 Night vigil and ehsan: the muhsinin (¶8–13)
- Quranic evidence cited from Surah al-Dhariyat (verses on the righteous in gardens who slept little at night and sought forgiveness at dawn) (¶8–9).
- Those who rise before dawn are "among the mohsineen"; the concept of muhsinin is "very, very important in the Quran" (¶9). God's mercy is close to the muhsin, and that mercy is "the source of all these mystical experiences", beyond ordinary human understanding and senses (¶9). Those who strive for God, He guides to His paths, and God is with the muhsinin (¶9).
- Levels of faith as mystics describe them: Islam (confession and imitating actions); Iman (serious worship, prayers and fasting acquire meaning, certainty); and a further depth, ehsan, which the person reaches through mystical practice (¶10).
- The Quran says the natural outcome of ehsan is knowledge and wisdom (hikmah). Examples: Yusuf, and Musa, before prophethood, given hukm and ilm on reaching maturity; "in this way We reward the muhsinin" (¶11).
- The lecturer reads "in this way" as a general rule: "the reward of the mohsenoon is nothing but knowledge" (¶12). Mystical knowledge "does not come by reading the books or analyzing your logical concepts"; God puts it in the heart, as in the hadith "knowledge is a light/alive" (as transcribed: "knowledge is alive") (¶12).
- Other aspects of ehsan: giving the due of the beggar and the deprived from one's property; reliance on God (tawakkul) (¶13).

### 2.4 Examples of unschooled knowledge (¶13–16)
- Shaikh Rajabali, mentioned by a sister last week, not highly studied yet a man of great understanding (¶13).
- Shaikh Ismail Dehlvi (as transcribed), who died about a year ago, in Tehran, famous, people came weekly to his majlis; almost illiterate and without hadith knowledge, he would state a concept and then ask the audience for the hadith or ayah on it, sure one existed (¶14–15). The lecturer calls this "very amazing" (¶15).
- Formal study is still needed: mystics stress that going without it is of no benefit; Imam Khomeini, Kashani and others all did formal study (¶15). Reason: formal learning is repetition of concepts from a teacher; when matched with "godly knowledge" in the heart, the two "resonate with each other, they support and reinforce each other" (¶16).
- Mystics chose certain hadith and made them common, leaving others, because their heart-knowledge resonated with those hadith (¶16).

### 2.5 Hikmah (¶17–18)
- Someone asked the lecturer to write something about Jesus for Christians' greeting cards; he was drawn to the verse where Isa says "I brought hikmah" (¶17).
- Wisdom is "to know what to do", what is good and bad; God gives it to some so they use knowledge properly, "because most of us don't know what's good and what's bad" (¶17). Most people waste over half their life learning that something is bad; one with hikmah knows from the beginning (¶18).
- Verse: God gives hikmah to whom He wills, and whoever receives it receives abundant good (¶18).

### 2.6 Surah al-Muzzammil and the philosophy of the night (¶18–21)
- Opening verses (rise by night, half or a little less or more, recite in measured tones) and the verse that night rising is "most potent for governing the soul" in one translation (the lecturer says of the translation "I have the word unworkable") (¶18–19).
- By day there is "prolonged occupation with ordinary duties", so pure dhikr is possible at night (¶19).
- Physiological argument: organs rested, memory fresh, attention not dispersed, stomach empty; this affects ibadah and "magferah"; night prayer shows seriousness: "if Prophet should do it, why not us?" (¶19–20).
- Prophet needed it because a weighty message was to be revealed; preparation means practice. "This is what mysticism is about: we prepare the soul to receive... the knowledge which is everywhere"; "There is no hindrance from the other side, the hindrance is inside us" (¶20–21).

### 2.6a Practical advice on dosage (¶23–27)
- Half an hour is enough to start, then gradually one hour, one and a half, "probably not more than that" (¶23). The half, third and two-thirds of the night were relaxed by the closing verse of Muzzammil ("recite what is easy") (¶23).
- Salat al-layl can be compressed (about half an hour) or prolonged with long surahs (Mulk, Yasin, Muzzammil), or with supplication; warning against exceeding limits, which causes a negative effect so that "at one point, we just stop and we just quit"; many examples of people imitating the Prophet beyond their capacity (¶23–24).
- A hadith of Amir al-Mu'minin: a little worship that you continue is better than much that tires you (paraphrased from a garbled sentence) (¶25). Choose one dhikr or dua and keep it; e.g. Surah Muzzammil nightly takes about three minutes; keep it for two years (¶25).
- Teachers wanted students to keep a practice at least a year (¶26). Hadith: Imam Jafar al-Sadiq was asked how long to reach the state of "those who seek forgiveness at dawn"; he said one year is enough (¶26). After a year the title "speaks for you"; this does not mean stopping, rather one gains more power to want to do more (¶27).

### 2.7 Night vigil in the Quran: further verses (¶27–30)
- Some think these verses are marginal and only the daily prayers matter (¶27–28). Verse from Surah al-Isra on prayer from the sun's decline to the darkness of night and the Fajr recitation witnessed; and "it may be that your Lord raises you to a praiseworthy station" (maqam mahmud), which the lecturer links to tahajjud: night vigil raises people to a higher station of understanding (¶28).
- Observation: the Quranic order begins from Zuhr; jurisprudence manuals (risalah) start with Zuhr; Zuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha come in close succession, then sleep, then night prayer, then Fajr, then the period from Fajr to noon has no prayer (¶29–30). Why? "Because you're busy": it is the effective time for work and study (a guess) (¶30).
- Warning about prayer becoming mere habit or imitation: some who pray daily still commit what Muslims should not (¶32).

### 2.8 Q&A on tahajjud, and the Nimatullahi Sufis (¶33–40)
- See section 6 for the question. Lecturer: the Prophet and the Imams did salat al-layl with Quran recitation and many duas, lasting over two hours, longer on specific nights (¶34). Tahajjud without salat al-layl is not much advised, though mystics advise rising before dawn even if you do nothing (¶34).
- Nimatullahi Sufis in Iran (some are relatives of the lecturer): the only Shia tariqa, with sub-branches; "very scrupulous about Shariah" is stated but then contradicted: they do not wear hijab and usually do not pray; "Sunni tariqas are better in this regard than Shiah tariqas" (¶34–35). Their practice: wake two hours before Fajr, visit, eat, drink, because that is when "rizq is distributed" and "knowledge is distributed" (¶35–36). The lecturer doubts they benefit (¶36).
- Condemned in the Quran: sāmir, staying up at night talking nonsense or watching TV (verse from Surah al-Mu'minun) (¶36). Most important in tahajjud: salat al-layl, then Quran recitation, then dua and istighfar (¶36–37).
- Tahajjud means waking before dawn to worship; it is used for salat al-layl because the Prophet did only prayer after waking; verse of Isra 79 (¶39).
- Surah al-Zumar comparison: one obedient in the night hours, prostrating, fearing the hereafter, versus those who forget God; "Are those who know equal to those who do not know?"; night risers "know something that others don't" and are in "a different type of station" (¶39–40).
- Conclusion: night vigil shows Islam is mystical, not "dry, very legalistic"; the Quran's injunctions (set times of azkar) resemble mystic practice; mysticism has a bad reputation because of those who call themselves Sufi, but its essence, "serious attention to God, and real knowledge, and love for God", underlies the Quran (¶40).

### 2.9 Seclusion (uzla / khalwa) (¶41–54)
- Sufi attitudes differ: Naqshbandis do not advise seclusion; Suhrawardis advise a 40-day seclusion at the beginning (¶41). Shia mysticism is bound to the hadith of the Imams so this is not done, except among Nimatullahis, "not very favorable in the Shiah world" and diminishing in number (¶41–42) (the transcript here is garbled).
- Shia seclusion is "seclusion of the heart rather than seclusion of the body"; some physical seclusion may be needed first, and can come gradually (¶42). Example: itikaf, in the mosque with others, practised by the Prophet in the last ten days of Ramadan (¶42–43). Today it is uncommon, with dispute whether it must be in the four great mosques or any jami mosque; in Tehran one cannot tell which is the jami mosque (¶43–44).
- Tahajjud as daily seclusion: you are "completely alone" for half an hour to an hour and a half every night; its effect "might be more than a 40 days of seclusion" followed by forgetting it (¶44).
- Allama Tabatabai, called "a symbol of Shiah mysticism" in the scholars' (not tariqa) mode, said 40 days of seclusion and extraordinary asceticism (not eating for days) can boost the soul to extraordinary feats; possible, "but it's not desirable" (¶44–45). Pressing the physical side expands the soul (¶45).
- Illustration: Sufi practice of whirling or shaking the head in circles while repeating dhikr produces dizziness, images and "intoxication" not from substances but from physical activity (¶46). "We are not taught to do this" in Islamic worship; Tabatabai says it is not important; what matters is lifelong practices like tahajjud, an hour's nightly seclusion (¶47).
- Seclusion should reach the heart so that one is "completely immune from whatever goes outside" (¶47).
- Quran proof from Surah al-Qasas on Christian converts, rebuked by their community, who turn away from idle talk: "we have our deeds and you have yours, peace be to you, we do not desire the ignorant" (¶48). This is isolation from what you hear, with inner certainty: "as if you're living in a cave" in a big industrial city. Reverse: one in a cave whose heart is elsewhere (¶49).
- Verse from Surah al-Ma'ida (5:105 per the transcript): guard your own souls; those who go astray cannot harm you if you are guided (¶50). "If you have found guidance inside then no one else can misguide you from outside" (¶50).
- Surah Muzzammil: "remember the name of your Lord and devote yourself to Him with exclusive devotion" (tabattul) (¶50). Objection: the Prophet was highly social (many wives, many battles) so tabattul cannot mean cutting off from people; an audience remark (inaudible) suggests the verse came early; after it was revealed he did not cut himself off; what he did was isolation of the heart "directed completely towards Allah" (¶50–51).
- This can be experienced at home; difficulty "from every wall"; take no notice, be happy inside with connection to Allah (¶52). You have a complete internal personality, working and striving without being affected by what others do; "you sell honey everywhere and you don't care" what others are doing (¶53).
- Verse: be patient and "leave them with noble dignity" (hajr jamil); wahjurhum is spiritual distance (¶53).
- Paradox: "in mysticism always take good paradox": isolated and not isolated, social and not social, you see God and not see God, everything is manifestation of God but is not God (¶53–54).
- Fatima al-Zahra and Maryam are called "batul", from tabattul, cut off from everyone, devoted to God (¶54).

### 2.10 Continuous remembrance (dhikr kathir) (¶54–65)
- Verse (Q 33:41-42): remember God abundantly, glorify Him morning and evening (¶54).
- Hadith: the tasbih of Fatima al-Zahra after each prayer (Allahu Akbar 34 times in the transcript's wording, with 33 stated earlier in the same paragraph, Subhan Allah, Alhamdulillah) is the minimum of dhikr kathir, if said with attention; "there is no limit for zikr" (¶55).
- The following verse: God and angels send blessings on the Prophet (¶56). Then the believers also receive such blessing from God and angels, "to take you out of darkness into light" (Q 33:43), but only "after zikrun kaseer": by repeating God's names "you bring inside yourself that very holy atmosphere and ambience of vicinity of Allah" (¶57). All religious traditions have set dhikr at set times (¶57).
- Mystics usually say the sheikh must induce or teach the dhikr; in Shia tradition azkar all come from hadith, and the teacher only selects which suits the novice at each time; "nothing that someone should improvise" (¶58–59).
- Imam Jafar al-Sadiq: only prayer and fasting have qada; if one misses a dhikr one says it later, before sunrise or sunset (¶58–59). Lecturer cites a dhikr of "la ilaha illa Allah wahdahu la sharika lahu..." (partly elided), found in Mafatih; Imam al-Sadiq called it compulsory, meaning for those who want more certainty and not only the minimum (¶60).
- Verse 2:152 "Remember Me and I will remember you": audience asked its meaning (¶60–61). Lecturer: mutual mention; "You have my memory so to speak in your heart, I have your memory with myself" (a "human type of concept") (¶62). God's favour is everywhere always; "the only thing is that we have to go and get" it, through continuous dhikr (¶62–63).
- Hadith qudsi through Imam al-Sadiq: remember me within yourself, I remember you within Myself; in solitude; in a gathering, I remember you in a better gathering (¶63). God mentions you to angels and forces to pour down bounties: "if you remember God, everything is somehow working for you" (¶63).
- Prophets controlled things this way: Sulayman, Dawud with mountains glorifying with him (¶64). Verse "mention Maryam in the Book" (and Ibrahim); they rejoiced that God mentioned them; if He mentions them in the Book, He mentions them in lofty places (¶64–65).
- Final verse: hearts find tranquillity in the remembrance of God (Q 13:28) (¶65).

### 2.11 Stations, and Sufi tariqas begin (¶65–73)
- Suluk has many aspects (¶65). The orafat (irfan people) speak of movement station to station, state to state; analysis reduces to five (samt, ju', uzlat, sahr, dhikr) (¶66). Sufis counted seven stations, or twelve; Abdullah al-Ansari (Khwaja Abdullah Ansari) elaborated 100 stations to the final station, "al-farq ba'd al-jam" (as transcribed "alfarq waadal jaam"), separation after union, discussed earlier (¶66). Stations will not be covered, for students to study (¶67).
- Next topic: tariqas. Importance: Sufism influenced Islamic theology and culture across the world: West, North, East Africa, India, Iran, Turkey, Egypt; "more powerful than the jurisprudential schools like Hanbali, Hanafis"; rulers influenced by tariqa leaders (¶67). Goals: how tariqas developed, their present status, and influence on history (¶68).
- Terminology: tariqa literally "path"; also rendered "brotherhood", "orders", "fraternities" by analogy with Christian monasteries (¶68). Converts come to Islam seeking spiritual experience which juristic rules alone do not give, and may have a high view of Christian orders; they choose Sufism as representative of Islam; but none of these English terms means "path" (¶69). The lecturer will use "tariqa" untranslated (¶70).
- Quran usage: tariqa is used mostly of non-humans (the lecturer is unsure why); for humans, sabil and sirat; examples: Surah al-Ahqaf (jinn heard a book confirming earlier ones, guiding to "tariqin mustaqim") and Surah al-Jinn (if they stay straight on the tariqa, We would give them abundant water) (¶70). It is interesting that tariqa appears when jinn are concerned; hell is made for Shaytan and jinn, so the tariqa leading to it is Shaytan's, perhaps coincidence (¶71). Our path is sirat; Sufi path tariqa; also the shariah/tariqa pairing; "our serat should be their serat"; what tariqa is is left for next time (¶72–73).
