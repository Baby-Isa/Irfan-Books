# Lecture 6: Sharia, Tariqa, Haqiqa, and the Marks of a True ʿArif

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan06.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture opens the practical turn of the series: the lecturer says that what the ʿurafaʾ practise is not yet what the class will practise, and that beginners must first "find willingness" to become an ʿarif (¶2). He gives a definition of the ʿarif attributed to Amir al-Muminin (¶3), then builds the central triad of sharia, tariqa and haqiqa. The triad appears as law, path and truth; as shell, core and kernel; as words, deeds and "sense" of the Prophet; as ʿilm, ʿayn and haqq al-yaqin; and as Islam, Iman and Ihsan (¶7–15, 23–25, 38, 43–48). The thesis is that irfan starts from, and never replaces, the sharia. Claimed powers (karamat) without sharia are magic, and real knowledge of God comes through the heart, purified by good morals, which are themselves reached through sharia (¶17–22). The lecturer also discusses the difficulty of attention in prayer (¶26–33), the endless nature of knowledge of God and of the afterlife (¶50–58), the levels of paradise (¶57–60), and the warnings against pop spirituality and ascetic feats without sharia (¶65–70). It fits the series as the "map" lecture before the practices come later (¶2, 72).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Willingness, and the definition of the ʿarif (¶2–6)
- Today's material is "not what we're going to practice"; it is what the ʿurafaʾ practise and what they find. For beginners "the most important thing is to find willingness" to become an ʿarif (¶2).
- Most of the class "wouldn't have willingness to go through all the hardship", so there are steps to prepare the willingness. The experience that follows is "very pleasurable" (¶2).
- Definition attributed to Amir al-Muminin (as), "one of the most beautiful" descriptions of a mystic: the knower knows his soul, frees it, purifies it, and removes whatever keeps it at bay (¶3).
- Three moves in the definition: (1) know the self; (2) free it, since the self is "settled by some sort of chain" binding it to earth and body; (3) purify it so that it can "move up", away from whatever keeps it at a distance from Allah (¶3, 6).
- Why we are at a distance: the lecturer's own image of the "police force" of Allah. When reciting the verses on Jahannam and "shadid al-ʿiqab", he imagines that a society needs a police force, and so does all creation, to keep order and punish violators. Without this justice "the whole creation of Allah would be destroyed" (¶4).
- That "utmost justice keeps us at distance" unless we prepare. Analogy: the house of a head of state, where police do not let you in without permission, qualification or an appointment. With Allah no appointment or authority is needed, "only purification" (¶5).
- Summary: knowing the self, freeing it from desire, purifying it, moving towards Allah and "entering the holy precinct" (¶6).

### 2.2 Sharia, tariqa, haqiqa defined (¶7–11)
- Mystics have their own terminology, "often taken from some hadith from the Prophet". Every field of knowledge has its terms (¶7).
- Sharia: "the very outward shell of religion", the law. It means confirming the Prophet's injunctions and practising them: how and when to pray and fast, and the prerequisites of each (¶7–8).
- We do not do these things for their own sake. We want to "go somewhere", namely "the vicinity of Allah". So there must be a path from sharia to that destination, and it is called tariqa, "literally path" (¶8).
- The Sufi tariqas you have heard of are "only the systematization of this path". Tariqa is "realizing the manners and attitudes of the Prophet in one's own self" (¶9).
- Example: the Prophet prays beside us and a jury watches; we imitate him exactly. The jurists would say both prayers are correct. Jurists can judge only the correctness of form, and that is "the boundaries of the jurists' sharia" (¶9–10). The Prophet's salat was nonetheless "different from our salaat" in meaning. The jurist cannot know what goes on in the heart of the Prophet or in ours, and that inward matter is tariqa (¶9–10).
- Tariqa should lead to haqiqa, "the truth", becoming like the Prophet and "seeing what they see" (¶10).
- The orafa he will discuss are "acceptable ones, especially according to Shia tradition", though there are many Sunni orafa (¶10).
- Claim: Sunni Sufism was not in the Shia world because "the Shia was in itself a type of mystical understanding of Islam". He cites Henry Corbin for the view that the Sunni Sufis are "the Shia who do not like to be called Shia", with improvised paths that bring them "almost near" to the Shia view of sharia and haqiqa (¶11).
- What we want from religion is the sharia and also its meaning, which means doing it "with a manner and attitude which the Prophet" had, so as to arrive at haqiqa. The Prophet saw the haqiqa "by practically doing all these things" (¶11).

### 2.3 Irfan begins with sharia; the ship, sea and pearl (¶12–17)
- "The beginning of Irfan is Sharia." Some traditions say to train the heart and ignore the "scruples of Sharia", because the training may differ from sharia rulings. The Shia mystics and most Sunni tariqas start from sharia and reach haqiqa through it (¶12).
- The parable (given first with the quotation unfinished, ¶13): sharia is the ship, tariqa the sea, haqiqa the pearl. You cannot find the pearl except by sailing the sea, and you cannot sail without a ship (¶13, 15, 17).
- Attributed in the lecture to Shaikh Najm al-Din Kubra, "one of the students of Suhrawardi", founder of the Kubrawiya; its silsila goes to Ali b. Abi Talib (¶15, 17).
- A hadith "which would explain whatever I said in a very concise way": "Sharia is my word", "tariqa is my deeds", "haqiqa is my sense / what I see" (¶13–14). Sharia is the Prophet's description of what to do, and complying with it is salat, fasting and the rest (¶14–15).
- Tariqa: the inward way of doing the acts, while the outward framework remains sharia. Haqiqa is "the reality, the truth which is seen after moving through that path" (¶15).
- Link to prayer: the Prophet said "salat is the miʿraj of the momin", a ladder to the vicinity of Allah, "not every momin but a momin like himself". So the way he prayed took him to haqiqa (¶14).
- Shia versus Sunni: "we don't have Shia tariqa except one tariqa which we will deal with later", because Shiism has the Imams leading. Sunnis lack this; they have shura and rely on the tariqa and the Shaikh (¶15–16, 37).
- Sunni Sufi lineages: all go back to Ali b. Abi Talib "except Naqshbandis, who claim Abu Bakr" and are "staunch Sunnis who see Shia as evil", "an exception". Many go through disciples of Imam al-Ridha (as), "like Maʿruf" (¶16). They are "very, very close" to the Shia meaning of Imamate and wilayat, and wilayat in their terminology "has a very important point" (¶16).

### 2.4 Karamat versus magic (¶17–20)
- Kubra, as reported: if you see someone flying in the air, walking on the sea or eating fire, but he has abandoned even one injunction of God or one sunnah of the Prophet, "you should know that they are liars" (¶17–18).
- Karama for a sufi is "like a miracle for a Prophet". A miracle is done only by Allah's permission and is for the Prophet. Karama comes from those close to Allah or those who have found "spiritual power", and the "human soul is very powerful". Magic is done "by the help of Jinn"; karama is done by the help of the human soul (¶17–18).
- "Liars" does not mean they cannot do it. It means they are not true knowers of Allah and do it "for personal desires" (¶18–19).
- Can someone of high degree act on desire? Yes: "even if you're a Prophet it is possible". At every level shaytan is there, and no station gives immunity. The masumeen are masumeen "as long as they protect themselves"; "Taqwa is the protection from shaytan" (¶19) [QUERY: this claim about prophets and the masumeen is the lecturer's own; a reader may want to raise how it fits Shia ʿisma].
- The lecturer concludes that the performer is doing "magic", usually via jinn "but there are other methods as well". He is tentative on whether a true karama-worker could neglect sharia ("probably it is possible") (¶20).
- Muslim mystics stress sharia and are distinguished by stressing tariqa and haqiqa as well (¶20).

### 2.5 Contemporary and Shia voices: Bahari, Hamadani (¶21–25)
- Ayatullah Muhammad Bahari (Iranian mystic, "recently passed away"), as reported: jurisprudence is an introduction to purification of morals; good morals are an introduction to knowledge of tawhid (¶21).
- Consequences: no good morals without sharia realized in oneself, and no knowledge of God without good morals. You cannot learn about God in a university; you can learn theology (e.g., Muslim versus Christian) but "not the reality of God", which comes only through self-purification, "not through mind" but through the heart (¶21–22).
- Real tawhid knowledge, "not the scholastic knowledge", needs good morals, which need complete sharia. A Sufi who says "now I am an ʿarif, sharia is for ordinary people" has "something wrong with this argument" (¶22).
- Mulla ʿAbd al-Samad Hamadani: "a huge mystic" with a hadith from the Ahl al-Bayt ready for every point of the mystical path. He said sharia, haqiqa and tariqa are "different names for the same reality seen from different perspectives", differing "in depth and level": sharia the shell, tariqa the core, haqiqa the kernel (¶23).
- Example from salat: khidma (serving God) is the shell, qurba (coming close) the core, and wusul (arriving) the kernel. A salat like the Prophet's does all three, so a real salat "would actually make you arrive" (¶24–25).
- Shiism does not use all these names; it says only "ʿarif", the knower of Allah, who seeks the deeper meaning and the "pearl". He ties this to the opening verses of the series: "signs for those who understand, ponder, see, hear" (¶25).

### 2.6 Attention, and the practical problem (¶26–37)
- Diagram: sharia the shell, haqiqa the kernel, and tariqa as the way to purify one's practice of the sharia "more and more" and "give it more depth" (¶26).
- Thus you need thoughtfulness and constant remembrance. When you say Allahu Akbar, "you have to know what you are saying", and likewise with la ilaha illallah, subhanallah, and the steps of ruku and sujood (¶26).
- Attention is "very elusive": you can guard mouth, eyes and ears, but not attention, "as soon as you want to divert your attention, it goes other places" (¶27).
- Other traditions: a Sikh lecturer at the college described formulas similar to the Sufis' for containing attention. One tariqa has you recite in rhythm with breathing, imagining the physical heart, for two or three hours, so that it is internalized (¶28).
- Different tariqas are different "improvised methods" to bring thoughts under control: some prescribe seclusion in a zawiya; Naqshbandis say go to the people; "Hawaaris" (see §7) say solitude (¶28–29).
- The vicious circle: you cannot say la ilaha illallah from the heart without practice, and the "knowledge comes after you have internalized it". Yet to get it you must contain your attention. Example: "Suddenly, you think about did I lock my car?" (¶29–30).
- Audience objection (second person): on a cliff edge you would not think of the car, because the reality is in front of you (¶31). Lecturer: "You're talking logically... but can you do it yourself?" It is "a vicious circle", "the whole difficulty of this tariqa", and "not everyone can" while "sharia could be done by everyone" (¶32).
- Example: we do 100 rakʿat in congregation on Laylat al-Qadr or recite the Quran, and the lecturer says we know we did a physical act, but the Quran recited is "just words going in my head. What did I do? I don't know" (¶32–33).
- We are "fond of Sharia... rather than fond of tariqa", wanting the apparent side. When you go for depth you find difficulties; he cites Hafiz's first poem in his diwan: love seems easy at first, then "a lot of difficulties come" (¶33).
- Audience asks how one changes; a reply suggests reading more Quran (¶34–35). The lecturer says that is "not usually what mystics say": they say to remember and mention God's name, so that attention is gradually diverted from others, hence their long hours of dhikr (¶36).
- Sharia already includes this: besides the obligatory and nafila prayers there are many other supplications and acts "to make us think more about God and less about other things" (¶36).
- A teacher is needed. Shia have the Imam; Sunni mysticism relies on the Shaikh and his silsila. He cites Musa searching for someone (the Quran story, ¶37) as proof that time, effort and energy are required (¶37).

### 2.7 Minimum versus more (¶37–42)
- Guarantee: for Shia who perform their duties at the minimum, "you go to Jannah guaranteed... by Imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq" (¶37–38).
- Analogy to work and money: "if you want more money you have to work more", so more spiritual work means investing more time (¶38).
- Another description: sharia is to worship, tariqa is to go to his presence and feel it, haqiqa is to witness. Feeling presence is not the same as witnessing; after time in his presence "you have actually changed yourself" and then can gradually witness (¶38–39).
- Witnessing (in answer to a question) means seeing God, his attributes and names, and finally seeing his actions; "his actions become the witness". It is the highest station (¶40–41).
- "We are all at his presence, but he is not at our presence." God is closer than the jugular vein, but "we are not closer to him"; we must go into the depth of sharia to feel that he is here (¶41–42).
- "Worship God as if you're feeling his presence" is attained through tariqa. Muraqaba is trying to see yourself in his presence, as if God is watching you all the time; it differs from feeling his presence and precedes it (¶42).

### 2.8 The three degrees of certainty; Islam, Iman, Ihsan (¶43–49)
- Sharia, tariqa and haqiqa correspond to ʿilm al-yaqin, ʿayn al-yaqin and haqq al-yaqin (¶43).
- Paris analogy: you are certain there is a city called Paris, just across the Channel, though you are not there. This is the certainty sharia can give: "you are certain that he is there but you cannot see him". Likewise you know people are doing Umra in Makka now. ʿAyn al-yaqin is like seeing the houses from the car on arrival, and haqq al-yaqin is "when you really watch what's going on in Paris" (¶43–44).
- Another triad: Islam, Iman, Ihsan, said to be in "Ashʿari theology" as three levels of faith; the mystics use "Iqan" for the third (¶45).
- Islam is submission and doing the sharia. Iman is feeling the meaning. Evidence from the Quran (49:14): the Bedouins said "we believe"; they are told to say "we have submitted", since "faith has not yet entered your hearts". They submitted to the Prophet's power (¶45–46).
- Ihsan: "when you do good... when you respect the presence of God". The Quran defines it in Surat al-Dhariyat (51:15–19): the muttaqin in gardens and springs, who were "muhsinin" before; they slept little at night, sought forgiveness before dawn, and in their wealth was a right for the beggar and the deprived (¶46–47).
- "To be a momin it's easy; to be a mohsin" is the harder thing, so "the path has become difficult" (¶46–47). The Quran's rewards for the momineen differ from those for the muhsineen; "the reward of ihsan is that knowledge" (¶47).
- Musa as a young man in Pharaoh's house, Quran 12:22 (as cited): "We gave him wisdom and knowledge; thus we reward the muhsinin". The lecturer's gloss: if you are muhsin, "your knowledge is guaranteed". The same formula for Ibrahim, Yunus, Musa, Harun and Nuh in Surat al-Saffat (¶47–48).
- Ihsan is "above Iman". A further triad: zahir, batin, batin al-batin. Sharia has a hidden side, and its hidden side is haqiqa. Parallel: al-ʿamm, al-khass, khass al-khass, "the elite of the elite" (¶48–49).
- "Personal knowledge and personal experience of God" is what he means by knowing God, and "mystical experiences are always exclusive". The course aims only to "give some encouragement" to the willing (¶49).

### 2.9 Two stages of knowledge, and humility before God (¶50–54)
- Amir al-Muminin (as), as reported (partly inaudible, ¶50): knowledge has two steps. The first brings arrogance and pretension: "superficial knowledge", "I know, other people don't know", with people "like animals" (Quran 7:179 as cited). Such knowledge is "bound to be accompanied by arrogance", and the beginner wants many karamat and shows off (¶50–51).
- The second step brings humility: one's position before God is "like something which could be crushed", with awe in the heart, and the person feels "indigent" (Quran 35:15, "you people are the poor [fuqaraʾ] towards Allah") (¶51–52).
- The tariqa path ends in the knowledge "that you know nothing" about Allah, which is "a bit disappointing". The angels' words in 2:32 are cited: "no knowledge but what you taught us" (¶52).
- Abu Bakr (via Ibn ʿArabi): "confessing one's ignorance is knowledge". Ibn ʿArabi adds that there was one companion with no ignorance, Ali, whose position is higher. The lecturer reads "no ignorance" as within human capacity, with things beyond that no one can know. Otherwise God "would have been limited" (¶53–54).

### 2.10 Paradise, knowledge and the levels of the saved (¶54–60)
- Entering Jannah is "just the beginning" of knowing Allah; one then moves ever closer and "knows more and more", and this knowledge is "very pleasurable" (¶54).
- In Jannah "every morning and every evening you have a window open to you about a new knowledge... a new unveiling of Allah in a new form". It "is not a place where you eat and merry" (¶55).
- Why is this less conspicuously described in the Quran than gardens and rivers? Because most people are ahl al-sharia who only understand "business, the reward": "if you do this, you get this" (¶55–56). A glimpse of God would mean little to them: "give me a houri or a garden" (¶56).
- Taqwa has depths; the outer framework of taqwa is the people of sharia, who are "muttaqin". The Quran is "huda lil-muttaqin" (2:2, as cited), so you need taqwa first and ride on it to go deeper (¶57).
- Few Muslims recite the Quran knowing its meaning, yet they get their reward and the places of God as well, "although they don't want it" (¶57).
- The Jannah of the muqarrabun (Waqiʿa, Rahman) differs from the Jannah of the mominin: "physically the same thing, but spiritually... totally different". Those in the lower "never ever can realize" what is given to the higher (¶58).
- Answering a question: ashab al-yamin are the people of sharia, who may go some way into tariqa and still be ashab al-yamin. Moving to the muqarrabun, "at the lowest level", needs "a complete change of attitudes... outlook... desires" (¶59).
- Quran 16:97 (as cited): whoever does righteous deeds, male or female, while a momin, "we give them a pure life", meaning not mixed with "lowly desires of this world"; this is "only the Grace of Allah", though our effort prepares for it (¶59–60).

### 2.11 Q&A on the end of knowledge (¶61–64)
- See §6 for the questions. Answer: there is no end; "you just find out more things you don't know about God". In Jannah you never get bored (18:108 as cited: "they never wished to go somewhere else") (¶62). To increase knowledge, God told the Prophet to say "rabbi zidni ʿilma" (20:114) (¶64).

### 2.12 Levels, pop-spirituality and ascetic feats (¶64–72)
- An ʿarif may be in the "first class of primary school", or a professor. Judging them is hard for an outsider, like going to India or Africa and hearing languages you cannot tell apart (¶64–65).
- Nowadays irfan has "become public property": people say they do meditation, do yoga each morning, sit together men and women, and think Allah is "coming down to our mind". If it were so easy, "everyone would have been an ʿarif" (¶65–66).
- Allama Tabatabaʿi (as quoted): anyone, of any theology, who does riyadha such as 40 days of hunger, eating less and less and then nothing for three days, will "find something". Because maʿrifa is so unknown to us, "whatever you find, you see you have found God" (¶66–67).
- The ʿAziz story (¶67; see §5). Irfan "has a beginning, a middle, an end, different stations and states", and "most of the mystics that we meet unfortunately, they are at just the beginning" (¶68).
- Tabatabaʿi again: sit in a well for 40 days with little food and you come out able to see jinn, a real power, but people use it to do sahar against others. Such riyadha is "very easy" and not for arriving at Allah; "continuously practicing Sharia is difficult" (¶68–69).
- Sharia means every day and night, every dawn, the adhkar, watching backbiting and vile listening. "You can do this for 40 days. But the whole life is very difficult." The real ʿarif "continuously acts on the Sharia" (¶69–70).
- First advice, said to be from the Prophet: every night before sleep recite the Musabbihat (Saff, Hadid, Hashr, Jumuʿa, Taghabun, plus "Araaf" as heard) for one year. Then the ʿarif gives a second advice (¶70).
- It is a "very slow process" because the disease of the heart is hard to cure; unlike flu, no three-day course (¶70–71). Do not mention it to anyone (¶71).
- Rabiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya ("the lady mystics (al Adwiyyah)", ¶71): "first rule of mysticism: cover your good doing as you cover your bad" (¶72).
- Scales (maqamat) are left for next session; there will be a gap of a week and an extra session (¶72).
