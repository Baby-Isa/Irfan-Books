# Lecture 07: Practical Irfan I: Tajliyah, Takhliyah, Tahliyah and Fana

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan07.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
This lecture opens the "practical Irfan" half of the series (the first ~20 slides were theoretical; ¶2–3). The whole practical effort is the removal of the veils covering the heart so that the soul can receive Allah's favours (¶4). The lecturer takes Isaac of Nineveh's threefold scheme (repentance for the body, purification for the lower soul, perfection for the higher soul) as a "crude" preliminary map (¶6–11), then presents the Islamic fourfold first journey: tajliyah (furbishing through strict Sharia), takhliyah (emptying the heart), tahliyah (adornment by Allah) and fana (annihilation) (¶11–12). The longest section defends a specific reading of fana: it is the loss of self-perception before God's glory and the replacement of imperfect attributes by divine ones. It is not the loss of individuality, not Buddhist cessation, and never a fusion of divine and human essence (¶41–63). Fana ends only the first journey; a second journey follows, to be treated later (¶11, ¶64).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Framing: practical Irfan, veils and the tariqas (¶2–5)
- Slides: practical Irfan is the second part, from about slide 12 / page four; the first ~20 pages are theoretical (¶2–3).
- Question posed: how does an arif start building the soul so it is ready to receive Allah's favours and blessings? (¶4)
- The "more practical side" is sulook, "moving towards Allah"; sulook will be treated later (¶4).
- The whole effort is "to remove the veils which are covering the hearts". It is not easy, so different mystics improvised different methods, and this is "how the tariqas have appeared", the Sufi orders. The basic principle is the same: remove the veil that stops the heart from seeing Allah's glory and feeling His presence (¶4).
- Five major concepts prevail in all mystical traditions (Islamic or not). What qualifies them makes one a Muslim, Christian or Buddhist mystic, but the main elements are similar (¶5). Only two of the five are named in this lecture: continuous dhikr and abandoning everything for His sake (¶28). The rest are not listed here.

### 2.2 Isaac of Nineveh's threefold scheme (¶5–10)
- The lecturer quotes a Christian mystic, Isaac of Nineveh (seventh century), of the Nestorian church, "the Church of the eastern part of Christianity", now tiny or perhaps non-existent, with some members in Iran. Armenians are "a bit close" to it (¶5–6).
- Why Isaac: he is "very close to our mystics", and the Nestorians did not hold that Jesus was Son of God, so were "closer to our ideas" (¶9).
- Human being = body + lower soul (nafs) + higher soul (ruhh). The soul has two sides: one always attending to bodily affairs (lower), one facing upwards towards Allah and longing for spirituality (higher). Islamic names such as nafs ammarah and nafs mutmainnah are "different aspects of the same thing" (¶6–7).
- To remove the veils all three must be purified, because "we want to go towards the purest of the pure"; "dirt and filth cannot enter into that threshold" (¶7).
- Body: purified by repentance, which covers sin and also "whatever we usually casually do in our life which is offensive" to Allah. Older mystics put repentance first (¶8).
- Lower soul: purified by purification (¶8, ¶9).
- Higher soul (ruhh): is itself pure and cannot be polluted; when we are polluted "the ruhh doesn't exist anymore, it is taken away". It needs "perception", growth and development, so it is perfected (¶8–9).
- Isaac's definitions as relayed: repentance is righteous works done with the body (fasting, rituals, alms) and covers making up what was omitted. Purification is virtues of the lower soul (neighbourly love, humility towards peers, what is in the heart rather than the bodily appearance). Perfection is gifts bestowed by God (light, exaltation, love) that "you do not do anything for" (¶9–10).
- Link back: love of God cannot be created by us; after purification it is "placed there by Allah", so perfection is "the acts of God" (¶10).
- Lecturer's verdict: "very crude and large preliminary" study; Islamic mysticism has developed and expanded it, and its practices are "more consistent than other mystics" (¶11).

### 2.3 The Islamic fourfold first journey (¶11–12)
- Four steps or stations end the first journey, which ends in fana (the transcript has a gap here: "Sana……(11:15)"). When the soul is annihilated, the first journey is finished and a second, "much more wonderful and amusing" journey begins, to be discussed later (¶11).
- Mapping to Isaac: tajliyah (furbishing, polishing, making shiny; body and lower soul) corresponds to repentance. Takhliyah (abandoning, "also called tarkhiyyah", to purify) corresponds to purification. Tahliyah (adorning) corresponds to perfection and affects the ruhh. Annihilation was not mentioned by Isaac; it is "very, very important" and "probably" a characteristic of Islamic mysticism. It can be compared with concepts elsewhere but "certainly it's not the same" (¶12).

### 2.4 Tajliyah: strict adherence to Sharia (¶12–21)
- Tajliyah means furbishing body and soul with whatever the Prophet ordered: wajibat and mustahabbat, and avoidance of haram and makruh. The first step is sticking to Quran and Sunnah "in the strictest sense of the verse" (¶12).
- Seriousness of approach: the Quran urges taking the Book "with strength", i.e. seriously and with one's best effort. Cited for Bani Israil (Q 2:93) and for Yahya (Q 19:12) (¶13).
- Shia mystics say that "unless you have furbished your soul and your body with whatever sharia said you cannot move to the next step" (¶13).
- On the teacher problem: beginners say "who is going to guide us?" and give up if they find no teacher. The lecturer replies that such a teacher cannot be found until one has moved beyond the preliminaries. "If you want a teacher or a mentor, instructing the beginning of the way is not the one you need" (¶14). First comply completely with Sharia (¶15).
- Extras: the Sharia and hadith contain recommended acts that ordinary people are not ordered to do but the traveller must do. A teacher is needed only to say which hadith to prioritise (¶15).
- Example of such a practice: the Prophet recited al-Musabbihat every night before sleeping. These are the five surahs beginning with "sabbaha": Hadid (57), Hashr (59), Saff (61), Jumu'a, Taghabun. Some add Surah al-A'la (the transcript says 86), which the Prophet also recited. The hadith is "very authentic" and in al-Kafi. Most people do not act on it, since six surahs nightly is hard (¶15–16).
- Teacher example: Allama Tabataba'i's teacher Sayyid Ali Qadi Tabrizi told beginners to recite these surahs before sleep for one year before giving the second instruction. This shows "a very gradual path" (¶16–17).
- "There is nothing which goes outside Sharia." Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (as) is quoted as calling a certain dhikr, said morning and evening (before sunrise and sunset), obligatory on every believer. The lecturer explains this is obligatory only for those setting out on the deeper, irfani understanding, not in fiqh; otherwise it would appear in the Tawdih al-Masa'il (¶17).
- The dhikr text is given (a tawhid formula with "yuhyi wa yumit"); see section 4 (¶18).
- Not all mustahabbat can be done; one must choose a regular dhikr. Even the Imams and the Prophet, "very highly station people", kept regular dhikr (¶18).
- Hadith of Unwan al-Basri: the old man came to Imam Sadiq late in life; the Imam first rejected him, then accepted him after insistence and prayer. The Imam told him he was busy, with azkar for every hour of the day, and that he could go learn from Malik ibn Anas (¶18–19).
- Even the Prophet was told to repeat what he never forgot. Q 87:6 says he will not forget (¶20). The lecturer notes "a very funny hadees in bukhari": a companion recited a verse and the Prophet said "may God have mercy upon you" because he had forgotten that piece (¶20). Yet the Prophet was told to recite Surah Muzzammil (Q 73:1–4 is paraphrased in ¶21), and Q 16:44 calls the Quran "zikr" (¶20–21).
- Conclusion: no mystic, even at the highest station, can say "I'm now done with these things". Tajliyah "should accompany a mystic until the end", and dropping it "pollutes" the whole achievement (¶21).

### 2.5 Takhliyah: emptying the heart (¶22–35)
- Once practice is in place, takhliyah follows: "removing what is in the heart in terms of occupation other than Allah". Practice is easy; the heart is hard (¶22).
- Up to tajliyah, "ordinary people can start doing" it; the travelling in the "deeper understandings called Irfan" begins here (¶22).
- Ibn Sina handout (previous session): the beginning of Irfan is "tafreequn wa nafsun tarqun": separation, dusting off, abandonment, rejection. These are four levels of takhliyah (¶22–23, ¶29).
- Separation: separating the knower from whatever diverts from God. Many loves do not contradict love of God (some are in line with it), so the test is "whatever would occupy the heart, which wouldn't let you completely dedicate yourself to God" (¶23).
- Heart vs. body: you can easily abstain from wine or from meeting people, but not from thinking evil of others or from being occupied in prayer. "When we pray you see how difficult it is to remove all other thoughts except the thoughts about Allah" (¶23–24).
- Dusting off: analogy of removing a cancerous organ where "the very loose of it remain" and may regrow; hence traces, even the desire for diversions, must be dusted off (¶24–25).
- Value of dhikr: reward-talk (sawab, houses in jannah) is fine "inshallah", but "for a mystic the effect of zikr is very practical, is real is at hand". Saying Subhan Allah, al-hamdu lillah, la ilaha illa Allah, Allahu akbar ten times morning and evening helps dust off remaining worldly inclinations (¶25).
- Sufi saying, heard by the lecturer "from a sikh sufi about two weeks ago": after dhikr for a while "you become zikr". He explains it as becoming better, so immersed that you grasp the greatness and are diverted from material desires (¶26; the transcript has an inaudible gap).
- Soul-as-vessel analogy: "mention pure things you become valuable, mention vile things you become vile"; the soul takes the shape of whatever it is poured into, as stated at the series' beginning (¶26–27).
- Q 33:41 (much dhikr): the effect is real, not mere gratitude. The lecturer imagines the question "why remember God all the time?" and answers that "it has a real effect" (¶27–28).
- Continuous dhikr is one of the five common concepts, as is abandoning everything for His sake (¶28).
- Rejection of the self is "the last thing which you abandon", because we worry about ourselves and think we have independence from Allah (¶28).
- Swimming analogy: a non-swimmer thrown into the sea is distressed, but once able to float finds it pleasurable. So leaning the soul on Allah feels like drowning at first, then "this is where you have to be" (¶28–29).
- Abandoning is leaving "with some sort of reluctance"; rejection is outright "you don't like it anymore", rejecting what contradicts love and remembrance of Allah (¶29).
- Q&A on "rejection" (¶30–35). See section 6. In answer: the self (origin of selfishness) is rejected; the soul (origin of fana) is not. Bounties are not rejected. Misusing them is what is rejected: eating for pleasure to the point that one cannot pray or think "is rejection of the name of Allah" and "the misuse of the nemat" (¶31–34).
- Charcoal and diamond analogy: same substance, but in the diamond all the molecules are directed in order. Practice directs everything towards Allah (¶34).
- Ameer al-Mu'minin is cited: believer and unbeliever use the same bounties in the same amount, but the believer directs them all to Allah and the unbeliever uses them in disorder (¶34). See section 4. The lecturer concludes that bounties are used "not for lowly base desires, but for more splendid desires" (¶35).

### 2.6 Tahliyah: adornment by Allah (¶36–41)
- After tajliyah and takhliyah, the soul "starts to take adornment from the spiritual world". "Allah would start to feed you with things which of course others cannot take". This covers visions, ilhamat (inspirations/instructions) and deeper understanding of the Quran (¶36).
- It is Allah's act on the soul, not the Sufi's; angels (as intermediaries) bring these things to everyone, but only a purified soul can "catch" them (¶36–37).
- A mystic sees and hears what we do not, "not because of course they are chosen ones but because they have furbished their souls". The soul was blind and deaf, now sees and hears (¶37). Cited Q 2:18 (deaf, dumb, blind) (¶37–38).
- Hadith of Zayd ibn Haritha: asked how he was, he said he sees the people's places in paradise; the Prophet approved. The lecturer: Zayd was no different from others; "any single one of us here who goes through this process of purification... can see things that other people may not be able to see" (¶38).
- Caveat: "this is not a great deal"; it does not mean one has "arrived at Allah", and "there's a long way" still. These are just things "going around us" (¶39).
- Q 6:122 (the dead given life and a light to walk by): many verses concern this new life through faith. Q 8:2 (hearts tremble at mention of Allah): not all believers are like this, only "the momenoon who have purified themselves" (¶39–40).
- Q 57:28 (believe in His messenger, a double portion of mercy, a light to walk in): the lecturer reads "believe in the Prophet" as implying many believers accept him as a prophet without accepting what he says. The reward is light in which "you see things" differently (¶40–41).
- Tahliyah is the soul adorned by Allah's blessings; then comes annihilation (¶41).

### 2.7 Fana: the puzzle, and the lecturer's reading (¶41–59)
- Objection: if the end is annihilation or destruction, what is the point of all this? "You might say that one shouldn't be selfish..." and the objector assumes "annihilate completely... and disown God" (¶41–43). The lecturer says all Muslim mystics see fana as the end of this portion of the path but "what annihilation mean[s] is a point of controversy" (¶42).
- Class replies (¶44–48): the reply that fana is real nothingness is questioned; another answer is that Allah is real and all else is not, so one becomes "more real than before" (¶44–45). The lecturer responds that it is not physical destruction but annihilation of the lower soul (¶47). Another student suggests a part of the person must be annihilated, and that we may resist this gradually (¶48).
- Individuality remains: "the human soul would not annihilate the human individuality... very important". Otherwise "what's the point of going through all these things?" (¶49).
- Buddhists: they lose individuality, but the lecturer thinks they equate individuality with suffering and do not accept an individuality that could feel pleasure (¶49).
- Argument from pleasure and creation: if the individual dissolves, then who experiences pleasure, us or God? If it is God's alone, "what was the point of creation right from the beginning?" Creating people to suffer and then dissolve is "a sort of contradiction". Individuality must remain "so that it could experience", though our experience is possible only through God's will, power and life (¶49–50).
- Quranic proof: in jannah, pleasures are for the individual, who recites God's name and is attended by Him. Q 76:21 (a pure drink) is cited as "given to the individual" (¶51).
- Orientalists: Julian Baldick (Mystical Islam; "a lecturer Kings College") is cited as saying there are differing views on what passes away (the lower soul, attributes, the whole person, personality) and that "it is not clear what survives" (God alone, man with God's attributes, or man as an original idea in God's mind). The lecturer sees "confusion" in someone who has searched mystical books and not found what fana is (¶51–53).
- Fana is always coupled with baqa (survival), which is "much more real" than present existence (¶52).
- Alexander Knysh (Islamic Mysticism) is cited with two "complementary" definitions. The first: passing away of all things from the mystic's consciousness, including self-perception; the vacuum is filled by pure consciousness of God, which "secludes any perception of others" (¶53, ¶57). The lecturer calls this "a very instrumental definition". The mystic who attains it sits "in front of us"; body and soul are still there; this is "this very world" (¶54).
- Lecturer's explanation: at the peak of tahliyah, God shows them His glory "at a very low level, very superficial". They lose self-perception because one "cannot both watch that glory and have a sense of self perception" (¶55–56).
- Analogy: when thrilled, frightened or deeply pleased, one does not feel a cut hand or foot (¶55).
- Loss of "selfishness", not of the soul. "Not that you have no soul, you cannot think about it" (¶56). They write of the experience, but it is like explaining a fragrance with nothing comparable; "there is no human experience comparable to this" (¶56–57).
- Order of events is the reverse of "vacuum then fill": consciousness of God comes and "everything else would go away even the self perception" (¶57).
- Second definition (Knysh): the imperfect attributes of the fallible creature fall away and are replaced by the perfect attributes of the Divine Self. The lecturer says it is the same thing as the first, "complimentary... if you take it to extreme identical": you see God's attributes and no longer yours (¶58). Fana has its own stages (¶58).
- Summary: fana is the annihilation of the mystic's self-perception in perceiving the unveiling of God's reality (his glory) (¶59). Some veils between Allah and creation never go, "otherwise the creation would go away", even for the Prophet; it is the outer veils that go (¶59).
- Contrast with nirvana: Knysh is cited as saying the definitions present fana "not as a mere cessation of individual experience, but as the development of a more ample and perfect selfhood". The lecturer says this is "very correct": existence expands to bear Allah's glory (¶60).

### 2.8 Errors about fana: incarnation, shath and the need for a guide (¶60–65)
- Fana must never be construed as a fusion of divine and human essence; this is "just a shirk or kufr" (¶60–61).
- Claims such as "I am dissolved in God" or "God is incarnated in me" come from "people with lack of understanding". The Christian doctrine of incarnation, and Hinduism, are said to be misunderstandings of fana (¶61).
- The lecturer suggests the experience "has existed right from the beginning of human creations" and that many false religions arose from people who confused themselves with God at this station (¶61).
- Muslim mystics had such shathiyat too: "Anal haq" (I am God) or "how glorified I am" (¶62).
- The lecturer says this must be dealt with: the mystic is not called sinful or a liar, but "they lose control" and should be trained (¶62). The transcript then says "you have to kill them" (¶63), so the passage is unclear; see section 7.
- Origin of religions: this confusion is "the origin of all the different religions" in the world, especially Hinduism (¶63).
- Guide: "at the beginning of the way you don't need an instructor"; here the instructor is the Sharia as well, but mystics at this stage "cannot understand these anymore" and need an instructor (¶64).

### 2.9 Q&A on non-Muslim mystics (¶65–70)
- Different paths exist. A Christian and a Muslim mystic can both reach fana "to a certain extent" but "don't arrive at the same destination". What matters most is how they construe it afterwards. Those who went by Sharia are "much more prone to success and being helped by Allah" (¶65, ¶67).
- Fana is "a human experience... not confined to Muslims or Christians or Jews" (¶68).
- Different types of annihilation, and annihilation in the divine essence, are deferred to the next session (¶69–70).

## 6. Q&A from the audience
- (¶30–31) Q: Does "rejection" mean rejection of natural things? A: Any such warning is possible but "the better meaning" is to reject desires that come from earthly nature. The self (origin of selfishness) is distinct from the soul (origin of fana).
- (¶32–33) Q (partly inaudible): If we reject everything, what of the bounties Allah gives? A: "You like everything which comes from Allah and you hate everything which comes from yourself"; misuse of bounties is kufr, not rejecting them.
- (¶43–48) Q (lecturer asks): If annihilation is the end, what is the point? Students: real nothingness? / only Allah is real; we become more real / a part is annihilated and part resists. A: not physical destruction but of the lower soul; individuality survives (¶47, ¶49).
- (¶64) Q (garbled): The same stage said to be only the beginning? A: a more expanded self begins another journey; to be discussed later.
- (¶66–67) Q (inaudible; likely about non-Muslim mystics): A: a Christian and a Muslim can both reach fana but at different destinations; Sharia-based route is more likely to succeed.
- (¶69–70) Q: Different types of annihilation, and annihilation in the divine essence? A: deferred to next session.

## 7. Problems in the transcript
- (¶11) "Sana……(11:15)…." gap; "fana" is presumably meant. Also "tajliyyah" etc. are glossed in ¶12 as "Sana" before explanation.
- (¶12) Takhliyah called "also tarkhiyyah"; this is likely a mishearing of "tazkiya"/"tarkiya" (purification). Do not fix silently.
- (¶12) The correspondence between takhliyah and "purification" and tahliyah and "perfection" was stated confusingly; "tahliyyah" and "tajliyyah" are easily confused.
- (¶16) Surah al-A'la given as "sura86"; it is 87 (confirmed by the lecturer's own Q 87:6 at ¶20).
- (¶17) Gap "(19:40)" in the middle of the Sadiq hadith; the dhikr source is lost.
- (¶18) The dhikr is partially mis-transcribed (e.g. "yahyo wayo meet", "khayrwa"). Do not reproduce as exact.
- (¶20) "Sura 87 ayat 6" is correct.
- (¶21) Q 73:1–4 is given as quoted text with no reference; "sura 16 ayat 44" attached to the dhikr claim.
