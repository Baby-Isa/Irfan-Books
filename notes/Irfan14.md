# Lecture 14: The Sufi Tariqa (Orders, Silsilas and Techniques of Dhikr)

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan14.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture introduces the Sufi tariqa: what the word means, why the lecturer prefers to leave it untranslated, and how it works as an institution (a founding elder, a silsila of masters back to the Prophet, total submission to a sheikh, sub-orders, dual membership, decline in modern times) (¶3–21). He contrasts it with the Shia position, where no tariqa exists, a seeker simply finds a trustworthy guide who has travelled the path himself, and the Imams hold the inherited esoteric knowledge (¶6, ¶8, ¶22–25). He then surveys the major Sunni orders, giving the most space to the Qadiriyya, the Suhrawardiyya (with a long digression on the Ghazali brothers, Fayd Kashani and the shaytan-worship claim) and the Naqshbandiyya (¶25–91). Throughout, the lecturer draws a distinctively Shia lesson: the techniques of dhikr are secondary, every person's journey and experience is different, and the soul is a "channel" that receives God's grace in its own shape (¶65–71). The lecture opens the series' treatment of organised Sufism; a Shia tariqa is promised for later (¶25).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 What "tariqa" means and how not to translate it (¶3–5)
- A common belief: to be a Sufi or mystic you must belong to a tariqa, have a silsilah and a Shaikh, otherwise "you cannot move into this journey towards Allah" (¶3).
- The literal meaning is "path", so a Sufi tariqa is a path towards Allah, "however, this concept has developed to be more than just a path" (¶3–4).
- Western comparisons: "brotherhood" or "fraternity" (members regard each other as brothers of one culture) and "order" (monastic orders, with people who work and people who organise) (¶4).
- Verdict: these comparisons only show similarity with Western mysticism; "the best thing is not to translate it to anything and just say that this is tariqa or a path towards Allah" (¶5).
- Two defining features, read from a quoted definition: (1) the emergence of "elders" with specific programmes of instruction, linked to a founder believed to have instituted the method; (2) every brotherhood has a "chain", silsila, a pedigree of masters going back to the Prophet (¶5–6).

### 2.2 Tariqa versus Shia practice (¶5–6, ¶8–10)
- "We don't have such a thing as tariqa in Shia"; as explained before, the concept of Imamah would not allow this type of silsilah to come in (¶6).
- The elder's role: he initiates an instruction method that makes wayfaring easier; people gather around him; he is "regarded to have extraordinary powers, extraordinary relationship with Allah" (¶6). Followers want to know which stations to pass through until the final stages (¶7).
- All major tariqas arose in "the seventh century"; before that there were great mystics (some discussed in the previous session) but no tariqa. It was "invented later on" when people felt they needed instructors rather than relying on their own practices (¶7). The lecturer says the reason these orders "popped up" in the Sunni world then needs deeper research (¶11).
- Every tariqa claims its knowledge came by silsilah from the Prophet. The lecturer calls this "mainly a Shia idea": the Prophet's knowledge is transferable by inheritance to the Imams, which is why Imam Ali is not "an ordinary companion"; no companion has this inheritance, the same rank, or the same way of receiving knowledge from Allah (¶8).
- So tariqas, "although they do not believe in Imam", hold that there must be an authority who receives the esoteric knowledge of the prophets (¶8).
- Exoteric/esoteric split: other companions received exoteric knowledge (sharia, exegesis); the Prophet's knowledge also had to pass "from heart to heart" (¶9). In Sufism the chain runs sheikh to sheikh, but "it's not an orderly type of transference", unlike the Shia line of Imams, which was clear from the Prophet's time (¶9).
- Hence the lecturer's claim, repeated "as we studied before": Sufism is "just a branch of Shiaism in Sunni faith", "a cult of Shia in the Sunni world", importing Shia concepts while professing belief in Abu Bakr and Umar (¶10).
- Succession disputes: a sheikh appoints his successor and there are conflicts, just as after each Imam some claimed imamah for themselves even though Imams appointed their successors (¶10–11).

### 2.3 Sub-tariqas and dual membership (¶11–14)
- Most tariqas around the world are offshoots of "six to seven" tariqas founded in the seventh or eighth century (¶11).
- A sub-tariqa arises when a forceful personality, more influential than the founder, clashes with the elder's teachings or brings new ones; the tariqa is then named after him (¶12). Quoted definition: "a path within another path" (¶12).
- Double-barrelled labels carry both founders' names. Example: the Naqshbandi tariqa was revived in India by Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi, the Mujaddid, giving "Mujaddid Naqshbandi" (¶13).
- Sometimes the sub-order eclipses the parent. Example: the Noorbakshiyya (which the lecturer calls very corrupt) came from the Suhrawardiyya but no longer calls itself that "because the teachings are completely different" (¶13–14). Also Suhrawardiyya giving rise to Kubrawiyya through the "formidable" Najm al-Din Kubra (¶14).
- In later centuries a Muslim may belong to two or more orders at once, even where practices conflict: Naqshbandi forbids sima, Suhrawardiyya allows music (¶14).
- Ijaza abuse: he knows people who hold ijaza to instruct from "almost all tariqas existing in the world", granted by heads of tariqas as "an honorary type of compliment" to a travelling prominent Sufi. This makes "the whole concept of tariqa" meaningless, since the instructions contradict each other (¶18–19).

### 2.4 Sima (listening to music and poetry) (¶14–18)
- Explanation: in old days refinement of the soul was thought to come from good preaching; Sufi gatherings added a nice rhythmic voice; in Iran today circles recite poems of Hafez beautifully (¶15).
- Some disallowed even the nice voice; in some orders music was added, the idea being that harmony draws the soul's attention (¶15–16).
- It was a great controversy; Shias attacked Sufis for holding sessions of music, poetry and voice (¶16). Today it seems "quite common", the world having changed, "especially after these fatwa about music" (¶16–17).
- Forbidden in one order, allowed in another (¶18).

### 2.5 Decline of the tariqa and the problem of total submission (¶19–21)
- Tariqas were very powerful after the seventh century until a few centuries ago; now they have lost weight because people study Islam and find their own methods rather than listening to a sheikh (¶19).
- Key asset of a tariqa: you must submit completely to the sheikh, "whatever he says whatever he does" (¶20).
- The lecturer claims the Ismailis, much influenced by Sufi tariqa, have become "a type of tariqa itself": the Agha Khan "does whatever he wants" and members cannot object because "he is the imam" (¶20).
- Analogy: the disciple before the sheikh is like a corpse before the person doing ghusl al-mayyit, "completely powerless"; the washer does as he wills. "Now it's very difficult for modern mind to accept this isn't it?" (¶21).

### 2.6 The Shia alternative: a guide who has walked the path (Q&A, ¶22–25)
- In Shiaism there is no tariqa; one finds an instructor who does not come from any tariqa or hold any ijaza (¶22).
- Reported advice of "Syed Ali Kazmini" (see section 7): do not enter a tariqa to find a sheikh; look in your life for someone you can trust with your soul (¶22).
- You cannot judge such a person by writings or studies; "you have to make sure that these people have themselves, actually have a journey along the path" (¶23).
- Reason: the nafs can reach strange states (hating worship, hating society) and the traveller does not know what they mean; one who has gone through them can say what to do next (¶23).
- Testimony: Ayatullah Hassanzadeh wrote that he met many experiences he did not understand, went to Allamah Tabataba'i and "very easily he explained for me, what you have to do next" (¶23–24). The hardest are experiences "without persons"; if you do not know what to do, "they get lost", so a sheikh is necessary at that point (¶24).
- Tariqas say you need an instructor from the start, "which is something we don't have in our Shia faith"; a great sheikh would prescribe which dhikr (Allahu Akbar or La ilaha illallah) suits which state (¶24–25). The tariqa claim that independent practice leads one astray is "something of an exaggeration" (¶25).

### 2.7 The seven major Sunni tariqas (¶25)
- Listed: Qadiriyya ("very famous"), Suhrawardiyya, Shadhiliyya, Naqshbandiyya, Kubrawiyya, Chishtiyya, Tijaniyya. "All Sunni tariqas", prevailing "from seventh to eighth ninth century"; a Shia tariqa will be discussed later (¶25).

### 2.8 Qadiriyya and Abd al-Qadir Jilani (¶26–34)
- Jilani was "from the north of Iran" (the lecturer's hometown region), raised and flourished in Baghdad; studied Hanbali fiqh at 17; converted to Sufism by the "stern" master Abu'l-Khair Hammad ibn Muslim al-Dabbas (¶27–28).
- Broader point: beyond Sunni/Shia, one could divide Muslims into Shias, Sufis and Sunnis; Sufi elders hugely influenced rulers and caliphs, who listened because the elders "said things about the future" (¶27–28).
- Life outline as transcribed: Baghdad at 17; Sufism; success until about 50 and becoming a "great mystic"; a dream urged him to teach, and he returned to social life as "a fiery Hanbali preacher" (¶28–29). He regarded himself mostly as a preacher and wrote little; students founded the tariqa in his name (¶29–30). His teaching: "a standard exposition of the ethical and moral precepts incumbent on the Sunni Muslim" (¶30).
- Criticised by Ibn Taymiyya for mystical listening; Ibn Taymiyya "the greatest enemy of Sufis", who attacked bidaat of Sufis first, then Shia, and spared almost no one (¶30).
- His Baghdad shrine, rebuilt by an Ottoman sultan (Suleiman), is said to be near Abu Hanifa's tomb; the tariqa is strong in India, Pakistan and Turkey; his name is invoked when cholera or epidemic strikes (read, not seen, by the lecturer); respected among Sunni, not Shia (¶30–31).
- Q2: Why do people pay attention to him? A: "he had some truth", was forceful and positive, and Sunnis like him because he attacked Shias fiercely, including the Ismailis "in the name of all Shia worlds"; Twelver ulema wrote books against him. Verdict: "80% of what he said was wrong. 20% was right", and the mixture attracted people (¶32–34).

### 2.9 Suhrawardiyya (¶35–60)
- Two Suhrawardis: Abu al-Najib Abd al-Qadir Suhrawardi (uncle, born about 497 in Suhraward, Iran, near the lecturer's hometown) and his nephew and disciple Shihab al-Din Abu Hafs Umar Suhrawardi, the true founder and author of Awarif al-Ma'arif, "the last classical sufi manual" (¶36, ¶56). Do not confuse either with Shihabuddin Suhrawardi the Illuminationist ("aluminous philosophy"), who was Shia whereas these are Sunni (¶36).
- Irony noted: Suhrawardis trace genealogy to Abu Bakr, yet "all these tariqas take the chain from Imam Ali to the prophet"; only the Naqshbandiyya differs, tracing through Imam Sadiq but saying the knowledge passes through Abu Bakr (¶36–37). "It is an irony that the Shias has created in this way" (¶37).
- Umar Suhrawardi's kunya and name match Umar b. al-Khattab (¶56).
- Al-Najib studied with "Abu Hamid al-Ghazali in Esfahan"; the lecturer then separates Abu Hamid from "Muhammad al-Ghazali", his elder brother, and calls the latter a moderate Sufi (¶37). See section 7.

#### Digression: Ghazali's Ihya and Fayd Kashani (¶37–45)
- Best reference for Sufi teaching in the fifth and sixth centuries is Ihya Ulum al-Din: "very resourceful, very influential, instructive, but full of rubbish" (¶37–38). Its hadith and anecdotes about virtues are unreliable; "it's amazing how this great man could have revealed these issues" (¶38).
- Q3 (inaudible) A: Ghazali was sincere and very knowledgeable, but relied on unreliable anecdotes and hadith, "just wrote them to preach people" (¶39–40).
- Some Shia scholars hold that Ghazali converted to Shiism late in life; this is promoted by Fayd Kashani, who refined the Ihya in a book "the revival of the revival" (¶41). Kashani's introduction praises Abu Hamid (ra) as a great man and says the book is useful (¶41–42). Passages of the Ihya can still move the reader (¶42).
- Q: Who is a Mustabshir? A: one who "finds insight"; Shias use it for anyone converting from Sunnism to Shiism (¶43–44).
- Kashani removed anecdotes and hadith he disbelieved and added hadith from the Imams; the result, al-Mahajja al-Bayda ("the clear path"), "has become a treasure"; Islamic College is translating it (¶45).

#### Digression: Ahmad Ghazali and the shaytan-worshippers (¶46–54)
- Ahmad al-Ghazali, "more well known in the world of mysticism", was stringent in following al-Hallaj and the intoxicated mystics (¶46). The lecturer says this gave rise to a tariqa that "worships shaytaan", also called Yazidi, seen in parts of Iran, e.g. a city near the Iraqi border where everyone is "shaytaan parast" (¶46–47).
- The argument attributed to Ahmad al-Ghazali: shaytan was "the first muwahid": though punished he refused to prostrate to anyone but Allah. His reply to God: even if put in hell, I will not betray my love by worshipping another (¶46–48).
- Rebuttals (Q4, Q5): the command was to humble oneself before Adam, so prostration was not shirk; shaytan refused from arrogance ("I am better than him"), not love (¶49–51, ¶54). Lecturer's comment: many Sufis "were not very much familiar with the reasoning of the Quran" and built on what they heard from masters (¶51). Quip: Satan says "the one who has written my story is my enemy", so don't believe him (¶54).
- Studying under Ahmad did not make Suhrawardi follow this, though the cult left "an ambivalent attitude" toward shaytan (¶52).

#### Back to Suhrawardi (¶55–60)
- On returning to Baghdad he became a disciple of Hammad al-Dabbas, also Jilani's teacher, and taught at the Nizamiyya (as Ghazali did while a Sufi); students included Ibn Asakir and al-Sam'ani (¶55).
- Nizamiyya schools: built in Abbasid times by the vizier Nizam al-Mulk; "like Oxford and Cambridge"; a CV line saying one taught there signalled excellence (¶55–56).
- Hallmark practice: perpetual La ilaha illallah. Popular with Shia mystics for two reasons (greatness of the formula itself; it is "zikrul khafi", the hidden dhikr, since SubhanAllah is audible to others while La ilaha illallah can be said unnoticed) (¶57–58). If you want to show off, SubhanAllah; if you want to hide, La ilaha illallah (¶58).
- Goal: the tongue's remembrance becomes the heart's, the heart empties of everything, and God manifests in it (¶58).
- Techniques: say it for a long time, in a dark room, in a set posture and rhythm, so the heart takes up the rhythm and the soul gets habituated to "no God but Allah" (¶59). "The whole issue of tariqas are these techniques" (¶59).
- Suhrawardiyya stresses prayer, dhikr, Ramadan fasting and sharia, but its dhikr exercises are modified along "Yogic" lines (posture); also Chishtiyya in India, and strongly in Naqshbandiyya (¶60).

### 2.10 Naqshbandiyya (¶61–91)
- Lecturer calls it the most important and powerful tariqa and "the biggest enemy of all Shias": "if you find a naqshbandi do not tell them that you are Shia because they will kill you to go to paradise". The founder (not Iranian) himself did not promote this hatred; his students did (¶62).
- Name: naqsh means imprint; he was not an embroiderer; the usual explanation (imprinting Allah's name on the heart by silent, permanent dhikr) was "made up later on" (¶62–63).
- Founder Baha al-Din Muhammad Naqshband: descent attributed to Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, yet silsila through Abu Bakr; known in Bukhara as "Khaji-e Bala-gardan", "averter of disaster", to whom followers do tawassul as Shias do to their Imams (¶63). Associated with the Mongol ruler Ghazan Khan, through whom he spread the order; buried at Qasr-i Hinduwan, renamed Qasr-i Arifan (¶64).
- Seven characteristics listed (¶64–81):
  1. Silent remembrance, not aloud; followers are socially inclined (¶64).
  2. Denunciation of dancing and music, such as the Mevlevi whirling of Turkey, "a symbolic type of movement" (¶72–73).
  3. Deprecation of karamat (¶74–75), an ascetic style.
  4. Pious company (suhbah) rather than retreats (khalwah) (¶76).
  5. Fidelity to sharia in social, political and devotional life, hence many Naqshbandis become politicians in the Arab world, Africa and Indo-Pakistan (¶77–78).
  6. Marked hostility to Shii Islam, probably developed after Baha al-Din; the only tariqa not tracing its silsila to Ali (¶79).
  7. Rejection of Ibn Arabi's wahdat al-wujud, which "cannot be understood by a simple mind"; these people "were very intellectual" (¶80–81).

#### The lecturer's lesson: no single method (¶65–71)
- Because Naqshbandis are social, silent dhikr-sayers and others secluded, loud dhikr-sayers can all be arifs: "there is no one single method for practicing irfan" (¶65). Techniques are "not very important", which is why Shia irfan does not prescribe loud/silent or this/that way (¶65).
- Image of the channel: your soul is a canal or river to Allah, and every river is different, so experiences differ; Shia urafa stress this (¶66).
- Coin analogy: some Sufis reported hearing a sound like dropping coins in the heart at a station; others anxiously ask why it does not happen to them. Answer: people differ "as their faces are different, as their taste are different"; "we are not photocopies of each other" (¶66–67).
- Purpose of creation (with a Quranic allusion, see section 4): the channel exists for a permanent flow of grace, knowledge and light from Allah (¶67–68). We block it with our actions, "like all kinds of different dirt going to river"; irfan's travel is mainly cleaning the channel (¶68–69).
- The grace "will take your shape", like water in a vessel; in paradise everyone's grace will differ: "Grace is one but it has come into different types of containers" (¶69).
- Hadith that "the ways to Allah are as many as the number of people": wrongly used to say any religion will do; the right reading is that within Islam (Quran, salah) each person's experience differs, like tasting the same fruit differently (¶70). Hence everyone should find his or her own suitable practice; the emphasis is on piety, and this reflects Naqshband's own experience (¶71).

#### Three "awarenesses" (¶82–89)
- Wuquf-i zamani ("temporal awareness"): checking one's state and improvement after each dhikr session (¶83).
- Wuquf-i adadi ("numerical awareness"): counting dhikr mentally, no tasbih, so the mind is not distracted; the lecturer is unsure how it works but sees value: "it doesn't let you to think about your business", provided one balances counting with meaning (¶84–85).
- Wuquf-i qalbi ("awareness of the heart"): directing attention to the physical heart so it joins the dhikr; imagine it is your heart saying La ilaha illallah. The lecturer calls it the most important and says it spread to other orders (¶86–88). Method: sit cross-legged, imagine the heart, breathe in on "La ilaha", out on "illallah" (¶88). Some Shia mystics advise this and cite a hadith from Imam Ali (emptying yourself of all but Allah on "illallah"); the lecturer is unsure of authenticity (¶88–89).
- Mujaddidi branch founded in India by Ahmad Sirhindi (d. 1034/1624), the Mujaddid (Renewer) (¶89).
- Q6 (inaudible) A: He has seen hadith from Imam Ali and Imam Reza on this dhikr, but "they are not very reliable. They might be made up"; do not rely on what Sufis made up in the past (¶90–91).
