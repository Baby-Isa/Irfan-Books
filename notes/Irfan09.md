# Lecture 09: Fanaa, its degrees and aftermath, and the Four Journeys

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan09.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture opens with a recap of the four steps of the mystic's path: tajliyah (furbishing), takhliyah (purging), tahliyah (adorning), then fanaa (¶2–3). It then deepens fanaa. Fanaa is defined as an epistemological rather than ontological event: the mystic sees only God and loses self-perception. It is the beginning of the journey and not its end (¶3–6, 20). The lecturer sets out the dangers and possible reactions after fanaa: being captivated for ever, shatahat with suspension of takleef, and licentiousness. He sets against these the healthy outcome, "separation after unification" (farq baad al-jamʿ) and baqaa (¶30–50). He then lists the graded degrees of fanaa: in action, in attributes, in essence (¶52–69). The lecture closes with the mystics' "four divine journeys", linking the discussion to Mulla Sadra's title and giving a Quranic anchor for each stage (¶70–93). In the series it completes the treatment of fanaa/baqaa begun last week and prepares for the later discussions of zikr and wahdat al-wujud (¶25, 64).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Recap: the four steps and what fanaa is (¶2–9)
- Last week's four steps: tajliyah (furbishing body and soul with what Allah recommended to the Prophet), takhliyah (taking out of the heart what is evil), tahliyah (adorning the soul with knowledge, extra faith, "bonuses from the supernatural world"), then fanaa/annihilation, followed by baqaa/survival (¶2–3).
- Readers of mysticism confuse fanaa with losing individuality so that "nothing of them would remain", which "sounds not very reasonable" (¶3).
- The best definition he knows is by Alexander Knysh (the Russian orientalist; transcribed "Kynsh"), "Islamic Mysticism". It has two complementary parts (¶3):
  - (a) the passing away of all things from the mystic's consciousness, self-perception included. The vacuum is filled by a pure consciousness of God (¶3–4).
  - (b) the falling away of the imperfect attributes of the fallible creature and their replacement by the pure attributes of the Divine Self (¶5).
- Metaphor: God becomes a veil covering the mystic so that he sees nothing but Him. For ordinary people creation is the veil that bars us from seeing God; at fanaa God "becomes a veil" to creation, so they no longer see creation (¶4–5).
- "It is not an ontological... but an epistemological issue": the mystic's knowledge is tampered with so that he cannot see anything but God (¶4).
- It is not the culmination but "just the initiation"; the mystics are thrilled and excited at the start (¶5).
- Mysticism aims at tasting reality (the term transcribed "tasty", see section 3). Fanaa is that taste, and nothing is more pleasurable. But it is not reality in its wholeness: the mystic is barred from creation and "still has a long way to go" to see both creation and Allah (¶6–7).
- Fanaa of human characteristics, meaning human instincts, drive and "whatever evil in you", paves the way for attaining the divine attributes. Knowledge and glory then "fall and land into your heart" (¶7–8).
- Fanaa is not Buddhist nirvana, which is destruction of individuality. In Islamic mysticism "it is the individual who is going to be there forever getting more expanded, more capable" (¶8).
- Yahya Yathribi (Iranian philosopher-mystic scholar): fanaa is a positive process, "breaking the lower limits and going to the higher stations" (¶8).
- It is also a return to man's original selfhood, lost to worldly desires. It is "breaking the walls of illusion". People think fanaa itself is the illusion and that those who break through have gone astray, when it is really a move "out of the imaginary you to the real you" (¶9).

### 2.2 Who is the "you"? Audience exchange and the layers of self (¶10–16)
- A student's remark (partly inaudible) is that the original self reflects through the material world, and that we wrongly think our actions and attributes belong to us. The lecturer agrees: "Of course that's true" (¶11–14).
- Exercise: when about to do something wrong, something says "don't do it". "Who is telling who?" He sets it as homework over the week: "a very, very crucial point" (¶14–15).
- Analysing daily self-talk (encouraging, rebuking oneself) shows that we have "different layers of reality". One part drives us to desires, another forbids. "It's all us all the same we". To annihilate some layers is to come out of an imaginary niche (¶15).
- When alone, why do we think of God? Not only punishment and reward: "there's something inside us which drives... towards Allah. It's as if he has... strings and pulling us". "Someone inside us which is different from us" tries to get away from the illusion. Annihilation lets that someone "release, grow and go back to the origin" (¶16).

### 2.3 Rumi on annihilation and survival; the Quranic verses (¶17–19)
- The size and greatness one feels can only be known by experience (¶17).
- A poem of Rumi, recited in Farsi with translation: "although joining with God is all survival in survival, however, at the beginning it is annihilation in annihilation" (the lecturer's own translation) (¶17).
- Commentary: the part of you that is lost is an illusion. It is scary at first ("you think you are dying") yet also pleasurable. Joining God means expansion of being, but at first one sees only the destruction of the illusion of self (¶17–18).
- When all illusions go: "who am I? What is it that exists?" One gets "familiar with the news you didn't know at all" (¶18).
- Q 39:47 (as quoted): they see from God what they never reckoned. He applies it to death and to the mystic: "we see God in the way that we never would have reckoned". Q 39:48 (as quoted) is applied to unbelievers: they see their evil deeds (¶18–19).

### 2.4 After fanaa: danger, Shaytan, shirk, and salat as culminating zikr (¶20–29)
- "That's the beginning, that's not the end." Most mystics never reach it, and for those who do "there are dangers still ahead" because the soul is "so complicated" (¶20).
- Example: Shaytan's station. He was so close to Allah that there was "no takabbur" in that place, and once he did takabbur it showed the station was still dangerous. Mystics know this from experience (¶20–21).
- Mukhlasin are safe from Shaytan "but not from their own" self: "There was no one to defeat Shaytan, it was his own self" (¶22–23). Partly inaudible; the sense is reconstructed.
- Q 39:65 (as quoted): the revelation to Muhammad and those before him that if you commit shirk your deeds will fail. The "shirk of the Prophets is different from our shirk", hence Prophets and Imams were careful "about every minute of life" (¶23–24).
- Ordinary believers follow them, "we have our own credit and we go to Jannah by their grace", hence we send salawat on Muhammad and the Family of Muhammad (¶24).
- Salat zikr: even though we neglect thinking of Allah in it, we are told to do it and will go to Jannah. "Because Mohommad did it in the real world" (¶24–25).
- Salat is "the culmination of human zikr"; Sufi gatherings, Christian and Jewish zikr are other types. He promises a full discussion when zikr is reached (¶25).
- Four levels of zikr, taken from the ethics class (¶25–26):
  - (1) speaking the name or a title of Allah;
  - (2) pressing the meaning back into the heart;
  - (3) it is simply present in the heart, always mindful of Allah's presence;
  - (4) it flows from the heart to the tongue and all parts of the body.
- The Meʿraj salat narrative as the model (¶26–27):
  - The Prophet saw such glory that he said takbirat al-ihram, then recited what was revealed to him seeking guidance.
  - After recitation a further glory came on him and he was overwhelmed.
  - Two interpretations: either he inadvertently bowed to the glory and said the ruku zikr, or, per some hadith, he did not know what to do, asked Jibraʾil, who told him to bow.
  - On rising he saw an even greater glory and went into prostration saying the sujud zikr. He did this twice and it was legislated for all Muslims.
- Our salat is thus the culmination of human zikr. We are not told to do it in his Meʿraj attitude: "Just anything and you go to paradise" (¶27–28).
- Therefore the situation of those close is "more perilous, more dangerous" than ours. The same holds for ʿulama versus laity: "don't try to become part of Olama, it is not always useful, sometimes works against you" (¶29).
- Hadith of Meʿraj as cited: the Prophet saw people whose lips were cut with fire scissors. Jibraʾil said they were ʿulama who preached and did not practise (¶29).

### 2.5 Three possible reactions after fanaa (¶30–44)
- Source: a "solid introduction" to the Taʾiyya of Ibn al-Faridh (transcribed "Ibne Faaris"), described as a great Arab mystic poet "like Hafiz in Persia" (¶30).
- **Reaction 1: permanent captivation.** The ʿarif is attracted to the Divine Light and mesmerized by God's beauty for ever. They go out of themselves and cannot see time. This is "a very minority", only a few mystics (¶30).
- Analogy: people react differently to the same accident, one in shock for days, another unmoved. So with encountering the supernatural. The weakness is of "the body potentials", which is why Prophets have the strongest bodies (¶31).
- Jannah digression: why wine in Jannah? So one can endure the glory of daily unveiling (¶32–33).
  - Q 76:5 (as quoted): the righteous drink from a cup mixed with kafur (camphor).
  - Q 76:17 (as quoted): a cup mixed with zanjabil (ginger).
  - The lecturer's tentative speculation: ginger-like warms and prepares the soul to receive unveiling, camphor calms after the excitement. "We cannot speculate... just playing with different verses... it might not be the case at all" (¶34–36).
- Possibly there is a cure before the encounter, even physical books, "I don't know" (¶37).
- **Reaction 2: temporary mesmerization with takleef lifted.** Orafa say at these stations a guide is needed, not for mustahabbat or makruhat, which are in books. In Shia tradition there is no formal shaykh–murid structure, "mostly Sunni Sufi tradition" (¶37–38).
  - They speak shatahat, "silly statements" such as "I am the Lord, there is nothing in me but Allah", or the one who said "how glorified I am" and later "I'm just a small humble servant" (¶39).
  - The mystics themselves call such statements "complete kufr", knowledge falling "much short of the knowledge that the prophet has provided". They come from the "unconsolable situation of the soul" and the speaker is out of his mind (¶39–41).
  - Some mystics claim takleef is permanently removed even for those in their senses. This is unacceptable to Shia and majority-Sunni mysticism (¶41).
  - Only a minority hold this, but laypeople lean towards it. Being out of takleef is true only as it is for the insane (¶42).
- **Reaction 3: licentiousness and irreligion**, the most dangerous. They cannot distinguish self, Lord and creation, so say "whatever sin is done is done by God" (¶43).
- Most who fall into this pitfall got there by wrong practice, rushing themselves. A person who practises balanced worship and the right zikr is gradually uplifted (¶44).

### 2.6 The healthy outcome: farq baad al-jamʿ / sahw baad al-mahw (¶45–48)
- The correct outcome is farq baad al-jamʿ (separation after unification) or sahw baad al-mahw (sobriety after intoxication or obliteration) (¶45).
- Having been united with God "in a way that they think", with illusions gone, they gradually separate themselves from God and God from creation. They see unity and diversity without either veiling the other (¶45).
- For us, God is veiled by creation. At fanaa creation is veiled by God. At the higher station neither veils the other (¶46).
- A story about Hazrat Musa illustrating unification and separation was announced but skipped for lack of time (¶46).
- Warning: mystical experiences are many, and "the mess there is more extensive than the mess in this world" (¶47).
- Entering needs correct instruction and a "dosage of medicine and food" to grow in balance, unlike imbalanced growth, for example an enormous head and tiny body. Sharia and the arkan given by the Prophet take us "in a gradual, wholesome and beneficial manner" (¶47–48).

### 2.7 Baqaa billah (¶48–51)
- The person has lost self-reliance and now relies on Allah, which makes tawakkul real (¶49).
- Baqaa billah is continual existence in God after acquiring some divine attributes, with renewed awareness of created plurality. Fanaa is sukr (intoxication) and baqaa the sobriety that follows, a higher sobriety than the sahw before fanaa (¶50).
- Fanaa is witnessing God's beauty and glory, which is promised for Judgement Day. Cited: "Wujooh yawma'idhin nadirah" (Q 75:22) (¶50–52).

### 2.8 Degrees of fanaa (¶52–69)
- Fanaa is graded and not sudden (¶52).
- **Fanaa in action (mahw).** The mystic sees that everything he does comes from God, and that "it is really not you who is seeing" (¶52–53).
  - Even killing is done by Allah's power. The hand moves because "the life of Allah... has flowed over into this hand" (¶53).
  - The origin of intention is "one of the most complicated issues": how intention is attributed to us and separated from Allah's will. He sets it aside (¶53–54).
  - Realisation of "la hawla wala quwwata illa billah". Hawl is from tahawwul, moving from place to place (¶54).
- Question (inaudible). Answer: thinking is not enough. "You can sit and think for weeks"; one must taste (¶55–57).
- Q 18:44 (as quoted): "Hunalika al-walayatu lillahi al-haqq", where no authority is known but Allah. "This should be experienced... rather than described" (¶57).
- Analogy: thinking of a recipe versus tasting the dish. "That is realization" (¶57–59).
- The practice: la hawla wala quwwata illa billah, seven times after fajr and maghrib (some say 10), advised by all sects and in many hadith: "it is magic" (¶60).
- **Fanaa in attributes (tams, probably tamas or tajalli; see section 7).** Wherever you look you see God's attributes. "Allah" is the name that comprehends all attributes, so the zikr is La ilaha illa Allah (¶60–61).
  - It is "one level further" than la hawla. Analogy: a city of lamps, computers, planes and cars all powered by a power station. Unplug it and everything stops. "This is not a right example" (¶62–63).
  - Q 2:255 (as quoted): the only living being is Allah. Life means movement, knowledge, power; at this level wherever you look, you look at Allah (¶63).
- **Fanaa in essence (wahdat al-wujud).** Not even attributes are seen, only his glory overshadowing everything. This will be discussed later: "very difficult... if you try to explain it people get confused" (¶64).
  - Its zikr: "Ya man laysa illa Huwa", where huwa is a pronoun alluding to Him alone (¶65).
  - Hadith attributed to Amir al-Muminin: on the ninth of Badr he dreamt of Khidr and asked for a du'a, who said "ya Huwa, ya man Huwa illa Huwa". It is reported that on the day of Badr he was seen attacking from every direction reciting Surat al-Ikhlas and this phrase (¶65).
- Huwa in the Quran: Q 112 is cited in full. "What is Allah? Allah hus-samad". "Lam yalid wa lam yulad" refutes questions of father and mother. Scholars could not give a better one-line description of God. A hadith (as cited): "This is my description and this is my genealogy" (¶66–68).
- Jurisprudence: it is makruh not to recite Ikhlas at least once a day. Hadith (as cited): a Muslim who does not recite it once a day or week is a kafir. The lecturer's explanation is that Amir al-Muminin was fond of it. Banu Umayya avoided it for that reason, and Sunnis now omit it in namaz, "a bad habit" from early Islam (¶68–69).

### 2.9 The four divine journeys (al-asfar al-arbaʿa) (¶70–93)
- Introduction: Mulla Sadra titled his philosophical book al-Asfar al-Arbaʿa. The lecturer asks why. He answers that Sadra claimed "rationalized mysticism", bringing felt experiences under rational arguments. Ibn Arabi, by contrast, is not worried about reason. Sadra called his philosophy "the transcendent philosophy", neither peripatetic nor illuminationist (¶70–71).
- The four journeys (¶71–72):
  1. from creation to the Haqq;
  2. by the Haqq in the Haqq;
  3. from the Haqq back to creation by the Haqq;
  4. in creation by the Haqq.
- **First journey** (¶72–77):
  - The wayfarer removes dark veils and luminous veils.
  - Dark veils belong to nafs; luminous veils to qalb and ruh.
  - The inner voice saying "don't do it" is qalb addressing nafs.
  - Dark veils are self-made through our actions. He cites Dua Abu Hamza: Imam Zayn al-Abidin says that He does not veil Himself from creation, and we veil ourselves by our deeds. This fits the "last verse" he mentions (¶73).
  - Other veils come from our existence itself: without them "the whole creation would burn out". Some buffers are put between God and creation (¶73–74).
  - Movement is from nafs (ammara), to qalb (lawwama, where understanding comes), to ruh (mutmainna, pure knowledge and light). It ends in fanaa and is "just one quarter of the way" (¶74–75).
  - The culmination of tazkiyah is fanaa (¶77).
- **Second journey** (¶77–82):
  - The wayfarer is now qualified to see the wonders of Allah. Most people will go through this journey in Jannah (¶77–78).
  - It is "by haqq" because he has nothing of himself and is under God's complete wilayah (¶78–79).
  - Du'a of the Imams in Salat al-Layl (as cited): guide me among those You guide; take charge of me among those whose affairs You take charge of; put me among the salihin (¶80).
  - He moves in the realm of Asmaʾ. Q 2:31 (as quoted): "wa allama Adam al-asmaʾa kullaha". God teaches the names, except those exclusively His or for those of higher caliber. Above everyone is someone higher, except the Prophet (¶80–81).
  - Fanaa is replaced by baqaa, and the ʿarif returns from mahw to sahw. He again feels his existence and servanthood. Note how the Quran describes the Prophet and "how we describe him" as servant (¶81–82).
- **Third journey** (¶82–89):
  - The wayfarer returns from God to the world under divine wilayah. He is shown how things exist besides Allah (¶83).
  - A tour of Jabarut (alam of Asmaʾ), Malakut (angels and arwah) and Nasut (the material world). "There is no difference": all are manifestations of Allah's names (¶83).
  - Q 6:75 (as quoted): "wa kadhalika nuri Ibrahima malakuta..." The outcome is reassurance (¶84–85).
  - He now knows God's attributes and acts and can inform others. The lecturer no longer calls him a mystical ʿarif, since mystics often cannot do this (¶85–86).
  - Q 2:260: Ibrahim asks how He gives life to the dead. Allah gives authority over His name: call them and they come hastily. This is "how the big people did things through Allah's names" (¶87).
- **Fourth journey** (¶87–93):
  - It is reserved for the Prophet, and the successors (the Imams). The third could be reached by very high mystics, but this one is exclusive (¶87, 89).
  - The ʿarif is acquainted with the reality of beings, their qualities and effects in this world and the next, and why things were created (¶87).
  - Cited: "wa ilayhi raji'un" (as quoted, "wa qullun elayhe raajeuun"). Everything's return to Allah and the consequences of actions are shown to him. This is "knowledge of the Prophet, not something artificial", stored in him (¶88).
  - He can inform us of the harms and benefits of everything and the causes of felicity or misery (¶88).
  - This is why the Imams knew answers to anything, including what was not in Sharia or the book. They also followed Sharia (¶89).
  - Brings him down to the level of ordinary people, so he can guide them. Mystics who finish only the first journey find it very difficult to mix with people. For the Prophet all is seen as connected to Allah (¶91).
  - Apparent answer to a question: someone who stopped midway has to "start all over again" (¶92–93).
