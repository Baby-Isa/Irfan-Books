# Lecture 16: Mystical Interpretation of the Quran, with Tusi on Hell and Paradise

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan16.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
This final lecture of the series (¶3) asks what legitimate "mystical interpretation" of the Quran is. The lecturer first excludes two things: arbitrary, subjective ta'wil unrelated to the verse, and readings imported from another ideology, with Ismaili Qadi al-Numan as the example (¶3–5). Legitimate mystical interpretation is an "insightful look" that finds deeper meaning which agrees with the apparent meaning and does not cancel it (¶5, ¶8). He grounds this in the Quranic parables (Light Verse; Surah Yasin's chains and barriers) and shows from internal Quranic comparison that "chain" and "yoke" in the afterlife may carry the same deeper sense as in this world (¶9–17). He then gives a long worked example: Nasir al-Din Tusi's account of why people enter hell or paradise, as the reverse of the soul's descent into creation. Existence, knowledge, power and will are given, then must be surrendered (will, power, knowledge, existence) in order to be re-given by God (¶17–32). Hell is the same process undergone against one's will, and the "chains" and "burning" are read through a multi-level (mulk/malakut/jabarut) ontology (¶39–55). It fits the series as its capstone: it applies the earlier teaching on fana, wahdat al-wujud, tawakkul, rida and shafa'a to Quranic exegesis (¶27–34).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Opening and aim (¶1–3)
- Formal opening (isti'adha, basmala). This is "our last session" and the topic is mystical interpretation; he wishes there were more time (¶1–3).
- The risk: interpretation can become "arbitrarily tahweel giving different meanings which have no relation to the verses" (¶3).
- What he does not mean: subjective meaning "at the will of some mystic", or a meaning "not at all attached" to the verse (¶3).

### 2.2 What mystical interpretation is not (¶4–5)
- Many mystical tafsirs exist. A very famous one (transcribed "Tafseer-e-Sunan"; see §7) reads the Quranic order to Musa's people to "kill yourselves" after the golden calf as killing all the desires of the soul, "which is not very much related to the history" (¶4).
- Also excluded: meanings taken from another ideology and laid over the verses, found "very much" in Ismaili exegesis (¶4).
- Example: Qadi al-Numan, "an Egyptian and the greatest Ismaili scholar of all history", whose Asas al-Ta'wil ("Foundation of Symbolic Interpretation") puts seven Imams and seven pillars into every verse. The story of Noah becomes a symbol of those, which is "not related at all" (¶4–5).
- Positive definition: "a type of insightful look at the verses of the Quran, to see what means you could get from a deeper look" (¶5).

### 2.3 Parables and the Light Verse (¶5–8)
- The Quran has parables (masal). The Light Verse (Surah Nur, 24:35) is the model: God is the Light of the heavens and earth, the "parable of its light" is a niche with a lamp. A parable brings meaning closer to the mind and, looked at with insight, "you might find lots of meanings in it" (¶5–6).
- Ghazali's Mishkat al-Anwar ("The Niche of Light") is "all a mystical interpretation of this verse". It reveals a Ghazali who is "known as an Ashari type of theologian" but here "believes in wahdatul wujud" and mystical readings of creation, "very different from the Ghazali that you find in other books" (¶7).
- Tabataba'i (al-Mizan) is cited for the principle that every Quranic parable is an allegory that points to a deeper meaning. That meaning must be "completely concurrent conforming with the apparent meaning and not against it" (¶7–8).
- Objectionable mystical interpretation is exactly where the deeper meaning fails to conform to the apparent one (¶8).
- Ghazali insists that when he gives a deeper meaning "the apparent meaning doesn't go away". The literal and the deeper both hold, as "different levels of understanding", and "that deeper layer of meaning has another deeper layer" (¶8).
- He plans to look at the Mishkat further if time allows ("tonight I need to have time") (¶9).

### 2.4 Test case: Surah Yasin 36:8–9, chains on the unbelievers (¶9–12)
- Text paraphrased: chains on necks reaching the chins, so heads are raised; a barrier before and behind; they are covered and do not see (¶9).
- His argument: nobody sees chains on the kuffar or chins forced up. It is not labelled a masal but "it is a masal, isn't it?" (¶10).
- He mimes the wooden yoke that pushes the chin up so that the person cannot see ahead, and notes that the unbeliever does not literally walk like that (¶10–11).
- No one takes it literally, "even those very stringent mufaseroon" who read the throne verse (20:5) literally, with God sitting on a throne (¶11).
- Deeper meaning: "we have covered the light, which is actually the shining light of Allah". The unbelievers walk blind to what is before and behind them (¶12).

### 2.5 Comparing worldly and jahannam verses (¶12–17)
- A first-impression reading could take the verse as about the kuffar in jahannam with raised heads. But jahannam "you cannot see it now", whereas this is said "about people in this world", so the deeper meaning is "transparent" (¶12–13).
- Then the Surah Haqqa verses (69:29–32) on the person led to hell: his power has perished, he is seized, chained, cast into the fire, in a chain of seventy cubits (¶13).
- Question posed: is this a "real material" chain? Perhaps it is "the chain of the ambitions, the chain of ignorance and the chain of everlasting long arrogant type of ambitions" they had in this world (¶14–15).
- Both may be true. He does not deny the material chain, but "we cannot deny the deeper meanings as well" (¶15).
- The point of the comparison: the two verse-types are similar. If the chain and yoke are not seen on the kuffar here, they may not be what is seen in jahannam either, "there is something else" (¶15–16).
- Further Quranic support: "whoever is blind in this world will be blind in the life to come" (17:72). The blindness there cannot be the same literal blindness, since the damned see and curse one another (7:38) and the weak and arrogant dispute (¶16–17).
- So there is a blindness, of kufr, that parallels worldly blindness (¶16–17, ¶36).

### 2.6 Tusi on maad: return as reverse of creation (¶17–20)
- Introduces "a very interesting mystical interpretation of hell and paradise" that "doesn't deny the material aspect" but explains why people go to each (¶17).
- Source: a book of Nasir al-Din Tusi (transcribed "Haji Nasiruddin Tusi, Reminder of the beginning and the end"). Mulla Sadra copied long excerpts into the Asfar. Tusi also wrote the commentary on Ibn Sina's Isharat, "that we just covered a little bit of" (¶17–18).
- Thesis: maad is "going back to the initial creation". We come into this world in a certain way and return "in the same way, it's a reverse type of process" (¶18). Cited: "as He began you, you shall return" (7:29, garbled; see §7) and the first creation (56:62) (¶19).
- Everyone is made for the return, but "they can't go there before coming into this process". Going to hell results from how one passes through coming and going (¶19).
- This world is made by God, and another world (hereafter, barzakh) is easy for Him (¶20).

### 2.7 The sequence of coming: existence, knowledge, power, will (¶20–22)
- First existence: from dust, a drop, a clot, then a child (40:67) (¶20).
- Then knowledge and awareness: the senses are "spies" of the soul. "Your senses are spies of your soul; your soul cannot know what's going on except through these senses" (a saying he calls "a very nice expression") (¶21). Cited 16:78: brought out of the mothers' wombs knowing nothing, given hearing, sight and hearts (¶21).
- Then power: a baby cannot do anything but gradually can "crawl for what you want", and power grows (¶22).
- Then will: first we know we are given power to obtain, then we will something and go and get it (¶22–23).

### 2.8 The sequence of return: will, power, knowledge, existence (¶22–32)
- To go back to God one must retrace in reverse. God now "wants you to go to the opposite direction" (¶22–23).
- 1. Will first: put aside your will. This is rida (contentment). When you have no will of your own, you are content with whatever comes from God (¶23–24).
- Paradise payoff: cited 50:35 ("they will have what they wish therein, and with Us is more"). If your will is God's will, whatever you want, you have. The "rizwan min Allah akbar" is "the greatest pleasure" (¶24).
- Tusi's point: the angel in charge of paradise is called Rizwan, so one cannot enter without contentment with God's will. Paradise is "full of treasures. And the key to it is this contentment" (¶25).
- Objection answered: if you have no will, what do you mean "do whatever you want"? You are given "a grace and a will", an everlasting will connected to God's will (¶25).
- Contrast: the angel of hell is Malik (43:77). The people of hell are his slaves, bound and without will (¶26).
- Recalled hadith (earlier lecture): an angel brings the people of paradise a letter from the Ever-Living who never dies to the ever-living who never dies, saying that "when I want something, I say be and it becomes", and "from today, when you want something you say and it will become" (¶26–27).
- 2. Power next: know that the power you use is God's power. This is tawakkul, and "doesn't mean that you just sit down and wait for God to do". Rather, "whatever you do you know that this is not you. This is him" (¶27–28). Cited 65:3 (¶28).
- 3. Knowledge: you submit and know that you "don't know anything"; only He knows and gives knowledge (¶28–29). Cited 4:65 (submit completely) (¶29).
- Recalled hadith from Amir al-Mu'minin: knowledge has three spans. In the first you become arrogant, in the second humble, in the third "you know that you don't know" (¶29).
- This is Ibrahim's submission (2:131) (¶30).
- 4. Existence: this is fana. You come to unity and recognise that existence belongs only to God. Wahdat al-wujud is "very complicated" but "your existence is a light of existence" (¶30).
- This is the station of those on whom God bestowed favour among the prophets (19:58), "a very luminous position" (¶30–31).
- Re-giving: once will, power, knowledge and existence are "done away", God gives new power, knowledge and existence (¶31).
- In paradise: ultimate consensus/contentment (rida); unrestricted power, "drowned" in God's power; innate knowledge that "will shine in you"; everlasting existence (50:34 "the day of eternity") (¶31–32).
- He advises students to read the book, "very, very enlightening" on tawakkul and submission (¶29).

### 2.9 Q&A on shafa'a and the light (¶33–39)
- See §6. In the answer he links the discussion to the necessity of shafa'a: "without the shafaa, it is impossible to enter paradise" because "this is a level which needs someone quite huge to take us there" (¶34).
- The light of the believers (57:12; 66:8): believers have a light before and at their right, but it is incomplete, and they pray "complete for us our light" (¶37).
- "Not humiliate the Prophet" (66:8) is read: if his followers went to hell, it would be a humiliation for him although he is perfect. So the Prophet completes their light by shafa'a (¶37–39).
- Hypocrites have no light and ask to "borrow" (57:13) (¶38–39).

### 2.10 What happens in the next world if the process is not done here (¶39–41)
- A believer must go through the process "either voluntarily or involuntarily". If not done voluntarily here, there is difficulty in barzakh and on the resurrection day (¶39).
- If completed here, one is among those for whom "good" was decreed beforehand and who are far from fear, quarrels, judgment and sirat (21:101–103, transcribed with wrong numbers) (¶40).
- If not completed, they must be stripped of will, shown that there is no power or life but God's, and "go as per the roll book" (¶41).
- Before paradise, grudges and envy are removed from hearts (15:47) (¶41).

### 2.11 Hell as opposed will (¶42–48)
- The people of hell have a will opposed to God's, and God's will wins. If God followed their wills, heavens and earth would be ruined (23:71) (¶42).
- So they "go without will", "cannot move without God overruling their will", and are "always in torment" (¶42–43).
- The seventy-cubit chain is "because of the length of the will that they have" (¶43). This reuses the chain reading from ¶15.
- Cited 3:162 (Rizwan versus the wrath of God) (¶44).
- Malik holds the damned as Sulayman held jinn as slaves, since their wills "would destroy the creation" (¶44–45).
- Helplessness: they lost their own power and do not know how to use God's. We are using God's power now without realising; if we realise it in the next world "it's very easy" (¶45–46). Cited 3:160 (¶46).
- Humiliation: instead of taslim they have hawan, because they thought they had knowledge and see they are "dumb, deaf and blind". Cited 6:93 (¶46).
- Separation: instead of union with God's will, power and knowledge, "they have separation" (¶47).
- Key theodicy point: hell "is not a punishment which is brought to you by God. God doesn't want to revenge." There is "only mercy"; people put themselves in a situation where they "don't deserve to use the power of God", and separation brings the problems (¶47–48).

### 2.12 Physical punishments and the levels of reality (¶48–54)
- Objection: what about eating thorns and drinking hot water? He says "of course it's all true. In what sense?" and asks the class (¶48–49).
- Student answer: it is not that it becomes terrible but that they lack the capacity to be nourished, so the effect is as though drinking boiling water (¶50).
- Lecturer: it is hard to drop the literal meaning, but there is another interpretation in an Imam Sadiq hadith: God created mulk (alam al-shahada) as an example of malakut, and malakut as an example of jabarut (¶51).
- Meanings in higher worlds "materialize in a different way" in lower ones. Fire in the world of mulk corresponds to something else in malakut. The Quran says those who eat orphans' property are eating fire, so in malakut it is that, and in jabarut something else, "all much related closely" (¶51–52).
- Ghazali's example (Mishkat): the Prophet saw Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf in a dream crawling or heading toward paradise. The meaning is that rich people cannot easily enter, with much reckoning. In the world of witnessing this appears as someone slowly climbing a hill (¶52–53).
- Moral: one reality has parallel true realisations in different worlds, as dream and reality differ yet both are right (¶53–54).

### 2.13 Summary contrast and conclusion (¶54–56)
- Hell versus paradise: instead of ultimate contentment, utter dissatisfaction. Instead of unlimited power, unlimited helplessness. Instead of innate knowledge, utter ignorance. Instead of existence, neither death nor life (87:13), "something in between" (¶54–55).
- Closing: this is "what I mean by insightfulness". The literal meaning is there but "there are different levels which are called mystical interpretation" (¶55). Closing salawat (¶56).

## 7. Problems in the transcript
- (¶4) "Tafseer-e-Sunan" is likely a mishearing of a mystical commentator's name or title (candidates are unverified). It should not be guessed.
- (¶17) "Haji Nasiruddin Tusi" is probably "Khwaja Nasir al-Din Tusi". "Reminder of the beginning and the end" is a translation of an unclear title.
- (¶19) "Come on by the 1000 isn't having the Quran" is garbled before the 7:29 citation.
- (¶40) "57:102, 57:103" are likely 21:102–103.
- (¶24) "rizwanu mennallahi akbar" is probably the Quran's "ridwanun min Allahi akbar".
- (¶37) 66:8 is given for both the "complete our light" phrase and the "not humiliate" phrase, which is consistent. The first is attributed to a "previous verse" confusingly.
- (¶28, ¶30) "tawaqqul" is likely "tawakkul".
- (¶5) "Allahma Tabatabai" (¶7) is Allamah Tabataba'i.
- (¶10) "yolks" means yokes.
- (¶51) "hades" means hadith. The hadith text is not quoted and "malaqut", "misale" are partly garbled.
- (¶53) "paradise crown" (travelling toward) is unclear. "Concept" in "what the prophet actually concept" is garbled.
- (¶33, ¶35) Audience questions are not audible.
- (¶9) "Niche of life" (also ¶52) is a mishearing of "Niche of Light".
- (¶53) The Ghazali dream story is not verified against the Mishkat.
- (¶26) A sentence about free will in hell ("Here, people are given utter free will...") appears garbled and may be reversed.
- (¶44) "Rizwan is for paradise... he is the same as the one who returns to wrath" is confusing (3:162 contrasts the one who follows God's pleasure with the one who incurs wrath).
- (¶50) Student answer is rendered awkwardly.
