# Lecture 04: The Human Soul as the Gateway to Knowing God, and the Worlds Around It

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan04.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture recaps the two branches of ʿirfan (practical and considerative/theoretical) and announces that the course will study theory first, then practice (¶3–7). It then begins the theory with the human soul, "the first and foremost concept," because the soul is the means of understanding (¶7). The argument runs in three movements. (a) Quran and hadith make the soul central: forgetting God means forgetting one's soul, and knowing one's soul is tied to knowing one's Lord (¶12–23). (b) A long Q&A tests how far "whoever knows his soul knows his Lord" can be pressed, covering the limits of knowing God, "meeting" God, and the roles of angels, prophets and the afterlife (¶24–60). (c) The mystics' cosmology: the soul is a compact copy of the cosmos, which has nested worlds (mulk/shahadah, barzakh/mithal, ʿuqul/arwah, then kursi, ʿarsh, jabarut). Dreams, death, and muraqabah are explained by the soul's movement between these worlds (¶61–96). The lecture sets up the later theory lectures (¶9, ¶66, ¶71).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Recap: two branches of ʿirfan (¶3–7)
- Mysticism has two branches: practical and considerative (theoretical). Considerative mysticism covers the mystics' theory of existence, the human soul and how they see the world. It is "more like philosophy," but its arguments rest on what is received "by the way of unveiling." Mystics see things, then "theorize it" and "put it in form of an argument" to give a world view. (¶3)
- Practical mysticism is the practice by which mystics gain unveiling knowledge. This knowledge is knowledge by presence (ʿilm huduri): the soul "encompasses" what is present to it. Analogy: we know the images in our mind because they are present to our soul and the soul encompasses them. (¶4–5)
- Difficulty: the soul cannot encompass God. The mystics' solution is to "take the limitations of the soul," which is fanaa or annihilation, so one can be unified with God and know him "as much as your capacity allow." (¶5–6)
- Considerative mysticism is acquired knowledge, full of jargon and technical terms. Anyone can study it and judge it sensible or nonsense. Practical mysticism cannot be spoken about except by going through the stages the mystics advise. (¶6–7)
- Course plan: first a "glance" at theoretical ʿirfan and how the mystics define the world through unveiling, then practical steps (¶7).

### 2.2 The soul in mystical thought (¶7–9)
- The soul is the first concept, "the means to understand and to learn," so one must know what it is, how it works and its capacities. (¶7)
- The mystics "exaggerate," in the lecturer's own word, in saying everything is inside the soul: all creation corresponds to some aspect of the soul. (¶8)
- Some mystics read the verse on teaching Adam "the names" (cited as 3:31) to mean that God put a "flavour" of every divine name into the human soul. (¶8)
- The well-known hadith that God created Adam in his own image is read as metaphor, not a sensible image: humans have all qualities that exist in God. (¶8)
- Those qualities are infinite and "very different" in God, but there is a similarity, so one can know God by referring to one's own soul. To be discussed later. (¶9)

### 2.3 Q1: Do students of ʿirfan have to believe? (¶10–11)
- Lecturer's answer: "We talk about considerative irfan but we do not practice." So one can understand, criticize and analyze without practicing, though mystics say some things cannot be analyzed without a sense of unveiling. (¶10)
- Orientalists who do not believe in God have written on Islamic mysticism. Their books are "just a pile of information," "many obviously are rubbish," and "there is no knowledge it's all information." (¶10–11)
- Distinction made: information (anyone can talk about it) versus knowledge. (¶11)

### 2.4 Quranic verses on the soul (¶12–23)
- Q 91:7–10. The soul has something that distinguishes good from evil, "not the power to be underestimated." The whole story of human beings is about developing the soul: "the reason why we are here is about developing our souls in a way that we want to fashion it." (¶12–13)
- Q 59:19: "those who forget Allah, He made them forget their own souls," and they are the faasiqoon. "Most of us have forgotten our souls and therefore, we are categorized as transgressors." The mystics say only by remembering God can attention turn to the soul. (¶13)
- Corollary: to remember your soul, remember God. If you forget God you forget your soul and are a transgressor. (¶14)
- Q2 (inaudible). The answer: it is not necessarily punishment. Most Muslims are like this, but they do not realize the potential in their souls. The lecturer cites 12:106 (most believers in Allah are also mushrik), "forgiven by Allah," and says this is "not about reward and punishment," but about "what station we acquire later on with Allah." (¶15–16)
- Istighfar: we commit sins we do not regard as sins, and istighfar compensates for these. On waking after death we will see them. "The most cancer type of sins are the sins that you cannot realize," because what is not recognized cannot be cured. The remedy is remembrance, purification and istighfar. (¶17)
- Q 6:20: "those who have lost their souls will not believe." Seeing your own soul leads to belief. The body is a composition and might be put down to evolution of matter, but "as soon as you see the soul, you see the creator of the soul." The soul cannot come about haphazardly. Theories of chance or evolution arise because people cannot see their souls. (¶18–19)
- Q 6:26: some relatives of the Prophet dissuaded others from following him and did not follow him themselves. They "only destroy their own souls." The lecturer says that reading the Quran can give the impression God never punishes, but most of the time "it is us punishing ourselves." (¶19–20)
- Q 42:30: misfortune is "the product of your own achievement." God protects us from many repercussions and forgives much, "however when you want to destroy your soul and you have free will what Allah can do about it." (¶20–21)
- Hadith of Imam Ali: "Whoever knows his soul knows his lord." Many meanings have been given. It is a two-way relationship: forget Allah, forget your soul, and vice versa. (¶21)
- The soul is held and preserved by Allah, seen "very brightly" in our souls rather than our bodies. There are stages: a limited stage of returning to oneself and believing in a Creator, and a full stage requiring knowledge of oneself and then "zikr of Allah" for full knowledge of the soul. (¶22)
- One meaning of the hadith: the soul is fashioned after God's image, so it is "a perfect way to know how Allah is." The soul has "the spirit of God," but "the spirit of God is not god." It is a created spirit. Allah does not have a spirit or soul like ours. (¶23)

### 2.5 Q&A: can one know the soul yet not know God? Limits of knowledge (¶24–39)
- Q3: Can someone know their soul and not recognize God? A: "No, this hadees denies that." (¶24–25)
- Q4 (Buddhists): the lecturer says he does not know exactly what they believe and declines to discuss it. Because the soul is fashioned after God and has "infinite potential," the more you know it the more you can know God. Mystics warn that one may reach stages of misunderstanding and "get lost completely like we may deny god," so a teacher is needed, which "might just be a Prophet or the Quran." (¶26–27)
- Most mystics say all they say is in the Quran. Ibn Arabi stresses this: "whatever I have found it is in the Quran," with deeper layers of meaning. One could try to know the soul without the Quran's guidance but may get lost. (¶28)
- Analogy: this world is small compared to the world of the soul. We are "bewildered" and make great mistakes even in knowledge of this world. (¶28)
- The purpose of the lecture series is to find the qualities of the soul. It is eternal, so it resembles the eternal God. Some say no one can know the soul, hence no one can know the Lord: "you can never know who you are and therefore, how you want to know your lord." (¶29)
- Q5 (inaudible). A: the depth of knowledge of Allah is unfathomable, as is the soul; we can get "some understanding" but complete knowledge is impossible. (¶30–31)
- Q6 (a student): the statement implies one should attempt understanding. A: the hadith can bear the impossibility reading, "but we have other hades" showing the possibility of knowledge is meant. The soul does not depend on matter, as God does not, so "human soul is a medium to know Allah." (¶32–33)
- Q7 (angels): if one knows an angel, can one know God? A: there is a stage angels cannot pass, a limitation proving they cannot know God. The lecturer paraphrases the student's point that the human soul has no limitation, otherwise it could not know God. (¶34–36)
- Q8/Q9 (Miʿraj): the Prophet went beyond the angels. A: there are levels above where even the Prophet could not go, "limited there." The student objects that the hadith says the only way to know God is through the soul. A: belief in God is not only through the soul; the example is "meeting Allah." (¶37–40)

### 2.6 What does "meeting Allah" mean? (¶40–58)
- Q 6:31: those who deny the meeting with their Lord are losers. Meeting means "we remove the layers that cover our soul"; our soul is "a reflection of Allah." We will see our soul "exactly for what it is with all the veils removed." This is seeing a reflection of God, not God. It happens for the believer and the kaffir alike. (¶40–44)
- Q12–13 (vision; partly inaudible), Q14: what part of us perceives the soul? (¶45–48)
- Key answer (¶49): the mystics, "expounded after Ibne Arabi" and made rational in philosophy by Mulla Sadra, hold that "the perceiver perceives the perception." The soul perceives itself: "the most outstanding example of ilumul huzuri," where perceiver and perceived are the same. We can perceive Allah because we are his creation and a reflection of him. (¶49–50)
- Imam Ali's hadith of the container: every container shrinks when filled except the container of knowledge, "the more you put in, the more the bigger it's become." The soul unifies with its knowledge. To know God one must unify with God and annihilate the limits of one's soul, since God cannot be limited. (¶50–52)
- Hadith: "Do not be ignorant to your soul," since whoever is ignorant of his soul is ignorant of everything; such a person can see only attributes, not the reality. "How one can know other things when they don't know themselves." (¶52–53)
- We need not go anywhere to meet him: "We are actually in his presence at the moment. However, he is not in our presence," as knowledge is present to the knower. One day he will become present to our souls. The believer perceives his mercy and reward, the kaffir his justice, hence jahannam versus jannah. (¶54–55)
- Angels: Q 37:164 ("Not one of us but has a place appointed"). There is a degree of perception the body cannot attain. This is what Musa asked for ("look at me") and was refused. (¶56)
- Perception in the next world is limited by perception in this one; "whoever is completely blind in this world is completely blind in the other world." Keeping faith until the end of life is crucial because stress at death may cause its loss. (¶57–58)
- Q15 (losing faith at the last moment): the lecturer points to Shaytan. Even before Allah the arrogant say "you're wrong, I'm right." The kafferoon swear lies to Allah on the day of judgment, and arrogance in the soul causes their loss. Jinn perceive more than we do. (¶59–60)

### 2.7 The soul as microcosm, and the worlds (¶61–66)
- The soul is a "character sketch" or "compact, zipped source" of the world. Some say the soul is the aalamul asghar (small world) and the cosmos the akbar. Others reverse this. The lecturer's view: the soul contains everything, has identified with it, so it is aalamul akbar compared to the outer world. (¶61)
- Our universe sits inside another world. Anecdote (¶62): a Christian Cardinal asked Imam Ali whether this world is inside the akhirah or the reverse. Answer: this world is inside the akhirah. Cited with Q 9:49 (Hell "surrounds" the unbelievers). (¶62)
- Aalamul shahadah (witnessed/material world) is encompassed by a medium world, aalamul barzakh or mithal, a "dimension to whatever we see." Barzakh means barrier. (¶62–63)
- Above are arwah/ʿuqul (pure intellects, angels, ruh). Matter and intellect cannot meet, so a medium is required. Whatever is in the world of intellect is reflected first in the medium world, which has "some presence of matter": dimensions and colours but no matter. (¶63)
- Evidence from sleep: you see a dead or living person, not in your mind but in your memory; sometimes the dream then comes true. "There is a corresponding person to that person which is another world." On death we take a body in the middle world, with shape and dimension but not bound like matter. (¶64)
- Q 15:29: the breathing of God's spirit into Adam needed a medium, which was the human soul. This brought Adam into the material world. (¶65)
- The middle world "encompasses" the material world non-materially and is "much bigger." The lecturer defers fuller explanation: "I will explain later on." (¶66)

### 2.8 Sleep, death, time, body (¶66–70)
- When we sleep our souls can go to that world and see things. Time there is different; one sees the correspondence of a future event, "which of course has no time." This explains hadith reports that at death many things (people, angels, discussions) happen "in the blink of an eye." (¶66–67)
- In sleep the soul is not fully out of the body, otherwise "you are gone." Besides the five senses, "our heart can perceive things," but constant activity drowns this out; in sleep "nothing else is blocking." (¶68)
- Reversal: "The body is not carrying the soul but the soul is carrying the body." At death "the soul doesn't leave the body, the soul dumps the body." As we age the body becomes more of a burden. (¶68–69)
- Imam Ali: were it not for the appointed time, the soul "would not have lingered in the body" even for a blink; God "made it a prisoner" until then. (¶70)

### 2.9 Muraqabah and visions while awake (¶70–79)
- Question posed: can someone alive lessen the attachment and reach barzakh? Yes, by diverting attention to Allah and becoming unaware of the body. This is the same as in sleep, and is done by muraqabah (later lecture). (¶70–71)
- Allamah Tabatabai: he could move to that world, though not at will. It is only for the Imams to do at will; for mystics "it is just opportunity given by Allah." He said the more muraqabah, the more clearly he saw what others see in dreams. (¶72)
- Example: you dream of Imam Husayn without having seen him. How do you know it is him? Because there are no veils in that world and perception is by the soul (ʿilm huduri). (¶72–74)
- Mithal is glossed as "image." Awake, "I don't mean that your soul comes out of your body," only that attention is diverted. (¶75–76)
- Some mystics use repeated mention of Allah's name and movement; after about 15 minutes the mind goes out. Combined with zikr, one gets "a flavour of what this name is" and "identifies with that name." The lecturer hedges: "We don't have such a practice after immah but this is what they say." (¶76–77)
- Q16: can one be present in two places? A: "It's not that your body is seen there. It is an image which comes through perception." Mystics stress Q 19:17 (the image of a person came to Maryam). Jibril's appearance to the Prophet is explained as the image of Jibril, perceived within the soul, though the Prophet "saw him outside." (¶78–79)

### 2.10 Illusions and dream interpretation (¶80–83)
- Q17 (inaudible). A: perceptions can be illusions, but riwayat say the Prophet and Imams cannot be falsely seen. Other images can be wrong. (¶81)
- Mechanism: when the soul receives a meaning for which it has no image, it attaches it to an existing image. Example: knowledge appears as swimming in an ocean. (¶81–82)
- Prophet Yusuf's interpretation of the seven thin and seven fat cows (famine and cultivation). A silly mind picks weird images, so no one could interpret the dream of Firaun. A knowledgeable interpreter like Yusuf can read the correspondence. (¶82–83)

### 2.11 The hierarchy of worlds restated (¶84–87)
- Mithal is a world of images without bodies. We cannot go to the higher world "without a body with image," even in jannah we have images. The imageless world is arwah. (¶84)
- Encompassing ladder: mulk, then mithal, then ʿuqul (angels, arwah, pure intellect, living and knowing), then the kursi (Q 2:255). Hadith: the heavens and earth compared to the kursi are "like a small ring," and the kursi compared to the ʿarsh likewise. Then the ʿarsh is encompassed by the jabarut. (¶85–86)
- The soul corresponds to three worlds. "Me and you" in mulk have a correspondence in mithal, "much bigger," found "only by reflection inside." Another correspondence exists in ʿuqul. For the Prophet, the first thing Allah created was noor, his correspondence in ʿuqul. In that world "you can expand more and more by gaining knowledge," so "human soul is limitless." (¶86–87)

### 2.12 Further Q&A (¶88–96)
- Q18 (inaudible). A: the Platonic world of perfect forms "corresponds to aalamul mithqal." The lecturer says Plato held that everything here has a perfect copy there, and that when we perceive something we match it to what exists in mithal. (¶89)
- Q19 (inaudible). A: do not underestimate the body. The body is a materialization of the soul, which is why we look different, and the image of the soul in the other world resembles the body here. Souls and bodies can be matched on the day of judgment, "might get better, brighter, or... worse." (¶91–92)
- Q20: how does Allah communicate with Jibril? A: "through perception and knowledge actually being induced to Jibreal or something like that," a tentative answer. (¶93–94)
- Q21: can we meet the Imams in the world of intellect? A: "almost impossible in this world"; only Prophets and Imams. Mystics "usually cannot go beyond aalamul mithqal." After death it is possible. (¶95–96)
