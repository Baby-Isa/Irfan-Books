# Lecture 3: How Knowledge Arises, and What 'Irfan Seeks to Know

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan03.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture, opening from a hand-out (Chodkiewicz's analysis of Ibn ʿArabi's al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya) on types of human knowledge, argues that knowledge is created in the human being by God, even when it travels through physical means (¶3, ¶9). Ordinary knowledge passes mind to mind through language and the body, but the knowledge sought in ʿirfan comes heart to heart, as light God places in a purified heart, reached through suluk rather than fikr (¶10, ¶18–21). Philosophy and science analyse, limit and divide things into accidents and characteristics; they cannot reach the essence (haqq/wujud) of anything, let alone God (¶25–27, ¶35). Ibn Sina is cited as admitting this limit, and Mulla Sadra as offering knowledge by presence in the soul as the only route (¶23–28). ʿIrfan's subject matter is God and His attributes, aiming at a positive rather than negative knowledge of Him (¶38). The Q&A covers motivation, guidance, Satan and the meaning of sounds or names (¶44–62). It builds on lecture 2's two types of knowledge (¶3) and promises next week's treatment of the soul (¶60).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 The hand-out and the two types of knowledge (¶3)
- The hand-out is from al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya of Ibn ʿArabi, as analysed by Michel Chodkiewicz; it is "very beautiful on types of human knowledge" (¶3).
- Recap of last week: two types, one from intellect and reasoning, the other from inside ("a type of aqidah, visualising of the soul"). What the soul captures is always knowledge by presence; what the mind captures is both presence and acquired knowledge (¶3, as transcribed; see §7).

### 2.2 How knowledge is transferred: an analysis of communication (¶4–9)
- The lecturer asks if we have ever thought how knowledge comes about. Example: "God is imageless". Even a superficial knowledge of this has entered the listener's mind. Likewise Quranic verses sometimes shake the hearer (¶4).
- Mechanism: I hold an image (of a concept, a being or a visible thing). From the heart I make a decision to convey it. I order the nervous system to produce something corresponding to the image, which goes to the vocal cord. The sound travels through the listener's nervous system to the mind and creates an image there (¶5–6).
- Puzzle: how can a physical medium produce something in the mind that is not physical? "Even the image of a tree in your mind is not physical" (¶7). The image cannot be on one small cell. Cells are separate, and the image is received across many cells or parts of a cell, so there must be something else that captures and unifies it (¶7–8).
- Conclusion: the physical image I created has given rise to a spiritual image in your mind, "because knowledge is not physical" (¶8).
- Islam teaches that knowledge comes only from Allah, who creates it in you through many means. It is not as simple as "I place the knowledge" (¶9). Two types are restated: acquired knowledge and knowledge with presence (¶9).

### 2.3 Heart-to-heart knowledge (¶10–11)
- We have experience of mind-to-mind transfer but not heart-to-heart (¶10).
- Imam Ali (as) reports that at the Prophet's deathbed the Prophet taught him a thousand doors of knowledge, each opening a thousand more. It "didn't come from language"; it went "from heart to heart". This is the knowledge of the ʿurafaʾ, coming from the heart and not from mind to mouth (¶10).
- The ʿurafaʾ say receiving directly from God to the heart is possible but very difficult; usually one needs an intermediary. In Shia belief all knowledge of God comes through the imam, even if we do not realise it (¶11).
- Reading the article is urged: we imagine our knowledge is just bits gathered and put together, but it is "completely monopolized by Allah" (¶11).

### 2.4 Divine names as models: Khaliq, Bari, Musawwir (¶12–17)
- Q 59:24 is recited ("Huwal Laahul Khaaliqul Baari'ul Musawwir...") (¶12).
- Khaliq: composing things and creating one thing from another. We too are khaliq, "a gift of god". God makes the soul from nothing (¶12).
- Exegesis, especially of the ʿurafaʾ, links Q 2:31 (teaching Adam the names) to God putting "a flavour of all his abilities" in us (¶13).
- Bari: decreeing things out of nothing. We are bari too, as we create something in the mind from nothing (¶13). A hadith says Allah created Adam after His own image, so Adam can do in a certain way what Allah does, as we will see in Janna. Other aspects of God are completely different and no mind can think of them (¶13–14).
- Another hadith: whoever knows his soul knows Allah. One meaning is that the soul is created after His image, though we are servants and "nothing", "just like an imagination in the mind so to speak" (¶14).
- Musawwir: giving shape and colour. Our imagination only recombines what we have seen, for example a horse with wings, or a "new" colour made from seen colours. Only He is Musawwir, creating all images from nothing (¶15).
- A hadith in Nahj al-Balagha says there was no prior experience before God fashioned things. Bari means maker; Musawwir means giving shapes, colours and images to what was made from nothing (¶16).
- A hadith is cited that "you obey me, I will make you like musawwir". The lecturer reads this as in human capacity, not a claim on the Day of Judgment to be Musawwir (¶16–17). Few reach that level. Powers are given only to those who are not arrogant, and not to those who "always make evil images" (¶17).

### 2.5 Purity (Quddus) and knowledge as light (¶18–21)
- God is so pure (al-Malik al-Quddus) that He cannot accept impurity in the soul. After a sin there is an awakening. We seek a place of purity and cleanness, which is difficult (¶18).
- Hadith: knowledge is not through extensive learning but is "the light that God sends in the heart of whomever He wishes to guide" (¶18).
- Hadith: knowledge consists of three things: the firm sign, the just duty and the established sunnah; all else is superfluous (¶19).
- Light versus darkness, with a reference to a verse of light for one who understands (¶19).
- ʿIrfan knowledge "is not something that you can read anywhere", which is why it comes through suluk (¶19).
- Two routes: university or hawza knowledge comes through fikr (think, assemble, analyse). Irfanic knowledge comes through suluk, a path of purification: "You purify yourself and then wait for Allah to put the light in your heart" (¶20).
- Q 24:35: the light is everywhere, but the heart is closed. Suluk opens it. God does not pick people to give light; His light is everywhere and "He is with you wherever you are" (¶20–21).
- The ʿurafaʾ look for a way to open the heart. There is no hindrance from God's side in pouring out His knowledge and blessings (¶21).

### 2.6 ʿIlm and maʿrifa (¶21–23)
- Recall the distinction from before. In religious terminology, when the ʿurafaʾ say ʿilm they mean maʿrifa; they do not count other types as real ʿilm (¶21–22).
- People are of two kinds: one who has maʿrifa and one who tries to learn it. The imams and prophets are "aalemul ulema" (¶22).
- Acquired knowledge (idrak) works with time, space and limits: you must limit, divide and analyse. Maʿrifa is arrival (the transcript says "usooli"; see §7): "you just find and arrive", through inner self-purification. If you put limits on God you cannot find Him (¶22–23).

### 2.7 Ibn Sina, Abu Saʿid Abu l-Khayr and the limit of the mind (¶23–27)
- Ibn Sina, master of philosophy, became towards the end of his life a master of ʿirfan after meeting Abu Saʿid Abu l-Khayr. He wrote asking to learn from him (¶23).
- The lecturer stresses his genius: at 15–16 no philosopher could compete (¶24). Ibn Sina was not an ʿarif but, through a visit and correspondence, wrote the irfanic part of al-Isharat wa l-Tanbihat. It is taught by most ʿurafaʾ in Qum, who say no chapter is better (¶24).
- His point: knowing the thing itself is beyond human capacity. We know only characteristics, colours, dimensions and functions, which are accidents attached to it, "but you don't really know what is the thing which all these come upon" (¶25).
- Q 29:44 (created the heavens and the earth bi l-haqq): the mystics discuss at length what this haqq is (¶25).
- Haqq is the reality of everything, wujud. It has no dimension or colour and cannot be grasped by mind. So we cannot know God's essence, nor the essence of anything (¶26).
- Question posed: "is it that we don't know or we can't know?" Ibn Sina's answer: we cannot, because the mind analyses and God has no dimension or colour (¶26–27).

### 2.8 Mulla Sadra and knowledge by presence (¶28–32)
- Mulla Sadra changed Islamic philosophy "from hikmatul mashaal to hikmatul mutaaal" (¶28).
- Reality can be known only by the inner faculty, the soul, through knowledge by presence. We can argue that God exists but not what He is (¶28).
- Wujud is a manifestation of God. It is "utter nonsense" to think you are God or a part of Him. Existence is known only through its characteristics; its reality is known only by the soul (¶29).
- "The eyes cannot catch him. It is the heart which gives reality to faith." Faith is the only way; for us it is a "much diluted type of knowledge", like weak eyes that need spectacles to see colour (¶29–30).
- Some people will look at Allah on the Day of Judgment. Munajat of the Lovers (Muhibbin) asks for the favour of looking at Him (¶30).
- A weaker form of this knowledge is placed in everything. On the Day of Judgment all knowledge grows stronger, so everything speaks in its own way. Even a leaf has a weak knowledge of God (¶30–31).
- Nahj al-Balagha story: the disbelievers demanded a miracle, a tree testifying to the Prophet's prophethood. Imam Ali (as) witnessed the trees reciting the shahada (¶31–32). It is easy for God to make everything speak. Those on suluk will "see numerous miracles" (¶33).

### 2.9 Positive and negative knowledge of God (¶33–34)
- The prophets are God's khalifa. They do not seek to prove God but to have real experience and knowledge of Him, a positive knowledge (¶33).
- What we know is negative: God is not an object, not in a place. "But what Allah is?" (¶33).
- Only the soul can grasp that: "Man ʿarafa nafsahu ʿarafa rabbah." It has degrees; "the more you know, the more bewildered you will become" (¶34). Mystics seem like mad people, absorbed in the greatness (¶34).
- Heart and soul differ; deferred to a later lecture (¶34–35).

### 2.10 Subject matter, principles, questions (¶35–41)
- Philosophy and science analyse, limit and divide into units and accidents. ʿIrfan seeks God in His unity (¶35).
- Every science has three parts: subject, principles and questions (¶35–36).
- Subject of a science is that which is studied with its immediate accidents. An accident could have been otherwise (a colour being brown); an essence is essential. Questions concern what other accidents can be given. Principles are self-evident or prior premises (¶36–37).
- Subject of ʿirfan: God and His attributes (a sentence of Ibn ʿArabi's, per the lecturer). It aims at primordial attributes, positive theology (¶38).
- Puzzle: how can God be Rahman and Rahim without moving or being affected? How can He forgive if there must be a sin that motivates Him? Theology works hard at this; ʿirfan has its own method, unveiling and illumination of the heart, not analysis (¶38–39).
- Principles in ʿirfan are the prophets' immediate knowledge and experience of God, accepted as axioms (¶40). Questions are rich and hard: how diversity comes from unity, returns to unity, how manifestations of the names create the world, and the way towards God (¶40–41).
- A khutba of Imam Ali (as), not in Nahj al-Balagha but with similar meaning there, is said to be reported by al-Mansuri; the lecturer glosses it with God being manifest and hidden, "everywhere, but still you see nowhere" (¶41–42).
- Objection: obeying is enough, so why strain to know what God is? (¶42–43).

### 2.12 Nahj al-Balagha passage on God (¶59–60)
- A passage is read: a being but not through coming into being; with everything but not in physical nearness; different from all but not physically separate; acts without movement or instruments; sees when nothing is there to be seen; unique; creates without reflection, experiment or movement (¶59).
- "Can you say you have knowledge of this? No." It is for the soul and the heart. Next week: what the soul is and how knowledge is possible without the mind's mediation (¶60).

## 6. Q&A from the audience
- (¶44–45) Q1 not audible. A: not everyone has a question. Pursuing this needs strength to drop immediate pleasures and a drive born of love.
- (¶46–49) Q2 not audible. A: the drive is placed in the heart; then awakening and tawba. He does not choose unless He loves. First polish the soul by following what the Prophet demanded; no maʿrifa without this. Do not ask a mystic for advice: if God cannot guide you no one can (Q 30:29 as cited); respond to God and the Messenger (Q 8:24); correct your soul and wait.
- (¶50–52) Q3 not audible. A: I can put an image or guidance in your mind or heart; guidance always from Allah, the rest from shaytan. It finds those who are prepared. Q 43:36 on the one who turns from remembrance. Remembrance drives shaytan back; zikr polishes the heart.
- (¶53–55) Q4 not audible (apparently about jinn and shaytans around us). A: they are all around us, hard to see; there is also Allah's mercy, with angels calling for repentance on Jumʿa night.
- (¶56–58) Q5 not audible. A: Q 21:18, haq crushes batil. Shaytan cried out when haq came. Our himma is weak; with effort we would see how strong the side of Allah is.
- (¶61–62) Q6 not audible, apparently about sounds or names. A: two meanings: they invoke a concept in the mind; and they reflect the attributes, flavour and essence of Allah, which mystics stress.
