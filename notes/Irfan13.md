# Lecture 13: Mysticism of Gnosis (Maʿrifa): Hakim Tirmidhi, Bayazid and Junayd, Ibn Arabi and Wahdat al-Wujud

<!-- Detailed notes from transcripts/text/Irfan13.md. Every point cites ¶. -->

## 1. Thesis
The lecture completes the series' survey of the four inclinations in early mysticism (fear, renunciation, love, gnosis) by treating the mysticism of maʿrifa through three figures. First is Hakim Tirmidhi, who brings Hellenistic and Platonic ideas into Sufism and introduces the "seal of the saints" and the hierarchy of saints. Second is the pair Bayazid Bastami (intoxication, fana) and Junayd Baghdadi (sobriety, baqa), who represent two paths. Third is Ibn Arabi, the culmination of theoretical mysticism, whose life, claimed inspiration, influence, Shia/Sunni ambiguity and controversy are discussed (¶3–55). The lecture closes with a long, careful account of Wahdat al-Wujud, set against rival theories of existence, with the claim that it can only be grasped by experience, not reason (¶55–67). The lecturer presents the material descriptively and cautiously ("it is not for us to judge"), and says the topics will be picked up again later (Sufi tariqas, ¶34).

## 2. Detailed content (in lecture order)

### 2.1 Recap: four types of mysticism; the gnostic type (¶3)
- Four inclinations: mysticism of fear, of renunciation, of love, and of gnosis (maʿrifa, "knowledge"). Three have been covered with their main proponents (¶3).
- Gnostics place "more emphasis on knowledge than love or renunciation". There are "many, many" such Sufis; only the most prominent will be mentioned (¶3).

### 2.2 Hakim Tirmidhi (¶4–12)
- Al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi, a third-century mystic, to be distinguished from the author of "Sahih al-Tirmidhi" (¶4).
- "Hakim" means philosopher, so he was a "mystic philosopher". Through him "some Hellenistic philosophical ideas penetrated into Sufism". Theoretical mysticism contains many neo-Platonic ideas that came through Sufis who knew Greek philosophy, "especially Plato" (¶4–5).
- His main contribution is the "seal of the saints" (Khatm al-Awliya), mentioned "very roughly" by him and taken up "very elaborately" by Ibn Arabi: as there is a seal of the prophets, there is a seal of the saints (¶5).
- Ibn Arabi did not identify the Seal with the Imam of the Age or with the "seal of the legatees" (khatm al-awsiya); he regarded himself as the Seal, "maybe it was some sort of illusion or whatever". The lecturer: "We cannot judge what he meant by this" (¶5–6).
- From this idea comes the pole (the lecture says "quds"), the "pivot or pillar of the mystic knowledge that should exist at every age". Every Sufi tariqa has one, "a pole to which everyone should rely and to which everyone should turn". The idea appears very early, in the third century, in Tirmidhi's "Seal of the Saints"; the pole heads the Sufi hierarchy and "governs the universe" (¶6).
- Comparison with Shia belief: it is "very close" to the Shia idea that the Imams have wilayat takwini (power over the world of creation) as well as wilayat tashriʿi (the world of religion). The lecturer says some Shia ideas were influenced by extremist views transmitted via Sunnis. "It's amazing that they thought about the saints in a way that we even don't think about our Imam": the saint is God's vicegerent and "can do whatever he wants" (¶7).
- Microcosm and macrocosm: the saint is the "bigger world" and the universe the "smaller world", "or vice versa, depending how they interpreted these two terms" (¶8).
- Hierarchy: groups of saints (a number given as "374 or 300", garbled) are entrusted with maintaining world order. There is a ghawth, then awtad ("pillars of the earth", three or seven), then abdal (40 or 300); without them "the world will perish" (¶8).
- Assessment: the same ideas as about the Imams "but they take it to very extreme" levels "not even acceptable by Shia"; "Even we do not regard some of these things for our Aimmah or for the Prophet". The Sufis were influenced by Shia ideas, pushed them to extremes, and when brought back to the Shia world look extreme. Different saints each thought themselves "the saint of all the saints" (¶9).
- His degrees of the saints follow illumination and gnosis, "not the hierarchy of love". In the mysticism of love, more love means nearer to Allah; here more knowledge means nearer (¶10).
- Reconciliation offered by some: love comes from maʿrifa; the more you know God, the more love you feel, the more ignorant, the less love. "So love is the outcome of maʿrifa", so the two schools can come close (¶10–11).
- Gnosis versus agnosis: "agnostic" derives from the opposite of gnosis, those who say "we cannot find any knowledge about God". This view, the lecturer says, appeared at the start of discussions of God in philosophy and mysticism and prepared the way for later speculation (¶11).
- Recalling an earlier lecture: mystics distinguish maʿrifa (knowledge of God) from ʿilm (knowledge of the universe). Maʿrifa is gained "through purification", ʿilm "through study and experience". This is "not a Quranic type of terminology" but comes from the Sufis, originating with Tirmidhi (¶11–12).
- Tirmidhi was "a turning point" in bringing theorization into mysticism; many ideas of Ibn Arabi, "the culmination of mystical civilization", came from him "as well as others" (¶12).

### 2.3 Two types: Bayazid (sukr) and Junayd (sobriety) (¶13–32)
- The two most famous third-century mystics, representing two types of mysticism that shaped later mysticism: Abu Yazid al-Bistami (Bayazid Bastami, the "easier pronunciation") and Junayd al-Baghdadi, the "culmination" of mysticism of that period, "after which mysticism took a different path" (¶13).
- The two types: mysticism of intoxication (sukr) and of "sovereignty" (see section 7) (¶13).
- Intoxication: wanting to "lose themselves in God", seeing nothing but God and speaking of Him not themselves; "intoxicated with this pleasant feeling of being annihilated in God". Persian poetry is full of "mystic intoxication": leave all bonds and attachments and get the "nice feeling of forgetting everything, but of course here for Allah" (¶13–14).
- Mystics like Junayd say this is "only a transient experience" (see section 7). Humans are inclined towards it: "we like to lose everything, and to only have the feeling of God" (¶14).
- Mansur Hallaj represents the school: executed for "very unacceptable statements" about himself and his relation to God; the role model of those who want to "sacrifice everything, offer everything for Allah". More advanced mystics like Junayd regard such people "as children", limited to the "beginning steps" of experience (¶15).
- Bayazid: born in Bastam, a small city in northern Iran. "Iran was a hotbed of these mystical experiences." He first formulated fana (annihilation) in Islamic mysticism, c. the late second to mid third century (¶16).
- On zuhd: asked what renunciation is, he said it "has no value because I am after intoxication". "I was three days in renunciation, on the fourth day I have finished." Day one he renounced this world, day two the other world, day three everything save God. On the fourth day "nothing was left to me but God"; a voice said "you are not strong enough to endure with me alone"; he replied "that is exactly what I want"; the voice said "you have found" (¶16–18).
- The lecturer's gloss: renouncing the other world means desiring nothing but Allah. He links it to a statement of Imam Sajjad in the Sahifa Sajjadiyya, about being unable to bear hell but deserving it ("Oh, God, if I had the capacity to be burnt into your hell..."). The lecture says this is "exactly the same thing". Such loneliness "not everyone can endure" (¶17–18).
- Shathiyyat: sayings of Sufis in ecstasy, "theopathic statements", "something which states about God which are not right... unhealthy" but arising from excitement and ecstasy. Bayazid's famous one: "Praise is to me, how great my majesty is", which "means I am God, isn't it?" (¶19–20).
- Two explanations. (1) Allah was speaking from his tongue, as from the tree of Musa ("Inni ana Allah", 28:30); "the tree was not Allah". This "best explanation... exonerates these Sufis". (2) Rejected by many mystics who had the same experiences: he lost sight of himself, saw only God, and spoke "when you see the glory of Allah while you have been removed out of the way, there is no you anymore". The lecturer calls this the "best way" to explain shathiyyat; it comes through mortification and emptying the self until, "at least for a moment", absolute unity (¶20–22).
- Later Sufis do not accept this unity as wrong reading of experience, i.e. "lover, beloved and love are one". Attar was "very much influenced" by Bayazid and believed this (¶23).
- Attar's Conference of the Birds (Mantiq al-tayr): birds meet to find the greatest bird, the Simorgh (a mythical Persian bird, "the glory of the birds"); many refuse the long journey; some return or die; at the end only thirty birds arrive and find nothing; "Simorgh" means thirty (si) birds (morgh), so "they themselves are the Simorgh". "This is exactly what Bayazid and mystic were teaching" (¶23–25).
- Junayd was Bayazid's student (stated in ¶25; see ¶7 on chronology) and "of course the student was bigger than the teacher". He held that Bayazid had not reached the final goal; intoxication belongs to the beginning of the path; there were "lots of other stations". Bayazid had arrived only "at the threshold of the Divine" (the attribution to Hallaj here is unclear, see section 7). The same is said of Hallaj. Bayazid's name occurs more in the literature than any other mystic's except Hallaj (¶25–26).
- Junayd recalls the earlier model of two journeys: the first ends in annihilation, then a second journey brings baqa after fana, "existence after annihilation". "He moved further than that intoxicated ecstasy." Annihilation "is not the ultimate goal but baqa... a new life in God" (¶27, ¶29).
- Biography: Iranian, born in Nahavand "very close to my birthplace, Kermanshah", settled in Baghdad (hence al-Baghdadi); raised by his maternal uncle Sari al-Saqati, "a great mystic himself"; disciple of al-Muhasibi (covered earlier) (¶27–28).
- Titles: "Sultan" and Sayyid al-Taifa among the ʿurafa, "like the jurists call him [Tusi] Shaykh al-Taifa"; "the biggest among the Sufis" (¶28).
- Sobriety versus intoxication: Bayazid preferred intoxication as it "obliterates the human attributes". Junayd considered the second sobriety "the highest and preferred state": after intoxication man becomes more aware of himself in the life in God, with attributes restored (the text is garbled, see section 7) (¶28).
- Analogy of the explorer: someone travels thousands of kilometres and, finding a river, everyone thinks it is the end of the world; better equipped and stronger travellers go further and find "many, many other things you haven't seen beyond the river". Bayazid stopped at the river; Junayd was "probably the first" to go on (¶29).
- Junayd criticised Hallaj for speaking openly of experience, and developed a language of allusion (ishara) unintelligible to outsiders, which became "a custom among old mystics". Reason: "if they reveal their experiences ordinary people cannot tolerate it" (¶30).
- Hadith: "if Abu Dharr knew what is in the heart of Salman he would have said he's a kafir"; Salman's status in knowledge was very high; people's concept of God "cannot digest the various stages" (¶30–31).
- Hafiz's verse: "our friend Hallaj was under barrels [i.e. on the gallows] because he revealed the secret" (loose paraphrase, see section 7). People's understanding of God "is very limited" (¶31).
- Analogy of the child: "It's just like confusing a child... You shouldn't confuse the child. And these mystics shouldn't confuse us about God... You don't understand it even if they say, so what's the point". Junayd said Hallaj deserved denunciation for revealing secrets (¶32).
- Junayd died in 298 (AH, implied); born near the end of the era of the Imams, so "his main fruitful life was after the ghayba of Imam-Asr" (¶32).

### 2.4 Ibn Arabi: life and miracles (¶33–41)
- Ash-Shaykh al-Akbar Muhyiddin Ibn Arabi: his huge output is "very confusing"; he is the "Shaykh al-Akbar for both Shia and Sunni mystics", the culmination of theoretical mysticism; he "claims experiences which are difficult to think that a human being could have had". Only some aspects of his life are touched on (¶33).
- Abd al-Qadir Jilani (head of the "Jilaniya" tariqa, to be covered when the lecture reaches tariqas) saw the child and found a "very powerful type of understanding of the other world" in him, and told his father to look after him. Muhyiddin means "the one who revives the religion". He signed notes as Abu Abdullah (¶33–34). (The lecture's wording is unclear on the chain of events, see section 7.)
- Story of Surah Yasin (see section 5): at 12, in a coma, he sees horrible beings attacking and a figure breathing perfume who says "I am Surah Yaseen, your protector"; he wakes to find his father in tears who had just recited Surah Yasin (¶35–36).
- Lesson: "anything that we do here has an image in aalam al-barzakh". Words (a lie, consoling someone) have a shape in barzakh according to how they are formed; this is "approved by lots of riwayat and also by the Quran" (the lecture cites no particular text). These images "are real things" (¶36–37).
- His writing "was completely moving from this world to barzakh and coming back". He claims to have seen all the prophets living in barzakh and the Prophet giving him a book to write "full of hikma" (¶37–38).
- On differences in disposition: ordinary people cannot move freely to barzakh. Some see true dreams that come quickly, others none, because "some are more connected to aalam al-barzakh and some less". It is "not a merit... because it's something which is part of our nature" (¶38–39).
- His claim to inspiration: "I do not write on my own accord. These are what I am inspired to write." This makes enemies: in Iran he is "the most loved among some and the most hated amongst some" (¶39–40).
- He says his inspirations are only understandings from Quranic verses given by Allah, and he experiences their tawil (¶40–41).
- Al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya ("Meccan illuminations", written in Mecca): an encyclopaedic work founded on the Quran and Islamic wisdom "from the perspective of the stages of the mystic path". The mystics call the highest experiences the "opening of the heart" or "victories" (futuh) or "illumination". Parts of the book were given to him by prophets, and many rely on it because it matches their own experiences (¶41).

### 2.5 Ibn Arabi: influence (¶42–47, ¶51)
- The theory of the names and attributes of Allah (asmaʾ) "originated from Ibn Arabi"; the Imams' hadith have the material, "but no one had experienced and theorized" it (¶42).
- Personal anecdote: the lecturer read a book by Allama Tabatabai on the divine names and was amazed, then read the Futuhat and found Tabatabai "had taken all those from Ibn Arabi". Tabatabai "was very much fond of Ibn Arabi" and acknowledged this; Hasanzadeh Amuli reports Tabatabai saying he learnt it from Ibn Arabi. Tabatabai's own experiences "matched very much" Ibn Arabi's. "It's not for us to judge because it needs someone who has the experience" (¶42–43).
- Fusus al-Hikam ("The Bezels of Wisdom"): received directly from the Prophet, who appeared in a dream in Damascus and said "this is the book to be taken to the people". He woke with the whole book in his heart and wrote it. Read cold, "you don't understand anything", a "secret language" for those with experience and prior knowledge (¶43–44).
- Over 800 books, by Osman Yahia's bibliography in two French volumes; a 90-volume Quran commentary "not published... we cannot find it now" (¶44–45).
- Influence: Iranian mysticism today, Tabatabai, Hasanzadeh and their teachers; Rumi (Masnavi) via Sadr al-Din Qunawi; Ala al-Dawla Simnani (a critic who admits being influenced); Nurbakhshi and Nimatullahi order founders; Sayyid Haydar Amuli; philosophers Mulla Sadra (whose Hikma Mutaʿaliya "rationalizes" Ibn Arabi's theoretical irfan), Mulla Ali Nuri, Hadi Sabzawari, and Imam Khomeini; Ismaili Shia, who rely on his Quran commentary (¶45–47, ¶51).
- Imam Khomeini's letter to Gorbachev urged Russians to "know about Ibn Arabi and know the true face of Islam". After it, many articles attacked Ibn Arabi and Khomeini (¶51–52).

### 2.6 Ibn Arabi: Shia or Sunni? Controversy (¶47–55)
- "It is difficult to say that he was a Shia", and if Sunni, "he had great respect for the Imams and for Abu Bakr and Omar as well". The lecturer's conclusion: mystical experience concerns "the World of God and the world of human beings", not Shia/Sunni truth; "This Shia Sunni business should only be solved by rational arguments rather than by experiences". One cannot argue that a mystic with miracles proves his school right (¶47–48).
- Critics' point: in the Futuhat the "Rajabiyun" (those who fast all of Rajab) said they saw Shias "in form of pigs". A Mashhad scholar criticised Tabatabai and Hasanzadeh for following him. Lecturer's reply: perhaps they were sinful Shias; "it was not because they were Shias". He respected Ahl al-Bayt and Imam Asr and believed in Imam Ali; but critics say he talks mostly of khatm al-awliya and khatm al-awsiya and "more about himself than others" (¶48–49).
- Wilayah is confusing in his work: mystics see it "not only as wilayah of aimmah" but as wilayah of the mystics (zuhd, as transcribed). "I don't know... it's very difficult to judge" (¶50).
- Why Shia mystics keep using him: his theory matches many mystics' experiences and "they found no better language", despite the insult to Shia ideas and Sunni objections. Ibn Taymiyya attacked him as "destroying the religion". Some say he was influenced by Shia (¶50–51).
- His vision of Abu Bakr at God's right hand, Omar at the left and Ali lower is "against Shia thoughts". Lecturer's answer: "you cannot 100% accept someone or reject"; he "had something valuable"; visions are construed according to one's theology and what was seen may not be in the order recalled (¶52–53).
- Titles: friends call him al-Wali al-Kamil and Qutb al-Aqtab; opponents "Mumit al-Din" (killer of the faith) and "Mahi al-Din" (effacer) instead of Muhyi al-Din (¶53).
- Two books: a two-volume Shia-leaning work by a student of Tabatabai (title as transcribed "Al Kalaamul Mateen Fi Tasshayyo Muhyuddin"), arguing Ibn Arabi was Shia; and another proving he was a "very prejudiced Sunni". Each uses different parts of his works. The Sunni-side argument "was much much more powerful", the Shia-side one "very weak": "we shouldn't be unjust" (¶54–55).

### 2.7 Wahdat al-Wujud (¶55–67)
- Ibn Arabi theorized it; others had only "theosophical statements" or allusions (¶55).
- Four views of existence, as the lecture presents them:
  - Kathrat al-wujud wal-mawjud (plurality of existence and existents): the peripatetics (Mashshaʾi); God, universe, sun, earth, animals are all separate beings with their own existence. Lecturer: "difficult to understand how one being can be actually outside" (¶56–57).
  - Unity of existence and plurality of quiddities (wahdat al-wujud, kathrat al-mawjud, or mahiyyat): attributed to the "illuminationists" (Ishraqi). One existence, God; others are mahiyyat who take existence from it. Contrast with the first: there, God has "one big existence" and creates other independent existences; here "there is no other existence" (¶57–58).
  - Wahdat al-wujud wal-mawjud with gradation (tashkik): Mulla Sadra and the pre-Islamic Persian philosophers (Pahlaviyyun). One existence "has ranks from strong to weak". Parable of light: a big sunlight and a small light, "all of them are light"; "one light that has been graded" (¶59–60).
  - The mystics' theory (Ibn Arabi): "there is only the existence and all the existent beings are but different manifestations, states and specifications" (¶60).
- A question to the audience: when God created, where is the creation? Outside God? A new existence? Or "a type of manifestation of what is in the world of God"? "You cannot say that there's anything besides God." This differs from Platonic theory, where God is "the sum total of whatever exists" (so the cycle ends when all creation is added up) (¶60–62).
- Ibn Arabi: "whatever you see, is God, is not God". "It doesn't mean that we do not exist at all." It can be understood only through experience. Mulla Sadra tried to rationalise it and came closest through tashkik, "the dependent aspects of one big existence" (¶62).
- Creation account: Allah, the eternal creator, "created" beings with limits (as essences in a state "between being and not being", like humans, angels, an organised world); then gave them existence from His eternal existence. "I'm not God or a part of God" because of those limits; "I have gained existence from him" (¶63–64).
- This is the meaning of "la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah". Only those like Hallaj or Bayazid, who see "nothing but God", understand it; everything else is "limitation, manifestation, specifications" given to a weak existence (¶64).
- Imam Ali's words (loosely cited): "He is with everything but doesn't need anything. He is different from everything but not away from anything"; and the verse "Huwa al-Awwal wa al-Akhir wa al-Zahir wa al-Batin" (the manifest and the hidden): "You see His manifestation but he's not him". The lecturer says this was "in the Quran" and Nahj al-Balagha as a statement, but not as a theory until Ibn Arabi; afterward they said it referred to Wahdat al-Wujud (¶64–66).
- Manifestation is "not illusion. It is more real than you think... not separate from God but still it is not God". The mind "is not set to understand"; only "hard experience" (¶66).
- Closing: "you can never rationalise this theory. It is above the rank of reason"; even the human soul as a reflection of God's attributes is neither "same" nor "different" (¶67).
- Closing salawat (¶68).
