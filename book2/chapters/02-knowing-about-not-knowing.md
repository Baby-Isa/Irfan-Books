On a day of fasting in Ramadan, by late afternoon, no one has to be told that they are hungry. They do not read it in a book or work it out from the clock. The hunger is known from the inside. Sheikh Bahmanpour uses hunger to describe a kind of knowing that differs from the kind gained by study.

Most of what we know of God is of the kind gained by study. We learn the rulings of fiqh, the beliefs the scholars set out, and the history of the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as); all of it is knowledge about God. Chapter One argued that the Qur'an invites more than the minimum, and that the aim is to know and love God. The 'knowing' it refers to is the kind of knowing that a fasting person has of their hunger: knowing God directly, and not only knowing about Him.

::: {.words}
*ʿIlm*: knowledge, especially knowledge gained by study · *ʿilm al-husuli*: acquired knowledge · *ʿilm al-huduri*: knowledge by presence · *ʿaql*: the reasoning intellect · *suluk*: wayfaring; the path of purification
:::

## Knowing about, and knowing

::: {.point}
Acquired knowledge comes from outside and works by defining, which is limiting. Knowledge by presence is already with the soul; it is arrived at, not worked out, and no argument can remove it. The ʿarif's knowledge of God is knowledge by presence.
:::

Let's start with the two kinds of knowing. The first is acquired knowledge, *ʿilm al-husuli*. It reaches us from outside, through the senses or by reasoning. We form concepts of the things around us and then analyse those concepts. Almost everything we learn from teachers and books is of this kind.

Acquired knowledge works by defining its object. To understand a thing, we sort it into a category and mark where it ends and something else begins. The Arabic for a definition, *hadd*, also means a limit: to define a thing is to limit it. The old definition of a human being as "the rational animal" works by drawing a line round us. Knowledge of this kind belongs to the realm of time and space.

The second kind is knowledge by presence, *ʿilm al-huduri*. It does not come from outside and needs no concept to carry it; it is with the soul already. The plainest case is our knowledge of our own self. Hunger, the example this chapter opened with, is another, and so are pain and anxiety. Anxiety has a cause outside us, yet the feeling arises inside the soul, and the cause is not the feeling.

Knowledge by presence is complete in a way that acquired knowledge is not. When the body is hungry, the soul knows it at once and without effort, and nothing of the hunger is missed. No one can argue a hungry person out of feeling hungry, and no one needs to argue them into it. The Sheikh even allows for a false hunger, felt when the body has eaten enough. Its cause can mislead, but the one who feels it cannot be mistaken about the feeling. Any error lies in what is concluded from it.

This second kind of knowing is what the mystics are after. Sheikh Bahmanpour defines mysticism as the urge to reach out to the infinite through knowledge by presence. The two kinds of knowing aim at different things: *ʿilm* aims at understanding, and *maʿrifah* aims at arriving. A person does not try to understand their hunger; they arrive at it and feel it. In the same way, the mystic's aim is to arrive at God and not to define Him, since a God who has been defined has been limited.

The two words are kept apart here only as a working distinction. The religious texts use *ʿilm* and *maʿrifah* for the same thing, and when they praise knowledge they mean the second kind. Separating the words simply makes the difference between the two kinds of knowing easier to see.

The great teachers of the tradition draw the same line. Imam Khomeini records the view that a gnostic of God "knows God through direct witnessing", while a scholar of God "attains the knowledge of God through philosophical proofs" [@forty_hadith, p. 542]. For Murtada Mutahhari, the philosopher's knowledge is like a mathematician's, reached by thought. The knowledge sought by the ʿarif "is experienced and witnessed", like the knowledge of a scientist in his laboratory [@light, p. 41].

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: ʿIlm al-husuli and ʿilm al-huduri"}
*Husul* means acquiring or obtaining; *hudur* means presence. In *ʿilm al-husuli*, acquired knowledge, what reaches the mind is a concept or an image of the thing, while the thing itself stays outside. In *ʿilm al-huduri*, knowledge by presence, the thing known is itself present to the soul, as our own self, our hunger, and our pain are present. Imam Khomeini's word for the gnostic's witnessing, *huduriyya*, comes from the same root. Chapter One's Word Study noted that there is no way to God except through knowledge, and left open what kind of knowledge that is. It is knowledge by presence.
:::

## Where reason stops

::: {.point}
Reason knows things by their attributes and never reaches what they are. With God, who has no limits for a definition to draw, reason can prove that He exists and say what He is not. Knowing Him positively needs knowledge by presence, and the only place we have that knowledge is within our own soul, so that is where the answer must be found.
:::

Acquired knowledge is the work of reason, *ʿaql*, the faculty by which we reason, understand, and argue. Reason has a limit. When we know a thing, we know its attributes: its colour, its size, and what it does. Reason does not reach the thing that carries those attributes. Every description says that it is brown or square or useful, and none says what the "it" is. Ibn Sina, the greatest of the Muslim philosophers, granted this. The reality of things, he held, is beyond human capacity.

If this is true of the things around us, it is true far more of God. God has no colour, no size, and no use for the mind to take hold of, and no boundary for a definition to draw. Imam ʿAli (as) says as much at the opening of the first oration in *Nahj al-Balagha*, where he praises God.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of Nahj 1.1.1 from Qutbuddin p. 121]" source="Nahj al-Balagha"}
Soaring thoughts cannot reach him and deep minds cannot fathom him. [@nahj, 1.1.1]
:::

Soaring and depth are the two strengths of reason, its range and its penetration, and the Imam says that neither arrives at God. The next sentence of the oration gives the reason. God, it says, "cannot be described, for he is beyond the limits of demarcated boundaries" [@nahj, 1.1.1].

What reason can do, it does. It can show that God exists, and it can say what He is not: He is not a body, He is not in one place and absent from another, and He has no partner. All of this is true, and all of it is knowledge about God. It is also negative; each statement removes something from Him. ʿIrfan asks for the positive side, to know God as the Merciful, the Compassionate, and the Forgiving. Those names can be learned from a book. Knowing Him as Merciful, in the way the hungry know hunger, cannot.

Positive knowledge of this kind can only be knowledge by presence, and that points to one place. We have knowledge by presence only of what is within us: our own self and its states. Of everything outside us, our knowledge has to be acquired. If God is to be known, and not only known about, the knowing must happen within the soul. Mulla Sadra held that this is the only way to know the reality of anything, and the only way to know God.

The soul is also where a sense of God already lives, however faint. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls it a vague idea of God that we carry inside us. Our knowledge of our own self is in the same state: always present, and mostly unnoticed. The answer to how God can be known lies there, in a soul we have hardly begun to know. Part Two of this book turns to that soul.

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Mulla Sadra"}
Sadr al-Din Shirazi, known as Mulla Sadra, changed the course of Islamic philosophy. He moved it from the Peripatetic school, *al-hikma al-mashshaʾiyya*, to what is called the transcendent philosophy, *al-hikma al-mutaʿaliya*. *Kernel of the Kernels* calls him "the pride of the philosophers of the East". It records that after a lifetime in philosophy he became absorbed in worship and in the purification of his inner being. He asked God's pardon for the part of his life wasted on the opinions of pseudo-philosophers and polemicists, until "it became clear to me that their syllogisms were barren and their path devoid of straightness" [@kernel, p. 7].
:::

## Light in the heart

::: {.point}
Knowledge of God is a light that God places in the heart. His light is everywhere and nothing is held back on His side; a closed heart is what stands in the way, and a purified heart receives it. This light passes from heart to heart.
:::

Knowledge that cannot be produced from analysis must arrive from another source. A hadith on knowledge, which Imam Khomeini quotes in his *Forty Hadith*, names it.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the hadith from Forty Hadith p. 331]" source="Hadith"}
Knowledge is not extensive learning. Rather, it is a light that God casts in the heart of whomever He wills. [@forty_hadith, p. 331]
:::

The hadith sets the two kinds of knowing side by side. Extensive learning is acquired knowledge, gathered from outside. The light is not gathered at all; God places it in the heart. The *Lantern of the Path*, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), uses nearly the same words. Knowledge, it says, is a light "which Allah casts into the heart of whomsoever He wills" [@lantern, p. 10].

The words "whomever He wills" might suggest that God keeps His light for a chosen few. Sheikh Bahmanpour speaks of the light differently, and he does so with a verse of Surah al-Nur.

::: {.ayah ref="24:35" words="1-4"}
Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth.
:::

Allah's light is everywhere, in the heavens and in the earth, and He is with us wherever we are. It is not the case that He picks out some people to receive light and passes over others; there is no hindrance on His side. The heart is what is closed, and the work of the ʿurafaʾ is to open it and let the light in.

Imam Khomeini explains why an open heart is enough. When the soul is purified, he writes, divine teaching follows, "for there is no niggardliness in the higher realms, which are bounteous by necessity" [@forty_hadith, p. 331]. He grounds this in a promise of Surah al-Baqarah.

::: {.ayah ref="2:282" words="121-124"}
Be wary of Allah and Allah will teach you.
:::

The teaching follows the wariness. The light does not have to be fetched or earned, because it is always being given; a purified heart is one that is able to receive it.

The light comes to the hearts of the pure, and darkness covers the impure. This book's first chapter reached the same conclusion by a different road. Surah al-Rum says that God's signs are for those who reflect, know, listen, and reason, and yet most people pass them by. Nothing in the signs is hidden and nothing is wrong with our eyes; the fault lies with an inner faculty that has rusted and does not receive. Here the same truth appears as light: the light is everywhere, and the heart is closed.

A light received in one heart can reach another. We are familiar with knowledge passing from one mind to another, through speech and writing; we are less familiar with knowledge passing from one heart to another. The knowledge of the ʿurafaʾ, the Sheikh says, passes in the second way, "not from mind to mouth but from heart" to heart. Receiving it directly from God is possible but difficult, and most travellers begin through someone who has already received it. Chapter Seven returns to that companion.

## The path is purification

::: {.point}
The path to this knowing is suluk, the purifying of the heart, and purification begins with doing what the shariʿa asks. Study has its place at the start, in learning what to do; practice purifies; and the purified heart receives a knowledge that study alone cannot give.
:::

If the light is everywhere and the heart is closed, the path to knowing God is the opening of the heart. Two words name the difference. Knowledge learned at a university or a seminary comes through *fikr*, thought: we think, put things together, analyse, and find out. Knowledge of God comes through *suluk*, wayfaring, which the Sheikh describes as the path to purity; a striving towards purification.

The knowledge of ʿirfan cannot be read anywhere or worked out, and that is why it comes through suluk. In the Sheikh's words, "You purify yourself and then wait for Allah to put the light in your heart." The waiting is not for a light that is absent. It is the patience of a heart that has done its part and leaves the rest to God, whose light never stops.

Purification begins with the shariʿa. Its first step is to polish the soul by doing whatever the Prophet ﷺ has asked, and without this there is no maʿrifah. The Qur'an calls the believers to answer Allah and the Apostle when he summons them to what will give them life (Q 8:24). That life, the Sheikh says, comes when a person follows the Prophet completely. Practising the shariʿa is the first purification of the heart.

Practice depends on knowing what to practise, and this is where study comes in. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, in the lectures recorded in *Kernel of the Kernels*, places it at the start. The first essential for the traveller, he says, is knowledge of the rules of the shariʿa, learned from a jurist. The traveller puts that knowledge into practice and persists until conviction and gnosis grow by degrees, "for knowledge gives birth to action and action in turn brings knowledge" [@kernel, p. 56]. Learning starts the circle, practice purifies the heart, and the purified heart receives a deeper knowledge, which leads to better practice.

Imam Khomeini values the study of God's names and attributes in the same way, as a preparation. Such study, he says, prepares "the soul for receiving knowledge through inspiration" [@forty_hadith, p. 331]. He also warns that a learning which does not bring faith with it "is the greatest of veils". Even so, "one has to approach a veil in order to tear it into shreds" [@forty_hadith, p. 403]. In his account, study brings the traveller up to the veil, and purification is what tears it.

Murtada Mutahhari names the ʿarif's means directly. The philosopher, he writes, works with reason, deductions, and proofs. The means of the ʿarif are "the heart and the purification, disciplining and perfecting of the self" [@light, p. 42].

The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah ties the light to the path in one line.

::: {.quote ar="إِنَّ مَنِ ٱنْتَهَجَ بِكَ لَمُسْتَنِيرٌ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
One who follows Your path is enlightened. [@light, p. 165; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §88]
:::

The prologue opened on a line from this prayer that asks for the eyes of the heart to be lit until they reach the source of God's grandeur. This line names who receives that light: the one who follows God's path.

::: {.box-story title="Story: The philosopher and the ʿarif"}
Murtada Mutahhari records a meeting between Ibn Sina and Abu Saʿid ibn Abi al-Khayr, one of the most famous of all the ʿurafaʾ. Abu Saʿid was preaching on the necessity of deeds, and on obedience and disobedience to God. Ibn Sina answered him with a quatrain. Wherever God's favour and grace are found, it ran, "Let the not-done be like the done, the done like the not-done." It was a clever case for leaning on God's forgiveness instead of on deeds. Abu Saʿid replied at once with a quatrain of his own, which ends: "Do not rely on forgiveness, for never / Was the not-done like the done, the done like the not-done" [@light, p. 29]. The philosopher had argued his way past the need for deeds, and the ʿarif would not let an argument take the place of practice. Ibn Sina remained "mainly a philosopher, not a mystic", Mutahhari adds, though "especially towards the end of his life" he "developed mystic inclinations" [@light, p. 42].
:::

## Knowing by drawing near

::: {.point}
Knowing God differs in kind from knowing about God, and it is reached by purification, which study serves but cannot replace.
:::

Those brought near to God, the ʿurafaʾ, know Him in the second way, through a veil removed and not a definition learned. Their knowing is present to the soul, as hunger is present to the one who fasts, and it comes as a light that God gives to a purified heart.

Knowing God therefore differs in kind from knowing about God. It is reached by purification; study, in the view of Imam Khomeini and ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, serves it but cannot replace it.

Where, then, does an ordinary believer already stand on this path?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
In every prostration of the daily prayer we say, "Glory belongs to my Lord, the Exalted, and Him do I praise." ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi uses these words to show how knowledge follows action [@kernel, pp. 56–57]. For one week, in one prayer each day, say them slowly, "believing it to be a fact". Notice your own lowliness with your forehead on the ground, then the majesty of the One you are calling exalted. From "a very minor action, such as this dhikr said in prostration", he says, a person comes to discover God's absolute majesty [@kernel, p. 57].
:::
