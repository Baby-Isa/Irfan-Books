On a day of fasting in Ramadan, by late afternoon, no one has to be told that they are hungry. They do not read it in a book or work it out from the clock. The hunger is known from the inside. Sheikh Bahmanpour uses hunger to describe a kind of knowing that differs from the kind gained by study.

Most of what we know of God is of the kind gained by study. We learn the rulings of fiqh, the beliefs the scholars set out, and the history of the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as); all of it is knowledge about God. Chapter One argued that the Qur'an invites more than the minimum, and that the aim is to know and love God. The 'knowing' it refers to is the kind of knowing that a fasting person has of their hunger: knowing God directly, and not only knowing about Him.

::: {.words}
*ʿIlm*: knowledge, especially knowledge gained by study · *ʿilm al-husuli*: acquired knowledge · *ʿilm al-huduri*: knowledge by presence · *ʿaql*: the reasoning intellect · *suluk*: wayfaring; the path of purification
:::

## Knowing about, and knowing

::: {.point}
Acquired knowledge comes from outside and works by defining, which is limiting. Knowledge by presence is already with the soul; it is arrived at, not worked out, and it is known without error. The ʿarif's knowledge of God is knowledge by presence.
:::

Let's start with the two kinds of knowing. The first is acquired knowledge, *ʿilm al-husuli*. It reaches us from outside, through the senses or by reasoning. We form concepts of the things around us and then analyse those concepts. Almost everything we learn from teachers and books is of this kind.

Acquired knowledge works by defining its object. To understand a thing, we sort it into a category and mark where it ends and something else begins. The Arabic for a definition, *hadd*, also means a limit: to define a thing is to limit it. The old definition of a human being as "the rational animal" works by drawing a line round us.

The second kind is knowledge by presence, *ʿilm al-huduri*. It does not come from outside and needs no concept to carry it; it is with the soul already. The plainest case is our knowledge of our own self. Hunger, the example this chapter opened with, is another, and so are pain and anxiety. Anxiety has a cause outside us, yet the feeling itself arises inside the soul.

Knowledge by presence is complete in a way that acquired knowledge is not. When the body is hungry, the soul knows it at once and without effort, and nothing of the hunger is missed. The Sheikh adds that the hunger may even be false, and yet what is felt is real. The point is worth drawing out. A person who has eaten enough can still feel hungry. They may be wrong to think that their body needs food. They cannot be wrong that they feel hungry.

Knowledge by presence is what the mystics are after. Sheikh Bahmanpour defines mysticism as the urge to reach out to the infinite through knowledge by presence. The two kinds of knowing aim at different things: *ʿilm* aims at understanding, and *maʿrifah* aims at arriving. A person does not try to understand their hunger; they arrive at it and feel it. In the same way, the mystic's aim is to arrive at God and not to define Him, since a God who has been defined has been limited.

Imam Khomeini records the same distinction, citing the view that a gnostic of God "knows God through direct witnessing", while a scholar of God "attains the knowledge of God through philosophical proofs" [@forty_hadith, p. 542].

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: ʿIlm al-husuli and ʿilm al-huduri"}
*Husul* means acquiring or obtaining, whereas *hudur* means presence. In *ʿilm al-husuli*, acquired knowledge, what reaches the mind is a concept or an image of the thing, while the thing itself stays outside. In *ʿilm al-huduri*, knowledge by presence, the thing known is itself present to the soul, as our own self, our hunger, and our pain are present. Imam Khomeini's word for the gnostic's witnessing, *huduriyya*, comes from the same root. The religious texts use *ʿilm* and *maʿrifah* for the same thing; this book keeps them apart only to make the two kinds of knowing easier to see. Chapter One's Word Study noted that there is no way to God except through knowledge, and left open what type of knowledge it refers to; it assuredly refers to knowledge by presence.
:::

## Where reason stops

::: {.point}
Reason knows things by their attributes and never reaches what they are. With God, who has no limits for a definition to draw, reason can prove that He exists and say what He is not. Knowing Him positively needs knowledge by presence, and the only place we have that knowledge is within our own soul, so that is where the answer must be found.
:::

Acquired knowledge is the work of reason, *ʿaql*, the faculty by which we understand and argue. Reason has a limit. When we know a thing, we know its attributes: its colour, its size, and what it does. Every description says that it is brown or square or useful, and none says what the "it" is. Ibn Sina, the greatest of the Muslim philosophers, granted this. The reality of things, he held, is beyond the reach of human reason.

If this is true of the things around us, it is true far more of God. God has no colour or size for the mind to take hold of, and no boundary for a definition to draw. Imam ʿAli (as) says as much at the opening of the first oration in *Nahj al-Balagha*, where he praises God.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of Nahj 1.1.1 from Qutbuddin p. 121]" source="Nahj al-Balagha"}
Soaring thoughts cannot reach him and deep minds cannot fathom him. [@nahj, 1.1.1]
:::

The Imam's two images, soaring and depth, name the two strengths of reason, its range and its penetration, and neither arrives at God. The next sentence of the oration gives the reason. God, it says, "cannot be described, for he is beyond the limits of demarcated boundaries" [@nahj, 1.1.1].

What reason can do, it does. It can show that God exists, and it can say what He is not: He is not a body, He is not in one place and absent from another, and He has no partner. All of this is knowledge about God, and all of it is negative knowledge: each statement says what God is not, and none says what He is.

ʿIrfan asks for the positive side, to know God as the Merciful, the Compassionate, and the Forgiving. The names can be learned from a book. To know God as Merciful from within, as a hungry person knows their hunger, is knowledge by presence, and we have knowledge by presence only of what is within us: our own self and some of its states. Mulla Sadra held that knowledge by presence is the only way to know the reality of anything, God above all. If God is to be known, and not only known about, the knowing must happen within the soul.

The soul is not an empty place to look. A sense of God already lives there, however faint; Sheikh Bahmanpour calls it a vague idea of God that we carry inside us, mostly unnoticed. The answer to how God can be known lies in a soul we have hardly begun to know.

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Mulla Sadra and the schools of philosophy"}
Mulla Sadra died in 1050/1640 [@light, p. 40]. Before him, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's account, Islamic philosophy had two main schools, the Peripatetic, *al-hikma al-mashshaʾiyya*, and the Illuminationist. The Peripatetic school is the way of reasoned proof that Murtada Mutahhari describes: the philosopher works with "reason, deductions and proofs" [@light, p. 42]. It is the school of Ibn Sina. Mulla Sadra called his own philosophy neither of these but the transcendent philosophy, *al-hikma al-mutaʿaliya*. He took what the mystics know only by experience and gave it rational argument, a rationalised mysticism, and held that the reality of things is known only through knowledge by presence. Put simply, the Peripatetics reason their way to the truth, whereas Mulla Sadra held that reasoning must end in the soul's own presence to what it knows. He studied the ʿirfan of Ibn ʿArabi deeply without belonging to any Sufi order, and rated Ibn ʿArabi above Ibn Sina [@light, pp. 35, 40]. *Kernel of the Kernels* records that after a lifetime in philosophy he became absorbed in worship and the purification of his inner being, until "it became clear to me that their syllogisms were barren and their path devoid of straightness" [@kernel, p. 7].
:::

## Light in the heart

::: {.point}
Knowledge of God is a light that God places in the heart. His light is everywhere and nothing is held back on His side; a closed heart is what stands in the way, and a purified heart receives it. This light passes from heart to heart.
:::

Knowledge that cannot be produced from analysis, and that has to be found within the soul, must be placed there. A hadith on knowledge, which Imam Khomeini quotes in his *Forty Hadith*, says by whom.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the hadith from Forty Hadith p. 331]" source="Hadith"}
Knowledge is not extensive learning. Rather, it is a light that God casts in the heart of whomever He wills. [@forty_hadith, p. 331]
:::

The hadith sets the two kinds of knowing side by side. Extensive learning is acquired knowledge, gathered from outside. The light is not gathered at all; God places it in the heart.

The words "whomever He wills" raise a question: does God keep His light for a chosen few? Sheikh Bahmanpour speaks of the light differently, and he does so with a verse of Surah al-Nur.

::: {.ayah ref="24:35" words="1-4"}
Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth.
:::

Allah's light is everywhere, in the heavens and in the earth, and He is with us wherever we are. The hadith and the verse can seem to pull in different directions: the hadith says that God casts the light into the heart of whomever He wills, as if He chose some hearts over others, whereas the verse says that His light is everywhere already.

Both are true. Every act is God's, including the lighting of any heart, and nothing happens except by His will. His will, however, is not to withhold: His light is given without stint. What keeps a heart dark is that it is closed, and the work of the ʿurafaʾ is to open it to a light that was always there.

Imam Khomeini explains why purification matters so much. When the soul is purified, he writes, divine teaching follows, "for there is no niggardliness in the higher realms, which are bounteous by necessity" [@forty_hadith, p. 331]. He grounds this in a promise of Surah al-Baqarah.

::: {.ayah ref="2:282" words="121-124"}
Be wary of Allah and Allah will teach you.
:::

In the verse, God's teaching follows on from wariness of Him: the light comes to the hearts of the pure, and darkness covers the impure. Chapter One reached the same conclusion by a different road. Surah al-Rum says that God's signs are for those who reflect, know, listen, and reason, and yet most people pass them by. Nothing in the signs is hidden and nothing is wrong with our eyes; the fault lies with an inner faculty that has rusted and does not receive.

A light received in one heart can reach another. The knowledge of books passes from mind to mouth, through speech and writing, whereas the knowledge of the ʿurafaʾ passes, in the Sheikh's words, "from heart to heart". Receiving it directly from God is possible but difficult, and most travellers begin through someone who has already received it. Chapter Seven returns to that companion.

## The path is purification

::: {.point}
The path to this knowing is suluk, the purifying of the heart, and purification begins with doing what the shariʿa asks. Study has its place at the start, in learning what to do; practice purifies; and the purified heart receives a knowledge that study alone cannot give.
:::

If the light is everywhere and the heart is closed, the path to knowing God is the opening of the heart. Two words name the difference. Knowledge learned at a university or a seminary comes through *fikr*, thought: we think, put things together, analyse, and find out. Knowledge of God comes through *suluk*, wayfaring, which the Sheikh describes as the path to purity.

In the Sheikh's words, "You purify yourself and then wait for Allah to put the light in your heart." The waiting is not for a light that is absent. It is the patience of a heart that has done its part and leaves the rest to God, whose light never stops.

Purification begins with the shariʿa. Its first step is to polish the soul by doing whatever the Prophet ﷺ has asked, and without this there is no maʿrifah. The Qur'an calls the believers to answer Allah and the Apostle when he summons them to what will give them life (Q 8:24). That life, the Sheikh says, comes when a person follows the Prophet completely.

Practice depends on knowing what to practise, and this is where study comes in. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, in the lectures recorded in *Kernel of the Kernels*, places it at the start. The first essential for the traveller, he says, is knowledge of the rules of the shariʿa, learned from a jurist. The traveller puts that knowledge into practice and persists until conviction and gnosis grow by degrees, "for knowledge gives birth to action and action in turn brings knowledge" [@kernel, p. 56]. Learning starts the circle, practice purifies the heart, and the purified heart receives a deeper knowledge, which leads to better practice.

Imam Khomeini gives study its place, with a warning. A learning that does not bring faith with it, he writes, "is the greatest of veils"; yet "one has to approach a veil in order to tear it into shreds" [@forty_hadith, p. 403].

The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah ties the light to the path in one line.

::: {.quote ar="إِنَّ مَنِ ٱنْتَهَجَ بِكَ لَمُسْتَنِيرٌ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
One who follows Your path is enlightened. [@light, p. 165; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §88]
:::

The prologue opened on a line from this prayer that asks for the eyes of the heart to be lit until they reach the source of God's grandeur. This line names who receives that light: the one who follows God's path.

::: {.box-story title="Story: The philosopher and the ʿarif"}
Murtada Mutahhari records a meeting between Ibn Sina and Abu Saʿid ibn Abi al-Khayr, one of the most famous of all the ʿurafaʾ. Abu Saʿid was preaching on the necessity of deeds, and on obedience and disobedience to God. Ibn Sina answered him with a quatrain (a poem of four lines). Wherever God's favour and grace are found, it ran, "Let the not-done be like the done, the done like the not-done." The verse leaned on God's forgiveness in place of deeds. Abu Saʿid replied at once with a quatrain of his own, which ends: "Do not rely on forgiveness, for never / Was the not-done like the done, the done like the not-done" [@light, p. 29]. The ʿarif would not let a verse, however well turned, take the place of deeds. Mutahhari adds that towards the end of his life Ibn Sina "developed mystic inclinations" [@light, p. 42].
:::

## Knowing by drawing near

::: {.point}
Knowing God differs in kind from knowing about God, and it is reached by purification, which study serves but cannot replace.
:::

Those brought near to God, the ʿurafaʾ, know Him by presence, through a veil removed and not a definition learned. Their knowing is present to the soul, as hunger is present to the one who fasts, and it comes as a light that God gives to a purified heart. Knowing God therefore differs in kind from knowing about God. It is reached by purification; study, in the view of Imam Khomeini and ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, serves it but cannot replace it.

Where, then, does an ordinary believer already stand on this path?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
In every prostration of the daily prayer we say, "Glory belongs to my Lord, the Exalted, and Him do I praise." ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi uses these words to show how knowledge follows action [@kernel, pp. 56–57]. For one week, in one prayer each day, say them slowly, "believing it to be a fact". Notice your own lowliness with your forehead on the ground, then the majesty of the One you are calling exalted. From "a very minor action, such as this dhikr said in prostration", he says, a person comes to discover God's absolute majesty [@kernel, p. 57].
:::
