::: {.quote ar="وَّقَلْبِي بِحُبِّكَ مُتَيَّماً" source="Duʿa Kumayl"}
And my heart enthralled by Your love! [@duas_dua_kumayl, §221]
:::

These words come near the end of Duʿa Kumayl, the prayer Imam ʿAli (as) taught to Kumayl ibn Ziyad, one of his close disciples. It is a prayer many of us hear on Thursday evenings, the time the Mafatih recommends for it [@mafatih, p. 129]. The line asks God for a heart enthralled by His love.

A heart enthralled by God's love is one of the two things ʿirfan seeks. Other prayers we recite ask for the second, knowing God. This chapter shows that we already know these words.

::: {.words}
*duʿa*: supplication, any request made to God · *munajat*: an intimate, whispered prayer to God · *Mafatih al-Jinan*: the standard manual of Shia prayers [@journey, p. 112] · *inqitaʿ*: severance of the heart from all but God
:::

## Cut off from all else

::: {.point}
The prayers' first request is a severance from everything but God, made in the heart.

1. Before the Munajat asks to see God, it asks to be cut off from everything else.
2. Taken at its word, that could mean leaving family, work, and society; the Sheikh answers that the cutting off is in the heart.
3. Duʿa Abu Hamza and Duʿa ʿArafah both place it there: love of the world and love for others leave the heart.
4. Therefore the severance the prayers ask for is in the heart.
:::

The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah asks to be cut off from everything before it asks to see God. This book opened on its request for light in the eyes of the heart. In the prayer, that line follows straight on from a request for severance. The order fits what Sheikh Bahmanpour says of those who seek God: they want to know Him and to look at Him, but "they have to cut off from everything first".

::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي هَبْ لي كَمَالَ ٱلاِنْقِطَاعِ إِلَيْكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, grant me complete severance of my relations with everything else [@light, p. 165; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §104]
:::

The Munajat asks for this severance (*inqitaʿ*) in full. Taken at its word, the request could sound like a call to leave family, work, and society behind. The Sheikh raises the question himself: is it good to cut off from everything else completely? His answer is "in the heart yes". Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali, recited on the nights of Ramadan, names what is to be cut away from the heart.

::: {.quote ar="سَيِّدِي أَخْرِجْ حُبَّ ٱلدُّنْيَا مِنْ قَلْبِي" source="Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali"}
O my Master, (please) expel love for the world out of my heart [@duas_ramadan_dua_abu_hamza_thumali, §384]
:::

The prayer asks that love of the world leave the heart. Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, describes the same severance from God's side: He has already made it in the hearts of those He loves.

::: {.quote ar="وَأَنْتَ ٱلَّذِي أَزَلْتَ ٱلأَغْيَارَ عَنْ قُلُوبِ أَحِبَّائِكَ حَتَّىٰ لَمْ يُحِبُّوٱ سِوَاكَ" source="Duʿa ʿArafah"}
It is You Who have removed love for others from the hearts of Your lovers so that they have loved none save You [@duas_dua_arafah_imam_husain, §783–784]
:::

Both prayers speak of love, and both remove it from the heart. The severance the prayers ask for is therefore in the heart.

## To see, to love, to draw near

::: {.point}
The prayers then ask for what ʿirfan seeks: to see God with the heart, to love Him, and to come near Him.

1. A heart cleared of everything but God is free to ask three things of Him.
2. The first is sight, though the Qur'an says sight cannot perceive God.
3. The prayer asks for light in the eyes of the heart, not the eyes in our heads, and treats that light as God's to give.
4. The second request is love: a place among God's friends.
5. That friendship is knowledge of God and love of Him, the two things ʿirfan seeks.
6. The third request is nearness.
7. The Munajat asks God for a heart whose passion brings it near; a prayer of the Sahifa asks to join those already near.
8. Nearness is felt when we call on God. Therefore the three requests, sight, love, and nearness, are what ʿirfan seeks.
:::

A heart cleared of everything but God is free for Him. The prayers go on to ask for three things: to see God with the heart, to love Him, and to come near Him.

The first is sight. The Munajat asks God to light the eyes of the heart so that they look at Him and pass even through the veils of light. Yet the Qur'an says that sight cannot perceive God (Q 6:103). The difficulty is a real one, and people unfamiliar with the mystics' wording sometimes call such lines a mistake.

The prayer does not ask for the eyes in our heads. It asks for the eyes of the heart. Our physical eyes work perfectly well, and still we miss God's signs, because the inner faculty behind them is blind. The prayer asks God to light that faculty, and treats the light as His to give. Duʿa ʿArafah says He has already lit that light in the hearts of His intimate servants, the friends of God (*awliyaʾ*).

::: {.quote ar="أَنْتَ ٱلَّذِي أَشْرَقْتَ ٱلأَنْوَارَ فِي قُلُوبِ أَوْلِيَائِكَ" source="Duʿa ʿArafah"}
It is You Who have lit the illuminations in the hearts of Your intimate servants [@duas_dua_arafah_imam_husain, §781]
:::

The second request is love. The Munajat asks for a place among God's friends, and it defines that place by love.

::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي أَقِمْنِي فِي أَهْلِ وِلايَتِكَ مُقَامَ مَنْ رَجَا ٱلزِّيَادَةَ مِنْ مَحَبَّتِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, place me among Your friends in the position of one who hopes for an increase in Your love. [@light, p. 165; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §92–93]
:::

The Sheikh reads the friendship asked for here (*wilaya*) as having two parts. One is maʿrifah: understanding God, looking at Him, and knowing Him. The other is love for Him. The love never stops, and the knowing keeps growing. These are the two things ʿirfan seeks, and both are in one line of the Munajat.

The third request is nearness, and the Munajat makes it earlier in the same prayer.

::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي هَبْ لِي قَلْباً يُدْنِيهِ مِنْكَ شَوْقُهُ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, provide me with a heart, the passion of which may bring it near You [@light, p. 165; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §82]
:::

The request is for a heart whose own passion brings it near, and that heart is itself asked of God. The Whispered Prayer of the Lovers is one of fifteen whispered prayers printed with al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya. It asks for a place among those God has chosen for His nearness.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of Sahifa, Whispered Prayer of the Lovers]" source="Whispered Prayer of the Lovers"}
My God, place us with him whom Thou hast chosen for Thy nearness and Thy friendship, … granted gazing upon Thy face [@sahifa, p. 656]
:::

The difference nearness makes is felt in prayer. For some of us, calling on God can feel like calling into the sky from far away. Others feel Him present when they call, and feel His response. With this third request, the prayers have asked for all that ʿirfan seeks: to see God with the heart, to love Him, and to be near Him.

## A pupil reading above his level

::: {.point}
Words above our level are still ours to say, as an honour and as a plea from need.

1. These are the words of those brought near, like a university book handed to a primary school pupil.
2. The prayers themselves speak from need.
3. Even our turning towards God is His doing.
4. The Sheikh's answer: we are honoured to be allowed to recite them, and God may grant a sincere request.
:::

Requests this high raise a difficulty, which Sheikh Bahmanpour puts plainly. These words come from the peak of understanding and longing, from those brought near to God, yet ordinary believers are told to recite them. He compares it to telling a primary school boy to read university books.

The prayers themselves speak from need. Duʿa ʿArafah comes to God with nothing to offer but its need of Him.

::: {.quote ar="هَا أَنَا أَتَوَسَّلُ إِلَيْكَ بِفَقْرِي إِلَيْكَ" source="Duʿa ʿArafah"}
Here I am begging You by my need for You. [@duas_dua_arafah_imam_husain, §689]
:::

Need is the only plea in this line, and the Munajat line at the head of Part One goes further. There the reciter admits to having had no strength to move away from disobedience "except when You awakened me to Your love" [@light, p. 164], so even the turning towards God is His doing.

The Sheikh's answer to the difficulty is that we have been honoured to be allowed to recite these words, though they are not of our level. The prayers' own pleas from need fit that answer. They claim no level, so a reciter may say them without understanding many of their meanings. If the reciter is sincere, God may grant the request, and what the Imams wanted, He granted them.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: duʿa and munajat"}
*Duʿa* is the general word for supplication: any request addressed to God, in any words. A *munajat* is a duʿa spoken as a private conversation with God. The Mafatih's English calls the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah a "confidential prayer" [@mafatih, p. 243], and William Chittick translates the fifteen munajat printed with the Sahifa as "whispered prayers" [@sahifa, p. 9]. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi suggests why the Imams' supplications reach further than their other words. In their narrations, he writes, they spoke with people, whereas in their supplications they spoke with God, and the level of speech depends on the understanding of the one addressed. The Qur'an, in his terms, is a "Descending-Qur'an", God's words to His servants. The supplications are an "Ascending-Qur'an", the words of His most distinguished servants rising towards Him [@journey, p. 43].
:::

## Prayers for ordinary nights

::: {.point}
These prayers are set on nights we already keep, so anyone may begin them.

1. Four of these prayers are in the Mafatih, each set on a night or a day.
2. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah belongs to Shaʿban, to be said with presence of heart.
3. Duʿa Kumayl, Duʿa ʿArafah, and Duʿa Abu Hamza have their own nights and days.
4. Said on nights we keep, the words become familiar.
5. Reciting is not comprehending: few grasp their depth.
6. Therefore anyone may begin them; what we have is access, not attainment.
:::

The invitation to recite these words reaches us through a book most believers already own. Four of the prayers quoted in this chapter are in the Mafatih al-Jinan, which sets each of them on a night or a day in the believer's year.

The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah is listed among the acts of the month of Shaʿban. The Mafatih reports it as the prayer Imam ʿAli (as) and the other Imams used to say in that month [@mafatih, p. 243]. It adds that it is "advisable to say it with presence of heart all the time" [@mafatih, p. 245]. The full text, in Arabic and English, is given in the Appendix.

The other prayers have their own times. Duʿa Kumayl belongs to Thursday evenings and to the night of mid-Shaʿban [@mafatih, p. 129]. Duʿa ʿArafah is the prayer of Imam al-Husayn (as) on the Day of ʿArafah [@mafatih, p. 374]. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali belongs to Ramadan, when Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as) said it in the last hour of each night [@mafatih, p. 282]. Maliki Tabrizi tells the traveller not to forget it, and to say it every night or every other night, as his mood and situation allow [@journey, p. 111].

Said on nights we keep, these words become familiar ones. Imam Khomeini could count on that familiarity. "You are already familiar with the words used in Imams' supplications," he told his listeners, before reading them lines from the Shaʿbaniyyah [@light, p. 156].

::: {.box-history title="History: the Mafatih and the Sahifa"}
The prayers in this chapter reach ordinary believers through two books. The *Mafatih al-Jinan* ("Keys to the Heavens") gathers the supplications of the Imams, the words to be recited at particular times, and the forms for visiting the Imams' shrines. Its compiler, Shaykh ʿAbbas Qummi, was a scholar of vast learning who died in Najaf in 1359/1940 [@journey, p. 112]. Al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya is far older. William Chittick calls it "the oldest prayer manual in Islamic sources", composed by Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as). Shia tradition ranks it behind only the Qur'an and Nahj al-Balagha [@sahifa, p. 7]. The fourth Imam died in Medina in 95/713–4, or a year earlier [@sahifa, p. 8]. The fifteen whispered prayers, the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers among them, were added to several modern editions of the Sahifa. They seem to have reached most Shia through ʿAllama Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, the compiler of *Bihar al-Anwar* [@sahifa, p. 9].
:::

Reciting the words is not the same as comprehending what they describe. Maliki Tabrizi sees the supplications as a servant's own words rising to God. That, he writes, "is not accepted and comprehended by any one, except a small number of believers who are thoroughly familiar and enlightened" [@journey, p. 43].

Sheikh Bahmanpour still recommends the Munajat to every listener, to be recited in Shaʿban as often as they can. The two positions answer different questions: how far a reciter reaches, and who may begin. This chapter claims only the second, access to these words rather than attainment of what they ask for.

## Words already on our lips

::: {.point}
The goal of ʿirfan is already on the believer's lips.

1. Therefore: the prayers ask for what ʿirfan seeks, from need, on nights we keep, so its goal is already on our lips.
2. The prayers keep asking God to work on the heart, the self within us, which leaves the question of what the self is.
:::

Access to these words is where an ordinary believer already stands on the path. The prayers ask for what ʿirfan seeks, in the voice of someone in need, on nights the ordinary believer keeps. The goal of ʿirfan is therefore not foreign to us: it is already on our lips.

These prayers keep asking God to work on the heart, the self within us. What is this self?
