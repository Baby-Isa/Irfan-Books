::: {.ayah ref="7:29" words="14-16"}
Even as He brought you forth in the beginning, so will you return.
:::

In many of our families, when someone is dying, a relative sits at the head of the bed and recites Surah Yasin. The Mafatih tells of angels who come when it is recited "at the head of an ailing person", and Imam al-Sadiq (as) calls it "the heart of Quran" [@mafatih, p. 21]. When the end comes, those in the room say the words of Surah al-Baqarah that we say at every death.

::: {.ayah ref="2:156" words="6-10"}
Indeed we belong to Allah and to Him do we indeed return.
:::

These words name a return to God that every soul makes. Chapter Sixteen followed the friends of God back to people, and this chapter follows every soul back to God by the road it came, giving back what it was given. ʿIrfan is making that return willingly and in this life. The Qur'an tells of the return mostly in pictures of collars, chains, gardens, and fire. The surah recited at the bedside holds one of them, so the chapter first settles how such pictures are read. <!-- src: L09 ¶88; L16 ¶18-19 -->

::: {.words}
*taʾwil*: drawing out the inner meaning of a verse · *maʿad*: the return to God · *rida*: contentment with God's will · *tawakkul*: reliance on God · *taslim*: submission to God · *shafaʿa*: intercession
:::

## Reading from the inside

:::: {.point}
The Qur'an's pictures of the next world carry an inner meaning, which is read only where it agrees with the outer meaning and never cancels it.

1. No one wears the iron collars of Surah Yasin (Q 36:8); their meaning lies inside the picture: the light of God is covered for those who refuse to believe.
2. That does not license any meaning: a mystic's whim and a scheme brought from outside are both ruled out (the lectures; Ayatullah Khomeini sets a similar guard).
3. What is allowed is an "insightful look"; following ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi and al-Ghazali, the Sheikh holds that the inner meaning agrees with the outer, and the outer stays.
4. The opposite mistake is also ruled out: treating the inner meaning as false closes the path too (Maliki Tabrizi). *(Word Study box.)*
5. The rule holds in the next world: hell's chain (Q 69:32) may be a real chain, and may also be the chain of long ambitions.
6. The Qur'an itself carries the inner reading across: blind there as here (Q 17:72), yet the people of hell see one another (Q 7:38).
::::

Near the start of Surah Yasin, the surah at the bedside, is a picture of those who refuse to believe.

::: {.ayah ref="36:8"}
Indeed We have put iron collars around their necks, which are up to the chins, so their heads are upturned.
:::

No unbeliever walks the street with an iron collar forcing his chin up, and no one reads the verse that way. The meaning lies inside the picture. The light of God is covered for such people, so they go through life without seeing what lies before them or behind them, and the next verse ends "so they do not see" (Q 36:9). <!-- src: L16 ¶9-12 -->

If a verse can mean more than its words, it might seem to mean anything we please. The lectures rule out two kinds of reading. One gives a verse a meaning "at the will of some mystic" with no tie to the verse. The other lays a scheme from some other system of thought over every verse in the Qur'an. Ayatullah Khomeini sets a similar guard when he writes that "Islam does not allow anybody to interpret the Qur'an according to his personal opinion or private judgment". Among them he counts people who give the verses "some spiritual meaning" to suit their own wishes [@light, p. 110]. <!-- src: L16 ¶3-5 -->

What Sheikh Bahmanpour allows instead is "a type of insightful look at the verses of the Quran". Following ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi and al-Ghazali, he holds that the inner meaning must agree with the outer and never go against it, and that the outer meaning stays when the inner one is found. Meaning in the Qur'an has layers, and beneath each layer we find a deeper one. The model is a parable (*mathal*) such as the Verse of Light from Chapter Two, whose niche and lamp bring a meaning about God closer to the mind. <!-- src: L16 ¶5-8 -->

Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi guards against the opposite mistake. To limit the Qur'an to its outward meaning and treat its inner meanings as false, he writes, "also closes the path of Qur'anic understanding" [@journey, p. 54]. <!-- src: L16 ¶5-8 -->

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: taʾwil and tafsir"}
In this book, *tafsir* means commentary on a verse, and *taʾwil* the drawing out of its inner meaning. Maliki Tabrizi names the two together, "their commentary (tafsir) and esoteric meanings (tawil)", among the meanings a reader must not treat as false [@journey, p. 54]. An inner reading done well uncovers one side of the Qur'an and never claims the whole. Ayatullah Khomeini says of the commentaries the great gnostics wrote that "what they have written is not the exegesis of the Qur'an. At the most it can be said that they have exposed some aspects of it" [@light, p. 109]. He says the same of every commentator, since "The Qur'an is not a book all aspects of which may be exposed by us or by anybody else". What lies beyond us, he adds, is "to be explained by the Imams" [@light, p. 110].
:::

The verses about hell test the rule. Surah al-Haqqa describes a man led to the Fire, and the command given over him.

::: {.ayah ref="69:32"}
Then bind him in a chain, seventy cubits in length.
:::

If the collar of Surah Yasin is not seen on unbelievers in this world, the chain in the next may also be "something else". It may be the long chain of ambitions and ignorance that keeps them from seeing God. Both readings stand in the lectures, which grant that "we cannot deny that it might be the real material type of chain" and, in the next sentence, that "we cannot deny the deeper meanings as well". <!-- src: L16 ¶13-16 -->

The Qur'an itself carries the inner reading into the next world. Chapter Four quoted Surah al-Isra on the soul's sight, and the same surah says that whoever is blind in this world will be blind in the Hereafter (Q 17:72). Yet the people of hell are not sightless; every nation that enters the Fire curses its sister nation (Q 7:38), and the damned know one another well enough to quarrel. The blindness there is therefore the blindness of the heart we know here, and the chain there can be read as the collar here is read. <!-- src: L16 ¶16-17 -->

## Given in order, returned in reverse

:::: {.point}
Every soul returns to God by the road it came, giving back in reverse what it was given.

1. Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi reads hell and paradise from the inside and keeps their material side. *(Who's Who box.)*
2. For Tusi the return (*maʿad*) is the way we came, run backwards (Q 7:29).
3. We came by four gifts, in the order a child receives them. *(Reader told: "Let's start with how we came".)*
    a. Existence first (Q 40:67).
    b. Then knowledge (Q 16:78).
    c. Then power, and last will.
4. We go back will first, then power, knowledge, and existence, gradually.
::::

Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi reads the verses on the return in this way. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls his account of hell and paradise a mystical reading that "doesn't deny the material aspect", and the reading asks what brings people to each. <!-- src: L16 ¶17 -->

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi"}
Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi died in 672/1273, the same year as Rumi [@light, p. 36]. He wrote the commentary on Ibn Sina's *Isharat* whose stages of the road Chapter Eight set beside its own [@light, p. 50]. Ayatullah Khomeini calls him "the best of the later scholars and the most perfect among the predecessors" [@forty_hadith, p. 517]. The account of the return in this chapter comes from another of his books, which the lectures call "Reminder of the beginning and the end", and Sheikh Bahmanpour adds that Mulla Sadra translated long passages of it into his own *Asfar*.
:::

For Tusi, the return (*maʿad*) is a going back in reverse along the road by which we came. The verse at the head of this chapter, from Surah al-Aʿraf, says as much. As God brought us forth in the beginning, so we will return. <!-- src: L16 ¶18-19 -->

Let's start with how we came. Tusi traces the road by the way each of us entered the world, and he finds four gifts given in order. Existence comes first. Surah Ghafir follows each of us from dust to a drop of fluid, then to a clinging mass, and then out into the world as an infant (Q 40:67). <!-- src: L16 ¶20 -->

Knowledge follows. Surah al-Nahl says that God brought us forth from our mothers' bellies knowing nothing. He then made for us hearing, eyesight, and hearts (Q 16:78), and what we come to know reaches us through the senses. <!-- src: L16 ¶21 -->

Power comes third. At first a baby can do nothing for itself. Then it can shout or crawl for what it wants, and its power grows as the child grows. Will comes last, once the child knows it has the power: it wants something, and it goes and gets it. <!-- src: L16 ¶22 -->

The return runs the same road backwards. The will is given back first, then power, then knowledge, and last existence itself. None of this is asked of us at the start. For years we grow in all four, wanting things, reaching for them, and getting them. Then, gradually, God asks us to turn and go the other way. <!-- src: L16 ¶22-23 -->

## Will, power, knowledge, existence

:::: {.point}
Each gift given back becomes a station on the way to God, and God gives each back without limit.

1. The will given back is contentment (*rida*), with no separate will (Ayatullah Khomeini).
2. Contentment loses nothing: in paradise the soul has whatever it wishes (Q 50:35), and contentment is its key.
3. Power given back is reliance (*tawakkul*): the power we use is God's (Q 65:3); Chapter Twelve met this truth as the first degree of annihilation, in action.
4. Knowledge given back is submission (*taslim*), the submission of Ibrahim (Q 2:131).
5. Existence given back is annihilation (Chapter Twelve).
6. God gives all four back, new and without limit (Q 50:34), and in Tusi's reading that is paradise.
7. Ayatullah Khomeini quotes Tusi's commentary on the *Isharat* to the same effect (powers, sciences and wills vanishing in God's), after Shaykh Bahaʾi's guard against union with God.
::::

The will is the first gift given back, and the station it opens is contentment (*rida*). A soul with no will of its own wants nothing but what comes from God, and is content with it. For Ayatullah Khomeini, the one who has reached this station "is one who has annihilated his will in the Divine Will, having no more a separate will of his own". He recalls a mystic who, asked what he wished, replied, "My wish is not to wish at all" [@forty_hadith, p. 207]. <!-- src: L16 ¶23-24 -->

A soul that wants nothing for itself might seem to have nothing left to enjoy, but Surah Qaf says the opposite of those in paradise.

::: {.ayah ref="50:35"}
There they will have whatever they wish, and with Us there is yet more.
:::

Once our will has become God's will, whatever we wish we have, and the will comes back everlasting and joined to His. The angel in charge of paradise, Tusi notes, is called Ridwan, and the name means that no one enters without contentment. Paradise is full of treasures, and in the lectures' words, "the key to it is this contentment". <!-- src: L16 ¶24-25 -->

Power is given back next, and its station is reliance on God (*tawakkul*). In its plain sense, Ayatullah Khomeini explains, *tawakkul* is the "admission of one's inability and reliance on one other than oneself" [@forty_hadith, p. 204]. Reliance could be mistaken for sitting and waiting for God to act, and the Sheikh rules that out: "whatever you do you know that this is not you. This is Him." The power we use is God's, and Surah al-Talaq promises that "whoever puts his trust in Allah, He will suffice him" (Q 65:3). Chapter Twelve met this truth at the first degree of annihilation, which is annihilation in action. <!-- src: L16 ¶27-28 -->

Knowledge goes back third, and its station is submission (*taslim*). The traveller knows that he knows nothing of himself. God alone knows, and whatever knowledge the traveller has is His gift, so he hands his judgement back to the One who knows. In Surah al-Baqarah this is the submission of the Prophet Ibrahim (as). <!-- src: L16 ¶28-30 -->

::: {.ayah ref="2:131"}
When his Lord said to him, ‘Submit,’ he said, ‘I submit to the Lord of all the worlds.’
:::

Existence goes back last, and its station is annihilation (*fanaʾ*). There the traveller sees that existence belongs to God alone, and Chapter Twelve followed him to that station and back. <!-- src: L16 ¶30 -->

Nothing of the traveller seems left once all four are given back, yet Tusi has God return them new. Contentment is made complete. Power returns without limit to a soul now "drowned" in God's power and "completely joined with that power". Knowledge comes from nothing in the soul itself and "will shine in you", and existence becomes everlasting on the day Surah Qaf calls "the day of immortality" (Q 50:34). That, for Tusi, is paradise. <!-- src: L16 ¶31-32 -->

Ayatullah Khomeini sets a passage of Tusi's beside this, in which the gnostic sees "all powers as vanishing in Divine power and all sciences as drowned in Divine knowledge and all wills as vanishing in His will", but only after Shaykh Bahaʾi's warning that such words never mean God dwells in a servant or becomes one with him [@forty_hadith, pp. 516–517].

## The return refused

:::: {.point}
The return happens whether or not a soul makes it: a believer who left it undone finishes it after death, helped by intercession; a will that never yields meets the four gifts as their opposites, which is hell, and it is not God's revenge.

1. A believer goes through the return either way; one who has not made it here finishes it after death, with difficulty.
2. Intercession completes the believers' light (Q 66:8).
3. A will that never turns is overruled and held (Q 23:71); that may be the seventy-cubit chain.
4. All four gifts come back as their opposites (Q 87:13).
5. Hell, as the lecture asks us to think of it, is not God's revenge, and the wrath is still real.
6. The fire is still true, as two forms of one thing are both true in a dream (Q 4:10). *(Story box: Yusuf reads the king's dream.)*
7. A believer holds fear and hope together (Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah).
::::

Not every believer gives these four back in this life, yet in the lecture's words a believer goes through the return all the same, "either voluntarily or involuntarily". One who has not made it here makes it with "lots of fears, lots of difficulty" in barzakh and on the Day of Resurrection. Barzakh is the middle world that Chapter Five described. There the will is taken from him, and he is shown that there is no power and no life but God's. <!-- src: L16 ¶39-41 -->

A believer cannot finish that road alone. In the lecture's words paradise "is not our level", and without intercession (*shafaʿa*) it cannot be entered, since it takes someone great to bring us there. Surah al-Tahrim shows the believers on the Last Day with a light running before them, but the light is not complete, and they ask for it to be made whole. <!-- src: L16 ¶34, ¶37 -->

::: {.ayah ref="66:8" words="34-38"}
They will say, ‘Our Lord! Perfect our light for us
:::

That light is completed through the Prophet ﷺ. The same verse says that on that day God "will not let down the Prophet and the faithful who are with him", and the Sheikh reads this as a promise against humiliation. If the Prophet's own followers went to hell, the shame would fall on him, though he has done no wrong, and so God completes their light through him. <!-- src: L16 ¶37-39 -->

A will that never turns towards God meets a different end. It carries its opposition into the next world, and there God's will prevails over it. Surah al-Muʾminun says why.

::: {.ayah ref="23:71" words="1-9"}
Had the truth followed their desires, the heavens and the earth would have surely fallen apart [along] with those who are in them.
:::

A will that is never followed is held in check, and its owner is "always in torment". The chain of seventy cubits from the first section may be this held will, and in the lectures its length is "because of the length of the will that they have". <!-- src: L16 ¶42-43 -->

All four gifts return the same way, each as the mirror image of the station it would have opened. In place of contentment there is utter dissatisfaction. In place of power there is helplessness, because these souls never learned that the power they used here was God's and cannot use it there. Those who thought they knew find themselves ignorant, and humiliation stands where submission would have been. Last, in place of union with God there is separation from Him, and an existence that is neither life nor death. Surah al-Aʿla says this of the one who enters the Great Fire. <!-- src: L16 ¶45-47, ¶54-55 -->

::: {.ayah ref="87:13"}
then he will neither live in it, nor die.
:::

Hell might then look like revenge, and one way the lecture asks us to think of hell answers that reading: "It is not a punishment which is brought to you by God. God doesn't want to revenge." Everything is God's creation, and only mercy comes from Him. Such a soul has made itself unable to use God's will, power, and knowledge. It brings that state back with it, and separation from Him follows.

Yet those who will against the will of God all the time, the lecture goes on, "will deserve the wrath of God". On this reading the wrath is what such a soul has made itself fit for, and not a change in God's mercy. <!-- src: L16 ¶43, ¶47-48 -->

Hell as the soul's own separation still leaves the fire and the boiling water, and of these the lectures say "of course it's all true". They recall the verse of Surah al-Nisaʾ in which those who consume the property of orphans ingest fire into their bellies (Q 4:10). One thing has parallel forms in different worlds, as in a dream: "in dreams you see something while in reality it is something else. Both of them are right." <!-- src: L16 ¶49, ¶52, ¶54 -->

::: {.box-story title="Story: Yusuf reads the king's dream"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour explains dreams in this way. When the soul meets a meaning for which it has no picture, it clothes the meaning in a picture it already holds. Knowledge purifies, for instance, so a sleeper who is about to be given knowledge may see an ocean and find himself swimming in it. An interpreter then tells him what the picture answers to, and that, in his telling, is the whole idea of reading dreams. The king in Surah Yusuf saw seven fat cows devoured by seven lean ones (Q 12:43), and his courtiers could make nothing of it (Q 12:44). In his account, the king's soul had reached for strange pictures, "an image which is very weird", which no ordinary interpreter could read. The Prophet Yusuf (as) could. He read the cows as seven years of good harvests followed by seven hard years of famine (Q 12:47–48). On this book's reading, the fat cows and the years of plenty were one thing seen in two worlds.
:::

A believer who hears all this is left with fear and with hope, and the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah holds the two in a single sentence.

::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي إِنْ دَعَانِي إِلَىٰ ٱلنَّارِ عَظِيمُ عِقَابِكَ فَقَدْ دَعَانِي إِلَىٰ ٱلْجَنَّةِ جَزِيلُ ثَوَابِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, if Your severe punishment calls me to Hell, the abundance of Your reward invites me to Paradise. [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §120–121]
:::

## Going back now

:::: {.point}
ʿIrfan is making that return willingly and in this life.

1. Kubra: God has set two journeys, one involuntary and one voluntary; the mystic's path is the shortcut.
2. The lecture adds that those who take it have already arrived while others are still on the road; the Great Terror does not upset them (Q 21:103).
3. ʿIrfan is the return of the third section, made willingly and in this life.
4. The words of Q 2:156 belong to the living, who say them of themselves.
5. The book's question answered, ending on Surah al-Fajr's call to the self at peace (Q 89:27–28).
::::

If all of us make the return in the end, we need a reason to make it now. Najm al-Din Kubra, whose ship and pearl Chapter Six used for the law and the path, gives one in advice the lectures pass on. God, he says, has set one involuntary journey (*qahri*) and one voluntary journey (*ikhtiyari*) for the children of Adam. Everyone meets the Lord on a journey through death, barzakh, and the resurrection that is "out of your control". The mystic's path (*suluk*) is the same meeting made by choice, and Kubra calls it a shortcut. <!-- src: L08 ¶5-7 -->

Chapter Four gave one reason not to wait: the involuntary journey is longer and harder. The lecture gives a second. While others are still on that long road, those who took the voluntary one have already completed it and live beside God. In a verse of Surah al-Anbiyaʾ the Sheikh finds those who finished the return in this world. <!-- src: L08 ¶8; L16 ¶40 -->

::: {.ayah ref="21:103" words="1-4"}
The Great Terror will not upset them
:::

The Great Terror leaves them untouched because their return is already made. ʿIrfan is that return made willingly and in this life. The will is given back as contentment, power as reliance, knowledge as submission, and existence as annihilation. All four are given back inside the law and with a guide beside the traveller.

The words for that return are already on our tongues. At a death we say "to Him do we indeed return" of the one who has gone, but in Surah al-Baqarah the speakers are the living. The verse describes "those who, when an affliction visits them" say these words of themselves (Q 2:156).

This answers the book's opening question, whether there is more to the religion than the obligations and how a believer reaches it safely. There is more, and it is ʿirfan. It travels through the self without leaving the shariʿa, and it is walked in a known order beside a tested guide. Its roots are in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams from his Ahl al-Bayt. It ends in return, to people with God and to God Himself, who calls the self at peace home in Surah al-Fajr.

::: {.ayah ref="89:27-28"}
O soul at peace! Return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing!
:::

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour reports a hadith that in paradise the reader of the Qur'an is told to recite and go up. In his reading, one rises by how deeply the verses have been taken in rather than by how many one knows. Each day this week, read a few verses slowly. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi asks the reader to take every command, warning, and piece of advice in them "as though all of them have been revealed especially for him" [@journey, p. 46]. Then turn one verse over from several sides, as he does with a verse of Surah al-Waqiʿa, "Have you considered the water that you drink?" (Q 56:68). He thinks of the plants and animals made from that water, of the human being made from it, and of the water itself as a sign of God's blessing [@journey, pp. 49–50].
:::
