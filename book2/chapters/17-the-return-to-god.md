::: {.ayah ref="7:29" words="14-16"}
Even as He brought you forth in the beginning, so will you return.
:::

When someone in our family is dying, a relative often sits at the head of the bed and recites Surah Yasin. The Mafatih recommends reciting it "at the head of an ailing person", and Imam al-Sadiq (as) calls it "the heart of Quran" [@mafatih, p. 21]. When the end comes, those in the room say the words of Surah al-Baqarah that we say at every death.

::: {.ayah ref="2:156" words="6-10"}
Indeed we belong to Allah and to Him do we indeed return.
:::

These words name a return to God that every soul makes. Chapter Sixteen followed the friends of God back to people, and this chapter follows every soul back to God by the road it came, giving back what it was given. ʿIrfan is making that return willingly and in this life. The Qur'an tells of the return mostly in pictures of collars, chains, gardens, and fire. The surah recited at the bedside holds one of them, so the chapter first settles how such pictures are read. <!-- src: L09 ¶88; L16 ¶18-19 -->

::: {.words}
*taʾwil*: drawing out the inner meaning of a verse · *maʿad*: the return to God · *rida*: contentment with God's will · *tawakkul*: reliance on God · *taslim*: submission to God · *shafaʿa*: intercession
:::

## Reading from the inside

:::: {.point}
The Qur'an's pictures of the next world carry an inner meaning, which is read only where it agrees with the outer meaning and never cancels it.

1. No one wears the iron collars of Surah Yasin (Q 36:8); their meaning lies inside the picture: the light of God is covered for those who refuse to believe.
2. That does not license any meaning: a mystic's whim and a scheme brought from outside are both ruled out (the lectures; Ayatullah Khomeini).
3. What is allowed is an "insightful look", whose inner meaning agrees with the outer, and the outer stays.
4. The opposite mistake is also ruled out: treating the inner meaning as false closes the path too (Maliki Tabrizi). *(Word Study box.)*
5. The rule holds in the next world: hell's chain (Q 69:32) may be a real chain, and it is also the chain of long ambitions.
6. The Qur'an itself carries the inner reading across: blind there as here (Q 17:72), yet the people of hell see one another (Q 7:38).
7. Therefore: the pictures are read from the inside, and both meanings are kept.
::::

Near the start of Surah Yasin, the surah at the bedside, is a picture of those who refuse to believe.

::: {.ayah ref="36:8"}
Indeed We have put iron collars around their necks, which are up to the chins, so their heads are upturned.
:::

No unbeliever walks the street with an iron collar forcing his chin up, and no one reads the verse that way. The meaning lies inside the picture. The light of God is covered for such people, so they go through life without seeing what lies before them or behind them, and the next verse ends "so they do not see" (Q 36:9). <!-- src: L16 ¶9-12 -->

If a verse can mean more than its words, it might seem that it can be made to mean anything. The lectures rule out two kinds of reading. One gives a verse a meaning "at the will of some mystic" that has no tie to the verse itself. The other lays a scheme from some other system of thought over every verse in the Qur'an. Ayatullah Khomeini sets the same guard when he writes that "Islam does not allow anybody to interpret the Qur'an according to his personal opinion or private judgment". Among them he counts people who give the verses "some spiritual meaning" to suit their own wishes [@light, p. 110]. <!-- src: L16 ¶3-5 -->

What Sheikh Bahmanpour allows instead is "a type of insightful look at the verses of the Quran". Its rule is that the inner meaning must agree with the outer and never go against it, and the outer meaning stays when the inner one is found. Meaning in the Qur'an has layers, and beneath each layer we find a deeper one. The model is a parable (*mathal*) such as the Verse of Light from Chapter Two, whose niche and lamp bring a meaning about God closer to the mind. <!-- src: L16 ¶5-8 -->

Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi guards against the opposite mistake. To limit the Qur'an to its outward meaning and treat its inner meanings as false, he writes, "also closes the path of Qur'anic understanding" [@journey, p. 54]. <!-- src: L16 ¶5-8 -->

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: taʾwil and tafsir"}
*Tafsir* is commentary on the plain meaning of a verse, whereas *taʾwil* draws out its inner meaning. Maliki Tabrizi names the two together when he speaks of the Qur'an's meanings and "their commentary (tafsir) and esoteric meanings (tawil)" [@journey, p. 54]. An inner reading done well uncovers one side of the Qur'an and never claims the whole. Ayatullah Khomeini says of the commentaries the great gnostics wrote that "what they have written is not the exegesis of the Qur'an. At the most it can be said that they have exposed some aspects of it" [@light, p. 109]. He says the same of every commentator, since "The Qur'an is not a book all aspects of which may be exposed by us or by anybody else". What lies beyond us, he adds, is "to be explained by the Imams" [@light, p. 110].
:::

The verses about hell test the rule. Surah al-Haqqa describes a man led to the Fire, and the command given over him.

::: {.ayah ref="69:32"}
Then bind him in a chain, seventy cubits in length.
:::

If the collar of Surah Yasin is not seen on unbelievers in this world, the chain in the next may also be "something else". It may be the long chain of ambitions and ignorance that keeps them from seeing God. Both readings stand in the lectures, which grant that "we cannot deny that it might be the real material type of chain" and, in the same sentence, that "we cannot deny the deeper meanings as well". <!-- src: L16 ¶13-16 -->

The Qur'an itself carries the inner reading into the next world. Chapter Four quoted Surah al-Isra on the soul's sight, and the same surah says that whoever is blind in this world will be blind in the Hereafter (Q 17:72). Yet the people of hell are not sightless; every nation that enters the Fire curses its sister nation (Q 7:38), and the damned know one another well enough to quarrel. The blindness there is therefore the blindness of the heart we know here, and the chain there can be read as the collar here is read. <!-- src: L16 ¶16-17 -->

The Qur'an's pictures of the next world therefore carry an inner meaning, which is read only where it agrees with the outer meaning and never cancels it. This chapter reads the return in that way and keeps both meanings in view.

## Given in order, returned in reverse

:::: {.point}
Every soul returns to God by the road it came, giving back in reverse what it was given.

1. Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi reads hell and paradise from the inside and keeps their material side. *(Who's Who box.)*
2. For Tusi the return (*maʿad*) is the way we came, run backwards (Q 7:29).
3. We came by four gifts, in the order a child receives them. *(Reader told: "Let's start with how we came".)*
    a. Existence first (Q 40:67).
    b. Then knowledge (Q 16:78).
    c. Then power, and last will.
4. We go back will first, then power, knowledge, and existence, gradually.
5. Therefore: every soul returns by the road it came and gives back its gifts in reverse.
::::

Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi reads the verses on the return in this way. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls his account of hell and paradise a mystical reading that "doesn't deny the material aspect", and the reading asks what brings people to each. <!-- src: L16 ¶17 -->

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi"}
Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi died in 672/1273, the same year as Rumi [@light, p. 36]. He wrote the commentary on Ibn Sina's *Isharat* whose stages of the road Chapter Eight set beside its own [@light, p. 50]. Ayatullah Khomeini calls him "the best of the later scholars and the most perfect among the predecessors" [@forty_hadith, p. 517]. Sheikh Bahmanpour adds that Mulla Sadra translated long passages of Tusi's book on the return into his own *Asfar*. The reading of hell and paradise in this chapter comes from a scholar whom the great teachers of our tradition held in the highest regard.
:::

For Tusi, the return (*maʿad*) is a going back in reverse along the road by which we came. The verse at the head of this chapter, from Surah al-Aʿraf, says as much. As God brought us forth in the beginning, so we will return. <!-- src: L16 ¶18-19 -->

Let's start with how we came. Tusi traces the road by the way each of us entered the world, and he finds four gifts given in order. Existence comes first. Surah Ghafir follows each of us from dust to a drop of fluid, then to a clinging mass, and then out into the world as an infant (Q 40:67). <!-- src: L16 ¶20 -->

Knowledge follows. Surah al-Nahl says that God brought us forth from our mothers' bellies knowing nothing. He then made for us hearing, eyesight, and hearts (Q 16:78), and what we come to know reaches us through the senses. <!-- src: L16 ¶21 -->

Power comes third. At first a baby can do nothing for itself. Then it can shout or crawl for what it wants, and its power grows as the child grows. Will comes last, once the child knows it has the power: it wants something, and it goes and gets it. <!-- src: L16 ¶22 -->

The return runs the same road backwards. The will is given back first, then power, then knowledge, and last existence itself. None of this is asked of us at the start. For years we grow in all four, wanting things, reaching for them, and getting them. Then, gradually, God asks us to turn and go the other way. <!-- src: L16 ¶22-23 -->

Every soul therefore returns to God by the road it came. It gives back in reverse order its will, its power, its knowledge, and its existence.

## Will, power, knowledge, existence

:::: {.point}
Each gift given back becomes a station on the way to God, and God gives each back without limit.

1. The will given back is contentment (*rida*), with no separate will (Ayatullah Khomeini).
2. Contentment loses nothing: in paradise the soul has whatever it wishes (Q 50:35), and contentment is its key.
3. Power given back is reliance (*tawakkul*): the power we use is God's (Q 65:3).
4. Chapter Twelve saw that truth at the summit; reliance acts on it along the way.
5. Knowledge given back is submission (*taslim*), the submission of Ibrahim (Q 2:131), voiced in Duʿa ʿArafah.
6. Existence given back is annihilation (Chapter Twelve).
7. God gives all four back, new and without limit (Q 50:34).
8. This is Tusi's own teaching, which Ayatullah Khomeini quotes after a guard against union with God.
9. Therefore: the four given back become four stations, and their return from God is paradise.
::::

The will is the first gift given back, and the station it opens is contentment (*rida*). A soul with no will of its own wants nothing but what comes from God, and is content with it. For Ayatullah Khomeini, the one who has reached this station "is one who has annihilated his will in the Divine Will, having no more a separate will of his own". He recalls a mystic who, asked what he wished, replied, "My wish is not to wish at all" [@forty_hadith, p. 207]. <!-- src: L16 ¶23-24 -->

A soul that wants nothing for itself might seem to have nothing left to enjoy, but Surah Qaf says the opposite of those in paradise.

::: {.ayah ref="50:35"}
There they will have whatever they wish, and with Us there is yet more.
:::

Once our will has become God's will, whatever we wish we have, and the will comes back everlasting and joined to His. Tusi notes that in the tradition the angel in charge of paradise is called Ridwan. The name is built on the root of *rida*, so no one enters without contentment. Paradise is full of treasures, and in the lectures' words, "the key to it is this contentment". <!-- src: L16 ¶24-25 -->

Power is given back next, and its station is reliance on God (*tawakkul*). In its plain sense, Ayatullah Khomeini explains, *tawakkul* is the "admission of one's inability and reliance on one other than oneself" [@forty_hadith, p. 204]. Reliance could be mistaken for sitting and waiting for God to act, and the Sheikh rules that out: "whatever you do you know that this is not you. This is Him." The power we use is God's, and Surah al-Talaq promises that "whoever puts his trust in Allah, He will suffice him" (Q 65:3). <!-- src: L16 ¶27-28 -->

Chapter Twelve met the same truth at the summit of the road, where the traveller sees that there is no power and no strength except through God. Reliance is the same truth lived before it is seen. In every task the traveller takes up, he knows that the power in his hands is God's.

Knowledge goes back third, and its station is submission (*taslim*). The traveller knows that he knows nothing of himself. God alone knows, and whatever knowledge the traveller has is His gift. In Surah al-Baqarah this is the submission of the Prophet Ibrahim (as). <!-- src: L16 ¶28-30 -->

::: {.ayah ref="2:131"}
When his Lord said to him, ‘Submit,’ he said, ‘I submit to the Lord of all the worlds.’
:::

Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, puts the same submission into the words of a prayer.

::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي أَنَا ٱلْجَاهِلُ فِي عِلْمِي" source="Duʿa ʿArafah"}
O my God, I am ignorant despite my knowledge [@duas_dua_arafah_imam_husain, §670]
:::

The one who prays has knowledge and calls himself ignorant all the same, because none of that knowledge was his own.

Existence goes back last, and its station is annihilation (*fanaʾ*). There the traveller sees that existence belongs to God alone, and Chapter Twelve followed him to that station and back. <!-- src: L16 ¶30 -->

With all four given back, it might seem that nothing is left of the traveller. Tusi's answer is that God gives all four back, and gives them back new. Contentment is made complete. Power returns without limit, because the soul is now "drowned" in God's power and joined to it, and uses a power that remains His. Knowledge comes from nothing in the soul itself and "will shine in you", and existence becomes everlasting on the day Surah Qaf calls "the day of immortality" (Q 50:34). <!-- src: L16 ¶31-32 -->

This account is Tusi's own, and Ayatullah Khomeini quotes it from Tusi's commentary on Ibn Sina's *Isharat*. He sets it down just after a warning from Shaykh Bahaʾi that such sayings must never be taken to mean that God dwells in a servant or becomes one with him [@forty_hadith, pp. 516–517]. When the gnostic is cut off from himself and joined to God, Tusi writes, he "sees all powers as vanishing in Divine power and all sciences as drowned in Divine knowledge and all wills as vanishing in His will… At this time God, the Exalted, becomes his hearing and sight, power, knowledge and existence" [@forty_hadith, p. 517].

Given back willingly, the traveller's will, power, knowledge, and existence become contentment, reliance, submission, and annihilation. God gives each of them back without limit, and in Tusi's reading that is paradise. <!-- src: L16 ¶31 -->

## The return refused

:::: {.point}
The return happens whether or not a soul makes it: a believer who left it undone finishes it after death, helped by intercession; a will that never yields meets the four gifts as their opposites, which is hell, and it is not God's revenge.

1. The return happens anyway; a believer finishes it after death, with difficulty.
2. Intercession completes the believers' light (Q 66:8).
3. A will that never turns is overruled and held (Q 23:71); that is the seventy-cubit chain.
4. The other three gifts come back as their opposites (Q 87:13).
5. Hell is not God's revenge, and the wrath is still real.
6. The fire is still true: one reality takes a true form in each world (Q 4:10). *(Story box: Yusuf reads the king's dream.)*
7. Ayatullah Khomeini keeps both fires.
8. A believer holds fear and hope together (Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah).
9. Therefore: the return is made, willingly or not.
::::

Not all of us give these four back in this life, and in the lectures' words the return happens all the same, "either voluntarily or involuntarily". A believer who has not made it here makes it with "lots of fears, lots of difficulty" in barzakh and on the Day of Resurrection. Barzakh is the middle world that Chapter Five described. There the will is taken from him, and he is shown that there is no power and no life but God's. <!-- src: L16 ¶39-41 -->

A believer cannot finish that road alone. In this account paradise is above our own level, and without intercession (*shafaʿa*) it cannot be entered; it takes someone great to bring us there. Surah al-Tahrim shows the believers on the Last Day with a light running before them, but the light is not complete, and they ask for it to be made whole. <!-- src: L16 ¶34, ¶37 -->

::: {.ayah ref="66:8" words="34-38"}
They will say, ‘Our Lord! Perfect our light for us
:::

That light is completed through the Prophet ﷺ. The same verse says that on that day God "will not let down the Prophet and the faithful who are with him", and the Sheikh reads this as a promise against humiliation. If the Prophet's own followers went to hell, the shame would fall on him, though he has done no wrong, and so God completes their light through him. <!-- src: L16 ¶37-39 -->

A will that never turns towards God meets a different end. It carries its opposition into the next world, and there God's will prevails over it. Surah al-Muʾminun says why.

::: {.ayah ref="23:71" words="1-9"}
Had the truth followed their desires, the heavens and the earth would have surely fallen apart [along] with those who are in them.
:::

A will that is never followed is held in check, and its owner is "always in torment". The chain of seventy cubits from the first section is this held will, and its length, the lectures say, is "because of the length of the will that they have". <!-- src: L16 ¶42-43 -->

The other three gifts return in the same way, each as the mirror image of the station it would have opened. In place of contentment there is utter dissatisfaction. In place of power there is helplessness, because these souls never learned that the power they used in this world was God's, and they do not know how to use it there. Those who thought they knew find themselves ignorant, and humiliation stands where submission would have been. Last, in place of union with God there is separation from Him, and an existence that is neither life nor death. Surah al-Aʿla says this of the one who enters the Great Fire. <!-- src: L16 ¶45-47, ¶54-55 -->

::: {.ayah ref="87:13"}
then he will neither live in it, nor die.
:::

Hell might then look like revenge, and the Sheikh answers that reading: "It is not a punishment which is brought to you by God. God doesn't want to revenge." Everything is God's creation, and only mercy comes from Him. Such a soul has made itself unable to use God's will, power, and knowledge. It brings that state back with it, and separation from Him follows. Yet the wrath is real. In his own words, those who set their will against God's all their lives "will deserve the wrath of God". <!-- src: L16 ¶43, ¶47-48 -->

If hell is the soul's own separation, the fire and the boiling water might seem to be only pictures. The lectures answer that "of course it's all true", because one reality takes a true form in each world. Surah al-Nisaʾ says that those who consume the property of orphans ingest fire into their bellies (Q 4:10), so the wrong done here is fire in a deeper world. Chapter Five found in the same way that some rewards are the deeds themselves made visible. A dream shows how two forms of one thing can both be true: "in dreams you see something while in reality it is something else. Both of them are right." <!-- src: L16 ¶49, ¶51-52, ¶54 -->

::: {.box-story title="Story: Yusuf reads the king's dream"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour explains dreams in this way. When the soul meets a meaning for which it has no picture, it clothes the meaning in a picture it already holds. Knowledge purifies, for instance, so a sleeper who is about to be given knowledge may see an ocean and find himself swimming in it. An interpreter then tells him what the picture answers to, and that, in his telling, is the whole idea of reading dreams. The king in Surah Yusuf saw seven fat cows devoured by seven lean ones (Q 12:43), and his courtiers could make nothing of it (Q 12:44). In his account, the king's soul had reached for strange pictures, "an image which is very weird", which no ordinary interpreter could read. The Prophet Yusuf (as) could. He read the cows as seven years of good harvests followed by seven hard years of famine (Q 12:47–48). The fat cows and the years of plenty were one thing seen in two worlds, and the one who knew could read the one from the other.
:::

Ayatullah Khomeini reads the fire of hell in the same two ways. He calls the fire of Divine Wrath in the next world "the Hereafterly form of our anger in this world", and he keeps the outer fire beside the inner one. In his words, "The fire of the hell of deeds and the physical hell encompasses the without and travels towards the within" [@forty_hadith, p. 137]. The soul meets both, one rising from within the heart and the other coming in from without.

A believer who hears all this is left with fear and with hope, and the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah holds the two in a single sentence.

::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي إِنْ دَعَانِي إِلَىٰ ٱلنَّارِ عَظِيمُ عِقَابِكَ فَقَدْ دَعَانِي إِلَىٰ ٱلْجَنَّةِ جَزِيلُ ثَوَابِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, if Your severe punishment calls me to Hell, the abundance of Your reward invites me to Paradise. [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §120–121]
:::

Both calls in the prayer come from God, and the sentence ends on the one that invites to paradise.

The return is therefore made whether or not a soul makes it willingly. A believer who left it undone finishes it after death, with difficulty, and intercession helps him through. A will that never yields meets its four gifts as their opposites. That is hell, and the separation in it is the soul's own rather than God's revenge.

## Going back now

:::: {.point}
ʿIrfan is making that return willingly, in this life.

1. Kubra: God has set two journeys, one involuntary and one voluntary; the mystic's path is the shortcut.
2. Those who take it arrive while others are still on the road; the Great Terror does not upset them (Q 21:103).
3. ʿIrfan is the return of the third section made now, by choice.
4. The words of Q 2:156 belong to the living, who say them of themselves.
5. The book's question answered.
6. Surah al-Fajr calls such a soul home (Q 89:27–28).
::::

If all of us make the return in the end, we need a reason to make it now. Najm al-Din Kubra, whose ship and pearl Chapter Six used for the law and the path, gives one in advice the lectures pass on. God, he says, has set one involuntary journey (*qahri*) and one voluntary journey (*ikhtiyari*) for the children of Adam. Everyone meets the Lord on a journey through death, barzakh, and the resurrection that is "out of your control". The mystic's path (*suluk*) is the same meeting made by choice, and Kubra calls it a shortcut. <!-- src: L08 ¶5-7 -->

Chapter Four gave one reason not to wait: the involuntary journey is longer and harder. Kubra adds another. While others are still on that long road, those who took the voluntary one have already arrived and live beside God. In a verse of Surah al-Anbiyaʾ the Sheikh finds those who finished the return in this world. <!-- src: L08 ¶8; L16 ¶40 -->

::: {.ayah ref="21:103" words="1-4"}
The Great Terror will not upset them
:::

The Great Terror leaves them untouched because their return is already made. ʿIrfan, at the end of this book, is that return made now and by choice. The will is given back as contentment, power as reliance, knowledge as submission, and existence as annihilation. All four are given back inside the law and with a guide beside the traveller.

The words for that return are already on our tongues. At a death we say "to Him do we indeed return" of the one who has gone, but in Surah al-Baqarah the speakers are the living. The verse describes "those who, when an affliction visits them" say these words of themselves (Q 2:156). The traveller says them of himself now and means them.

This answers the question the book set out with, whether there is more to the religion than the obligations and how a believer reaches it safely. There is more, and it is ʿirfan, which is worth every believer's pursuit. It travels through the self and never leaves the shariʿa. It is walked in a known order beside a tested guide. Its roots are in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams from his Ahl al-Bayt. It ends in return: to people with God, as Chapter Sixteen showed, and to God Himself.

The return to God Himself has its own words in Surah al-Fajr, which speaks to the self at peace that Chapter Five described.

::: {.ayah ref="89:27-28"}
O soul at peace! Return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing!
:::

The verse's word for "pleased" (*radiya*) names one who has reached contentment, which is where the giving back began. Ayatullah Khomeini calls such a person "the radi (one who has attained the station of rida)" [@forty_hadith, p. 207].

How, then, does a believer begin this week?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour reports a hadith that in paradise the reader of the Qur'an is told to recite and go up. In his reading, one rises by how deeply the verses have been taken in rather than by how many one knows. Each day this week, read a few verses slowly. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi asks the reader to take every command, warning, and piece of advice in them "as though all of them have been revealed especially for him" [@journey, p. 46]. Then turn one verse over from several sides, as he does with a verse of Surah al-Waqiʿa, "Have you considered the water that you drink?" (Q 56:68). He thinks of the plants and animals made from that water, of the human being made from it, and of the water itself as a sign of God's blessing [@journey, pp. 49–50].
:::
