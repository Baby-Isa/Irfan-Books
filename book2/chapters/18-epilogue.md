::: {.ayah ref="3:17" words="5-6"}
and they plead for [Allah’s] forgiveness at dawns.
:::

In a single verse, Surah Al ʿImran gives five marks of the people it praises. They are patient, truthful, obedient, and charitable. The fifth mark, in the words above, is that they plead for God's forgiveness at dawns. Sheikh Bahmanpour reports that someone once asked Imam al-Sadiq (as) how long a believer has to keep at it to reach that height. As he tells it, the answer was "one year is enough" [VERIFY: a printed source for this hadith of Imam al-Sadiq (as), if it is ever given as a hadith rather than as the Sheikh's report, L10 ¶26].
<!-- src: L10 ¶26 -->

That answer is a length of time rather than a feat, and it leaves open what is kept for the year and how we begin it this week, where Chapter Seventeen left off. Each chapter has offered one small step in its Try this box. The epilogue gathers them into the first steps of the road and sets them on three rungs: a week, a month, and a year. The rungs are the book's own arrangement, and they rest on the lectures' advice to grow gradually and on the year the teachers of this road asked of a student. Let's begin with the rules that keep every rung safe.
<!-- src: L10 ¶23, ¶26 -->

::: {.words}
*wajibat*: the obligatory acts · *tawba*: repentance, turning back to God · *istighfar*: asking God's forgiveness · *muhasaba*: the account of the day, taken each night · Musabbihat: the five surahs that open with God's glorification · Tasbih of al-Zahraʾ: the remembrance said after each obligatory prayer · Dhu'l-Qaʿdah: the eleventh month of the Islamic calendar
:::

## How to begin

:::: {.point}
Every step stands inside the law, suits the traveller, is small enough to keep, and grows gradually; a beginner need not wait for a guide, but tests any guide he follows.

1. The obligations come first: the law kept strictly is the first stage, and it holds to the last breath (Chapters Six and Nine, in words; L07 ¶15); after repentance, its first demand is putting things right with people (L08 ¶39–40).
2. Of the many recommended acts, one that suits the person: review your state, then "select the proper program which is suitable for him" (Maliki Tabrizi); Chapter Ten's dose measured to the person.
3. How much: a little, kept up; the measure is ours, not the Prophet's ﷺ (Chapter Ten, in words).
4. Story (D47): asked about Imam ʿAli's hard bread, the Sheikh answers "he's in level 1000 we just want to start level one" (L08 ¶82–83).
5. The Imam's own letter: "You will not be able to do what I do—but help me by practicing restraint and striving for good" (Nahj 2.45.2); what cannot be done in full is done "as much as possible" (Maliki Tabrizi).
6. Never rushed: the young man of Chapter Nine in a clause; "a linear gradual improvement" (L08 ¶26).
7. No need to wait for the guide who reads the soul's states: a teacher of practices is enough to begin (Chapter Seven's accounts in a clause, D20).
8. Any teacher is tested as Chapter Seven tests a guide: the law rules out; wonders prove nothing; long company; where he turns people.
9. Therefore: begin inside the law, with one act that suits you, small enough to keep and growing gradually, beside a tested companion if one is found.
::::

Every step on this road stands on the obligations (*wajibat*) that we already keep. Chapter Nine called the law kept strictly the first stage of the road, which it named polishing. Chapter Six showed that the law holds to the last breath, while only the reason for keeping it changes. The lectures put the first step simply, as complying completely with whatever the shariʿa says. Recommended acts are built on the obligations and never stand in for them. Within the law, as Chapter Nine also showed, one demand comes first after repentance: putting things right with the people around us.
<!-- src: L07 ¶15; L08 ¶39-40 -->

The recommended acts are many, and a beginner has to choose among them. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi tells the wayfarer who wants to take them on to review his situation first. He looks at his affairs and his workload to find out whether his mood is low, moderate, or high. Only then, he writes, "he should select the proper program which is suitable for him" [@journey, p. 109]. Chapter Ten made the same point through medicine, where each discipline is taken in a dose measured to the person.

Chapter Ten has also answered how much of the chosen act to take on, and its answer was to take a little and keep it up. Half an hour is enough to begin the night prayer; going past the limit ends in giving up altogether. The measure is our own rather than the Prophet's ﷺ, whose practice called for a personality like his.
<!-- src: L10 ¶23-25 -->

A listener once asked the Sheikh about the same measure in the case of Imam ʿAli (as). He had heard that the Imam's bread was so hard that people likened it to rock, and that the Imam told others not to follow him in it. The Sheikh's answer was that "he's in level 1000 we just want to start level one", and that the Imam's words to others are "instruction for level one".
<!-- src: L08 ¶82-83 -->

In a letter to his governor of Basra, Imam ʿAli (as) says the same in his own words. Content for his share of the world with "his two shabby garments and his two pieces of bread", he writes, "You will not be able to do what I do—but help me by practicing restraint and striving for good" [@nahj, 2.45.2, p. 626]. Restraint and striving are measured by what a follower can do, and Maliki Tabrizi adds that an act which cannot be done in full is not dropped for that reason. Instead, "he should perform it, as much as possible" [@journey, p. 161].

An act that goes well invites the opposite mistake, which is to take on more and to take it faster. Chapter Nine told of the young man in Tehran whose daily Ziyarat ʿAshura put a light in his face, until an elder of the path told his father to make him stop. The young man was rushing. What the elder wanted was "a linear gradual improvement" in place of a sudden burst of effort that leaves a person feeling abandoned by his own soul.
<!-- src: L08 ¶24-26 -->

Many who want to set out look first for a guide and, finding none, conclude that they cannot begin. Chapter Seven set side by side the scholars' accounts of how early a guide is needed. In the Sheikh's account the first steps are the law, and the guide who reads the soul's states comes after them. Before then a beginner needs a teacher who says which practices to begin with, as Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi set his beginners the Musabbihat. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's general teacher, needed at the beginning of the road, is consulted as one consults anyone learned.
<!-- src: L07 ¶15-16 -->

Any teacher who is found is tested as Chapter Seven tested a guide. One who breaks the law is ruled out, and wonders prove nothing about him. Only long company shows how far he has travelled, and where he turns people shows whether he is true.

A beginner therefore starts inside the law with one act that suits the traveller, small enough to keep and growing gradually, and beside a tested companion if one is found.

## This week

:::: {.point}
Begin with repentance, then take one act of a few minutes, fixed to the time of day in which the heart is already most present.

1. The first act is repentance: the prayer of Chapter Nine, allowed at any time, "the first step" (L08 ¶29–33).
2. One act follows, and Chapter Fifteen's test picks its time: of the acts we already keep, the one in which the heart is most present (L14 ¶70–71).
3. The chapters' steps fall around four such times. *(Reader told: "four times".)*
    a. In and after the prayer (Chapters Two, Four, Six, Ten, and Twelve).
    b. With the Qur'an and the duʿas (Chapters One, Three, Eleven, and Seventeen).
    c. At night (Chapters Five, Eight, Thirteen, and Fourteen).
    d. Among people (Chapters Seven and Sixteen).
4. Therefore: this week, repentance once, then one act of minutes each day, at the time the heart is already most present. *(Try this box: the table.)*
::::

The week opens with repentance (*tawba*), which Chapter Nine showed is open to anyone and is where the first stage begins. For setting out, the Sheikh suggests the prayer of repentance that the Prophet ﷺ taught for a Sunday in Dhu'l-Qaʿdah. The traditionists allow it at any time, and the lectures call it "the first step". Chapter Nine's Try this sets out its form from the Mafatih, with a ritual bath and wudu, four rakʿas, and asking God's forgiveness (*istighfar*) [@mafatih, p. 356]. It is prayed once, as the road begins.
<!-- src: L08 ¶29-33 -->

After repentance comes one daily act, and Chapter Fifteen's test chooses its time. The test looks at the acts we already keep each day (the salah, the Qur'an, or a duʿa) and notices in which of them the heart is most present. The new act is then fixed to that time. Chapter Fifteen grounded the test in the lectures' advice that every traveller should find a practice that suits them, since no two travellers are alike. One act at that time is enough for the first week.
<!-- src: L14 ¶70-71 -->

The chapters' steps fall around four times in our day: the prayer, the Qur'an and the duʿas, the night, and the company of other people. The table below gives every step with its source, and the first week needs only one of them.

Most of the steps belong to the prayer, which we already keep five times a day. Chapter Two asked us to say the dhikr of prostration slowly in one prayer a day and to believe it as we say it. Chapter Six asked for one prayer at the start of its time, and for that time to be kept free of other plans. Chapter Four's closing verses of Surah al-Hashr follow one daily prayer, and Chapter Ten's Tasbih of al-Zahraʾ follows each obligatory one. When we recite Surah al-Ikhlas in the prayer, Chapter Twelve's step is a pause on the word after "Say", *Huwa*.

Where the heart is most present with the Qur'an and the duʿas, the steps are readings. Chapter One's four verses of Surah al-Rum are read slowly once a day, and Chapter Three's petitions from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah are read once with the English beside the Arabic. Chapter Eleven offers the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers by Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as), which is read once and slowly. Chapter Seventeen asks for a few verses a day, read as though revealed to the reader alone, and for one of them to be turned over from several sides.

Other steps belong to the night, as we lie down to sleep. Chapter Five gives the intention *Lantern of the Path* sets for sleep. Chapter Thirteen gives the account of the day (*muhasaba*), which asks forgiveness for any fault and thanks God if there is none. Chapter Eight's step is one of the Musabbihat before sleep. When that surah is al-Hadid, Chapter Fourteen adds a stop at its third verse, "He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden" (Q 57:3). Once in the day, something in creation may stop us. What we see then is His manifestation, but it is not He.
<!-- src: L13 ¶64-65 -->

The last of the four times is spent among other people. Chapter Seven asks ʿIsa's three questions of the company we keep, and Chapter Sixteen asks that our duʿa name other believers first and only then turn to our own needs.

The first week therefore holds repentance once and then one act of a few minutes each day, which is kept at the time when the heart is already most present.

## This month

:::: {.point}
Keep that one act daily for a month; Shaʿban, Ramadan, and Dhu'l-Qaʿdah give it a longer form, within what the traveller can carry.

1. The month is the middle rung: the week's act, kept daily at its fixed time, with nothing added (the book's arrangement of "gradually").
2. Three months lengthen the act rather than add to it. *(Reader told: "three months".)*
    a. Shaʿban: the whole Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, as much as one can (L01 ¶22; Chapter Three).
    b. Ramadan: the verses read as revealed to oneself, every day of the month (Chapter Seventeen; Maliki Tabrizi's Ramadan etiquette); the disciplines already kept (Chapter Ten).
    c. Dhu'l-Qaʿdah: the prayer of repentance at its best time (Chapter Nine).
3. Heavier programmes are the ceiling: the thousand rakʿas for one whose "spiritual condition and mood allow him"; the Night of Power likewise (Maliki Tabrizi).
4. Therefore: this month, one act daily, lengthened by Shaʿban, Ramadan, or Dhu'l-Qaʿdah within what the traveller can carry.
::::

Once the first week has shown that the act can be kept, the month keeps it. Because the road is taken gradually, the month is the middle rung, and on it the week's one act is kept each day at its fixed time with nothing yet added.

Three months of the year give that act a longer form when one of them falls within the month being kept, and in each the act is lengthened rather than added to. In Shaʿban, Chapter Three's petitions grow into the whole Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. The lectures advise reciting it through that month as much as one can, and Chapter Three asked for the English to be read beside the Arabic.
<!-- src: L01 ¶22 -->

In Ramadan, the month of the Qur'an, Chapter Seventeen's step of reading a few verses as though revealed to oneself can be kept every day. Maliki Tabrizi gives that rule in his etiquette for reciting the Qur'an during Ramadan. As Chapter Ten observed, in Ramadan we already keep most of the disciplines without naming them.

Dhu'l-Qaʿdah brings back the prayer of repentance from the first week, on a Sunday of the month, which is its best time.
<!-- src: L08 ¶30 -->

Some programmes for these months ask far more of the traveller. In Maliki Tabrizi's programme for Ramadan, the thousand rakʿas of supererogatory prayer are for the wayfarer whose "spiritual condition and mood allow him" [@journey, p. 109], and the hundred rakʿas he sets for the Night of Power are of the same kind [@journey, p. 155]. Such programmes are the month's ceiling rather than its starting point, and the rules this epilogue began with still govern them.

The month therefore keeps the one act daily. When Shaʿban, Ramadan, or Dhu'l-Qaʿdah falls within it, that month gives the act its longer form within what the traveller can carry.

## This year

:::: {.point}
Keep one practice for a year and do not stop when the year ends; the keeping itself is asked of God, in the Munajat's words.

1. A year is the teachers' measure (Chapter Ten, in words); Qadi's year of the Musabbihat (Chapter Eight, a clause).
2. The practice: the month's, or Chapter Ten's al-Muzzammil each night or the night prayer from half an hour.
3. The opening answered: in the Sheikh's account, a year brings the traveller under the verse's title, those who plead for forgiveness at dawns (L10 ¶26–27; Q 3:17 recalled); kept in hope, since the title is God's to give.
4. The year is no end: it "doesn't mean that after one year you stop it"; it may bring "more power to ask for more" (L10 ¶27); each rung kept, as no stage is dropped (Chapter Eight).
5. A small act counts because God accepts it: "accepts deed, and not its being small or big" (Maliki Tabrizi).
6. Therefore: begin inside the law with one small act that suits you; keep it a week, a month, and a year, never rushed and never dropped; and ask God for the keeping.

Close: the petition at the head of Part Five in words; end on Munajat §124, "make me one of those who always remember You". Stop.
::::

The last rung is a year, which is how long the teachers of this road asked a student to keep one practice, as Chapter Ten recalled. Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, as Chapter Eight showed, gave his beginners the Musabbihat every night for a year before giving them a second instruction.
<!-- src: L10 ¶26; L07 ¶16 -->

The practice for the year can be the one the month has kept. Chapter Ten offered two others from the lectures: Surah al-Muzzammil each night, which takes about three minutes, and the night prayer begun at half an hour.
<!-- src: L10 ¶23, ¶25 -->

What the year gives is the height the opening verse described. In the Sheikh's account, a year of the night prayer or of asking forgiveness in the heart brings the traveller under the verse's title of those who plead for forgiveness at dawns. The year is kept in that hope, since the title is God's to give.
<!-- src: L10 ¶26-27 -->

The year is no finish line, and the lectures say so plainly. Keeping a practice for a year "doesn't mean that after one year you stop it", since stopping would let the traveller slip back to a lower place. What the year may bring instead, in the lectures' words, is "more power to ask for more". Each rung is kept as the next is added. Chapter Eight found the same of the four stages, where none is dropped when the next begins.
<!-- src: L10 ¶27 -->

A single act of a few minutes, kept for a year, can still seem too slight to count for much. Maliki Tabrizi turns the question from the size of the deed to the One who receives it: "what is important and useful for the servant is that Allah (the Glorious, the Exalted), accepts deed, and not its being small or big" [@journey, p. 161]. He adds that Allah accepts even insignificant deeds and rewards them handsomely.

The first steps therefore begin inside the law with one small act that suits the traveller. It is kept for a week, a month, and a year without being rushed or dropped, and the keeping itself is asked of God.

Part Five opened with a petition from near the end of the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. It asks to be joined to the light of God's majestic glory, so as to know Him alone and turn away from all else, and to have a heart fearful of Him and an eye watchful of Him.

A few lines earlier, the same prayer asks for the constancy on which every rung depends. Chapter Six began from the words that follow this request, which ask never to fail to thank God and never to take His commands lightly. The request itself puts the keeping in God's hands:
