::: {.ayah ref="3:17" words="5-6"}
and they plead for [Allah’s] forgiveness at dawns.
:::

In a single verse, Surah Al ʿImran gives five marks of the people it praises. They are patient, truthful, obedient, and charitable. The fifth mark, in the words above, is that they plead for God's forgiveness at dawns. Sheikh Bahmanpour reports that someone once asked Imam al-Sadiq (as) how long a believer has to keep at it to reach that height. As he tells it, the answer was "one year is enough".
<!-- src: L10 ¶26 -->

The Imam's answer leaves open what is kept for the year and how a believer begins this week. Each chapter has offered one small step in its Try this box, down to Chapter Seventeen's few verses read slowly each day. The epilogue sets those steps on three rungs: a week, a month, and a year. The rungs are the book's own arrangement, and they rest on the lectures' advice to grow gradually. Let's begin with the rules that keep every rung safe.
<!-- src: L10 ¶23, ¶26 -->

::: {.words}
*wajibat*: the obligatory acts · *tawba*: repentance, turning back to God · *istighfar*: asking God's forgiveness · *muhasaba*: the account of the day, taken each night · Musabbihat: the five surahs that open with God's glorification · Tasbih of al-Zahraʾ: the remembrance said after each obligatory prayer · Dhu'l-Qaʿdah: the eleventh month of the Islamic calendar
:::

## How to begin

:::: {.point}
Every step stands inside the law, suits the traveller, is small enough to keep, and grows gradually; a beginner need not wait for a guide, but tests any guide he follows.

1. The obligations come first: the law kept strictly is the first stage, and it holds to the last breath (Chapters Six and Nine, in words; L07 ¶15); the extra acts are kept as well, and nothing goes outside the shariʿa (L07 ¶15, ¶17); after repentance, the law's first demand is putting things right with people (L08 ¶39–40).
2. Of the many recommended acts, one that suits the person: no one can do them all (L07 ¶18); review your state, then "select the proper program which is suitable for him" (Maliki Tabrizi, in his Ramadan programme); Chapter Ten's dose measured to the person.
3. How much: a little, kept up; the measure is ours, not the Prophet's ﷺ (Chapter Ten, in words).
4. Story (D47): asked about Imam ʿAli's hard bread, the Sheikh answers "he's in level 1000 we just want to start level one" (L08 ¶82–83); the Imam's own letter: "You will not be able to do what I do—but help me by practicing restraint and striving for good" (Nahj 2.45.2).
5. Never rushed: the young man of Chapter Nine in a clause; the lecture asks for "a linear gradual improvement" and compares rushing with a sudden three months' fast (L08 ¶26, as Ch 9).
6. No need to wait for the guide who reads the soul's states: a teacher of practices is enough to begin (Chapter Seven's accounts in a clause, D20).
7. A teacher who is followed closely is tested as Chapter Seven tests a guide: the law rules out; wonders prove nothing; long company; where he turns people.
8. Therefore: begin inside the law, with one act that suits you, small enough to keep and growing gradually, beside a tested companion if one is found.
::::

Every step on this road stands on the obligations (*wajibat*) that we already keep. Chapter Nine called the law kept strictly the first stage of the road, and Chapter Six showed that it holds to the last breath. The lectures put the first step as complying completely with whatever the shariʿa says. A traveller then takes on the further acts of the hadith as well, and none of them goes outside the shariʿa. Within the law, the first demand after repentance is putting things right with the people around us.
<!-- src: L07 ¶15, ¶17; L08 ¶39-40 -->

No one can keep all the recommended acts, the lectures point out, so a beginner has to choose among them. In his programme for Ramadan, Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi first has the wayfarer review his affairs and his workload to gauge his mood as low, moderate, or high. Only then, he writes, "he should select the proper program which is suitable for him" [@journey, p. 109]. Chapter Ten made the same point through medicine, where each discipline is taken in a dose measured to the person.
<!-- src: L07 ¶18 -->

Chapter Ten also answered how much to take on: a little, kept up. Half an hour is enough to begin the night prayer, and going past the limit ends in giving up altogether. The measure is our own rather than the Prophet's ﷺ, whose practice called for a personality like his.
<!-- src: L10 ¶23-25 -->

A listener once put the same measure to the Sheikh in the case of Imam ʿAli (as), whose bread was so hard that he told people not to follow him in it. The Sheikh answered that "he's in level 1000 we just want to start level one". The Imam says as much in a letter to his governor of Basra. Content for his share of the world with "his two shabby garments and his two pieces of bread", he writes, "You will not be able to do what I do—but help me by practicing restraint and striving for good" [@nahj, 2.45.2, p. 626].
<!-- src: L08 ¶82-83 -->

An act that goes well invites the opposite mistake, which is taking on more and taking it faster. Chapter Nine told of the young man in Tehran whose daily Ziyarat ʿAshura put a light in his face, until an elder of the path told his father to make him stop because he was rushing. The lecture asks instead for "a linear gradual improvement", and compares such rushing with a sudden three months of fasting that leaves a person feeling abandoned by their own soul.
<!-- src: L08 ¶24-26 -->

A beginner who looks for a guide and finds none may conclude that the road is closed. Chapter Seven set the scholars' accounts side by side. In the Sheikh's, the first steps are the law, and the guide who reads the soul's states comes after them. Before then a beginner needs a teacher who says which practices to begin with, as Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi set his beginners the Musabbihat. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's general teacher is of this kind, consulted as one consults anyone learned [@kernel, p. 80].
<!-- src: L07 ¶15-16 -->

A teacher who is followed closely is tested as Chapter Seven tested a guide. One who breaks the law is ruled out, and wonders prove nothing about him. Only long company shows how far he has travelled, and where he turns people shows whether he is true.

## This week

:::: {.point}
Begin with repentance, then take one act of a few minutes, fixed to the time of day in which the heart is already most present.

1. The first act is repentance, which goes on as long as the road (Chapter Nine: a continuous process); the prayer of repentance marks the start, allowed at any time, "the first step" (L08 ¶29–33).
2. One act follows, and Chapter Fifteen's test picks its time: of the acts we already keep, the one in which the heart is most present (L14 ¶70–71).
3. The chapters' steps fall around four such times. *(Reader told: "four times".)*
    a. In and after the prayer (Chapters Two, Four, Six, Ten, and Twelve).
    b. With the Qur'an and the duʿas (Chapters One, Three, Eleven, and Seventeen).
    c. At night (Chapters Five, Eight, Thirteen, and Fourteen).
    d. Among people (Chapters Seven and Sixteen).
4. Therefore: this week, the prayer of repentance to set out, then one act of minutes each day, at the time the heart is already most present. *(Try this box: the table.)*
::::

The week opens with repentance (*tawba*), which Chapter Nine showed is open to anyone and begins the first stage. Chapter Nine also called it a continuous process, renewed whenever the traveller falls. To set out, the Sheikh suggests the prayer of repentance that the Prophet ﷺ taught for a Sunday in Dhu'l-Qaʿdah. The traditionists allow it at any time, and the lectures call it "the first step". Chapter Nine's Try this sets out its form from the Mafatih [@mafatih, p. 356].
<!-- src: L08 ¶13-15, ¶29-33 -->

After repentance comes one daily act, timed by Chapter Fifteen's test. Of the acts we already keep each day, the test looks for the one in which the heart is most present, and the new act is fixed to that time. Every traveller, the lectures advise, should find a practice of their own, since the way each of us finds to God differs from that of the person sitting beside us. One act is enough for the first week.
<!-- src: L14 ¶70-71 -->

The chapters' steps fall around four times in our day: the prayer, the Qur'an and the duʿas, the night, and the company of other people. The box below gives every step with its source, and the first week needs only one of them.

Five of the steps belong to the prayer, which we already keep five times a day: Chapter Two's dhikr of prostration, said slowly in one prayer a day; Chapter Six's one prayer in the most meritorious part of its time, with that time kept free; Chapter Four's closing verses of Surah al-Hashr after one daily prayer; Chapter Ten's Tasbih of al-Zahraʾ after each obligatory one; and Chapter Twelve's pause on *Huwa* in Surah al-Ikhlas.

With the Qur'an and the duʿas, the steps are readings. Chapter One's four verses of Surah al-Rum are read once a day, Chapter Three's petitions from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah with the English beside the Arabic, and Chapter Eleven's Whispered Prayer of the Lovers once and slowly. Chapter Seventeen asks for a few verses a day, read as though revealed to the reader alone, with one turned over from several sides.

Other steps belong to the night. Chapter Five gives the intention *Lantern of the Path* sets for sleep, and Chapter Thirteen the account of the day (*muhasaba*), with forgiveness asked for any fault and thanks given if there is none. Chapter Eight's step is one of the Musabbihat before sleep. When that surah is al-Hadid, Chapter Fourteen adds a stop at its third verse, "He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden" (Q 57:3). It also asks for a daily recollection: what we see in creation is His manifestation, but it is not He.
<!-- src: L13 ¶64-65 -->

The last of the four times is spent among other people. Chapter Seven asks ʿIsa's three questions of the company we keep, and Chapter Sixteen asks that our duʿa name other believers first and only then turn to our own needs.

## This month

:::: {.point}
Keep that one act daily for a month; Shaʿban, Ramadan, and Dhu'l-Qaʿdah give it a fuller form, within what the traveller can carry.

1. The month is the middle rung: the week's act, kept daily at its fixed time, with nothing added (the book's arrangement of "gradually").
2. Three months give the act a fuller form rather than add to it. *(Reader told: "three months".)*
    a. Shaʿban: the whole Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, as much as one can (L01 ¶22; Chapter Three).
    b. Ramadan: Maliki Tabrizi's month of the Qur'an, whose etiquette of recital is Chapter Seventeen's step [@journey, pp. 45–46]; a longer reading (the book's arrangement).
    c. Dhu'l-Qaʿdah: a Sunday of it is the best time for the prayer of repentance, for one who sets out then (L08 ¶30).
3. Heavier programmes are the ceiling: the thousand rakʿas for one whose "spiritual condition and mood allow him"; the Night of Power's hundred likewise a large programme (Maliki Tabrizi).
4. Therefore: this month, one act daily, given a fuller form by Shaʿban, Ramadan, or Dhu'l-Qaʿdah within what the traveller can carry.
::::

The month is the middle rung. Once the first week has shown that the act can be kept, the month keeps it each day at its fixed time, and because the road is taken gradually nothing is yet added.

When Shaʿban, Ramadan, or Dhu'l-Qaʿdah falls within the month being kept, it gives the act a fuller form rather than adding to it. In Shaʿban, Chapter Three's petitions grow into the whole Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. The lectures advise reciting it through that month as much as one can.
<!-- src: L01 ¶22 -->

Maliki Tabrizi calls Ramadan a month of the Qur'an and supplications, and for that month he sets out the etiquette of recital that Chapter Seventeen's step follows: each verse is read as though revealed to oneself [@journey, pp. 45–46]. In that month the few verses a day can grow into a longer reading.

The lectures give a Sunday of Dhu'l-Qaʿdah as the best time for the prayer of repentance, and a reader setting out in that month can pray it then.
<!-- src: L08 ¶30 -->

Some of what Maliki Tabrizi sets for these months asks far more. His thousand rakʿas of supererogatory prayer in Ramadan are for the wayfarer whose "spiritual condition and mood allow him" [@journey, p. 109], and the hundred rakʿas he sets for the Night of Power are likewise a large programme [@journey, p. 155]. They are the month's ceiling rather than its starting point, and the rules this epilogue began with still govern them.

## This year

:::: {.point}
Keep one practice for a year and do not stop when the year ends; the keeping itself is asked of God, in the Munajat's words.

1. A year, at least, is the teachers' measure (Chapter Ten, in words; L10 ¶26); Qadi's year of the Musabbihat (Chapter Eight, a clause).
2. The practice: the month's, or Chapter Ten's al-Muzzammil each night or the night prayer from half an hour.
3. The opening answered: in the Sheikh's account, a year brings the traveller under the verse's title, those who plead for forgiveness at dawns (L10 ¶26–27; Q 3:17 recalled).
4. The year is no end: it "doesn't mean that after one year you stop it"; it may bring "more power to ask for more" (L10 ¶27); each rung kept, as no stage is dropped (Chapter Eight).
5. A small act counts because God accepts it: "accepts deed, and not its being small or big" (Maliki Tabrizi).
6. Therefore: the keeping, too, is asked of God. Close on Munajat §124, "make me one of those who always remember You". Stop.
::::

The last rung is a year, which the teachers of this road set as the least time for a student to keep one practice, as Chapter Ten recalled. Chapter Eight showed Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi giving his beginners the Musabbihat every night for a year before any second instruction.
<!-- src: L10 ¶26; L07 ¶16 -->

The practice for the year can be the one the month has kept. Chapter Ten offered two others from the lectures: Surah al-Muzzammil each night, which takes about three minutes, and the night prayer begun at half an hour.
<!-- src: L10 ¶23, ¶25 -->

In the Sheikh's account, a year of the night prayer or of asking forgiveness in the heart brings the traveller under the opening verse's title of those who plead for forgiveness at dawns.
<!-- src: L10 ¶26-27 -->

Keeping a practice for a year, the lectures add, "doesn't mean that after one year you stop it", since stopping would let the traveller slip back to a lower place. What the year may bring instead is "more power to ask for more". Each rung is kept as the next is added, as Chapter Eight found of the four stages.
<!-- src: L10 ¶27 -->

A single act of a few minutes, kept for a year, can still seem too slight to count for much. Maliki Tabrizi turns the question from the size of the deed to the One who receives it: "what is important and useful for the servant is that Allah (the Glorious, the Exalted), accepts deed, and not its being small or big" [@journey, p. 161]. He adds that Allah accepts even insignificant deeds and rewards them handsomely [@journey, p. 161].

Since what counts is God's acceptance, the traveller asks Him for the keeping too, in words the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah already gives. A few lines before the petition that opened Part Five, the prayer asks for the constancy on which every rung depends:

::: {.quote ar="وَأَنْ تَجْعَلَنِي مِمَّنْ يُدِيمُ ذِكْرَكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
make me one of those who always remember You [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §124]
:::
