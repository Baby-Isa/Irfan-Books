::: {.ayah ref="30:21" words="15-"}
There are indeed signs in that for a people who reflect.
:::

At many weddings, someone recites a verse most of us know. In Surah al-Rum, God says that He created spouses for us from among ourselves "that you may take comfort in them", and that He ordained "affection and mercy" between husband and wife (Q 30:21). The verse ends with the words above: this is a sign for a people who reflect. The sign is there for everyone, yet, as with beauty, seeing it lies in the eye of the beholder. It depends on something inside the person doing the looking. That something, and why it is worth seeking above and beyond the minimum, is what this chapter sets out to explore.

::: {.words}
*ʿIrfan*: knowing God from close · *maʿrifah*: knowledge of God · *ʿarif* (plural *ʿurafaʾ*): one who knows God · *muqarrabun*: those brought near to God · *awliyaʾ Allah*: the friends of God · *shirk*: setting up a partner beside God
:::

## What the minimum leaves out

:::: {.point}
The obligations save, but the Qur'an asks for more: to reflect on, know, listen to and reason about its signs.

1. A believer who keeps the obligations can hope for jannah; that is not contested. *(Story box: what was enough for jannah, and the Sheikh's question: then why do more?)*
2. This book disputes a second claim: that the obligations are the whole of the religion.
3. The Qur'an answers it: Surah al-Rum names three more sets of signs, and each ends by saying whom the sign is for.
4. Four times running, the signs are "for" those who reflect, know, listen and reason; that is what the Qur'an asks beyond the minimum.
5. This "more" never bypasses the law.
6. Therefore: the obligations save, but the Qur'an asks for more.
::::

Let's start with what nobody disputes. A believer who prays, fasts, pays what is due, and goes on hajj can hope for *jannah*, provided they do what is *wajib* and avoid what is forbidden. Sheikh Bahmanpour accepts this as fair for ordinary believers, and Mohammad Ali Shomali adds that the obligatory rules of fiqh are "the minimally necessary conditions of human perfection" [@self_knowledge, p. 17].

::: {.box-story title="Story: What was enough for jannah"}
Someone once came to the Prophet ﷺ and said that they had no eagerness for the night prayer (*salat al-layl*), the recommended fasts, and the like. They asked whether they would go to jannah if they prayed the five daily prayers, fasted in Ramadan, paid their zakat, and went on hajj once. The Prophet said yes. Sheikh Bahmanpour opens his first lecture with this story and draws his question from the Prophet's answer: if the obligatory acts are enough for jannah, why take on the hard work of ʿirfan?
:::

Some jurists and activists go further. They hold that the obligatory acts are the whole of the religion, and that anything beyond them has no basis in the evidence and is made up by the whims and wishes of certain mystics. This is the point that divides those who reject ʿirfan from those who seek it, and this book is written as a guide for the seekers.

The Qur'an answers this narrow view a few lines after the wedding verse, pointing in Surah al-Rum to three further sets of signs: the creation of the heavens and the earth, with the variety of our languages and colours; our sleep and our work; and the lightning and the rain that revives the dead earth (Q 30:22–24). Like the wedding verse, each of them ends by naming the people the sign is for.

::: {.ayah ref="30:22" words="9-"}
There are indeed signs in that for those who know.
:::

::: {.ayah ref="30:23" words="9-"}
There are indeed signs in that for a people who listen.
:::

::: {.ayah ref="30:24" words="16-"}
There are indeed signs in that for people who exercise their reason.
:::

Four times in a row, the Qur'an makes the same point. The signs are all around us: in marriage, in the people we meet, in sleep, in work, and in the weather. Yet they are for those who reflect, know, listen, and reason. This reflecting, knowing, listening, and reasoning is what the Qur'an asks of us beyond the minimum.

For the avoidance of doubt, this invitation from the Qur'an is not an invitation to circumvent the law or to abandon the prescribed wajibat. Imam Khomeini insists that a genuine seeker's devotion "is within the bounds of the Shari'ah" [@forty_hadith, p. 61]. Within those bounds, the Qur'an is just as insistent: it invites us to more than the obligatory acts.

## Signs most people miss

:::: {.point}
Most of us, the devout included, do not perceive those signs.

1. An invitation to those who reflect implies that many do not, the devout included.
2. Accepting that a sign exists because the Qur'an says so is not perceiving it, and the Qur'an names that gap (Q 12:105).
3. A sign points beyond itself, like a red light; most of us see only the lamp (Tabatabaʾi).
4. Therefore: most of us do not perceive the signs.
::::

An invitation addressed to those who reflect, know, listen, and reason implies that many people do none of these things. This is not limited to the careless. Most pious people, even those careful about the shariʿa, do not find a sign in these verses. Looking at the variety of languages and colours, we are more likely to see a reason for division than a sign of God.

We have heard these verses many times, and we accept that there are signs in them because the Qur'an says so; yet accepting that a sign exists is not the same as perceiving it. The Qur'an describes the gap in one verse.

::: {.ayah ref="12:105"}
How many a sign there is in the heavens and the earth that they pass by while they are disregardful of it!
:::

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi explains what passing by a sign means; a person who sees a red light thinks of the danger it warns of, not of the colour and shape of the lamp. In the same way, the things of the world are signs, and "what the signs indicate is not this world, but the person of its Creator" [@light, pp. 6–7]. Most of us see only the lamp. We witness the rain as rain and the marriage as a marriage, and we pass them by without perceiving what they say about their Creator.

## The fault is in the receiver

:::: {.point}
Since the signs are plain and we miss them, the fault is a faculty in us; it has rusted, so it can be repaired.

1. If the signs are plain and we still miss them, the fault is ours; the Qur'an names it: hearts, eyes and ears unused (Q 7:179).
2. Cattle see and hear, so the fault is not in the senses but in the faculty behind them that turns sight into understanding.
3. That faculty is the soul, a machine that has rusted; ʿirfan is its repair (the Sheikh).
4. This has two consequences. *(Reader told: "two consequences".)*
    a. The problem is not a lack of evidence: God is not withholding signs.
    b. The problem can be solved: a rusted faculty can be repaired, and the pull towards God is part of how we are made (Kernel). So the fault is in us, and it can be repaired.
5. Repair only starts a journey with "a beginning and no end". *(Breather, kept by the user.)*
::::

If the signs are present and plain, and yet we still pass them by, the fault is on our side. The Qur'an says where it lies, speaking of people who have every faculty and use none of them.

::: {.ayah ref="7:179" words="8-27"}
they have hearts with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, they have ears with which they do not hear. They are like cattle; indeed, they are more astray.
:::

Cattle see and hear perfectly well, so the comparison cannot be about the senses. It is about what lies behind the senses. Eyes and ears take in the surface of things, whereas inside a human being there should be something more: a faculty, which animals lack, that turns what the senses take in into understanding. In most of us, it is not working.

Sheikh Bahmanpour compares this faculty to a machine. The ear catches the sound, but "there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is [rusted]." That machine is the soul. It has to be repaired before it can catch the signals of God. This is the whole subject in one sentence: mysticism is about making the soul hear, see, and reflect. It is not easy, and it requires a great deal of training.

If the fault lies in the receiver, two things follow. One is that we have never lacked evidence; the signs are there, and we have failed to receive them.

The other, more hopeful, is that the problem can be solved. A faculty that exists but has rusted can be repaired; one that is absent cannot be made fit for purpose. The *Kernel of the Kernels*, a record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's early lectures on the path, opens by saying that the pull towards the unseen is "part of the innate instincts of the human being" [@kernel, p. 4]. The longing for God, and the faculty that perceives Him, are part of how we are made.

Repair is only the start. Once the inner faculty begins to work, a journey begins that, in the Sheikh's words, "has a beginning and no end".

## Some do see

:::: {.point}
If the faculty can be repaired, some have repaired it: the Qur'an describes believers in whom it works, the ʿurafaʾ.

1. If it can be repaired, there should be people in whom it works; the tradition calls them the ʿurafaʾ, ordinary believers, not prophets. The Qur'an describes them in four ways. *(Reader told: "four ways".)*
    a. Their hearts tremble at God's mention (Q 8:2), so they know Him.
    b. They "possess knowledge" and bear witness to His oneness (Q 3:18): they witness what others only say.
    c. They fear Him (Q 35:28), and not only His punishment.
    d. They are the friends of God (Q 10:62), who in Imam ʿAli's words see the world's reality when others see its surface.
2. Seeing inside the world does not mean leaving it: they live among people, with their hearts on God.
3. Therefore: in some believers the faculty works.
::::

If the faculty can be repaired, there should be people in whom it already works. The Qur'an describes them in four verses. People can be divided into two groups: those whose souls work, and those whose souls do not. The first group are the *ʿurafaʾ*, the plural of *ʿarif*. The prophets and the Imams (as) are a class of their own; the ʿurafaʾ are ordinary believers who see more than the rest. Surah al-Anfal gives the first of these descriptions.

::: {.ayah ref="8:2" words="1-8"}
The faithful are only those whose hearts tremble [with awe] when Allah is mentioned,
:::

A heart trembles only at the mention of someone it has some experience of; a name means little to a person who does not know whom it names. The believers of this verse are thus the believers who know Allah. A verse in Surah Al ʿImran goes further, giving them another name.

::: {.ayah ref="3:18" words="1-10"}
Allah … bears witness that there is no god except Him, and [so do] the angels and those who possess knowledge.
:::

For Sheikh Bahmanpour, "those who possess knowledge" is another name for the ʿurafaʾ. Every Muslim knows that there is no god but Allah. Our knowledge of *tawhid*, God's oneness, is still not the knowledge of those who witness it. Tawhid is simple to say and hard to grasp; the intellect alone cannot reach its depths, and only the awakened soul can. Surah Fatir describes the same people by what they feel.

::: {.ayah ref="35:28" words="8-13"}
Only those of Allah's servants having knowledge fear Him.
:::

Most of us fear Allah's punishment. The people of this verse fear Him with a fear that is not about punishment at all.

In Surah Yunus they are the *awliyaʾ Allah*, the friends of God (Q 10:62). Imam ʿAli (as) describes what these friends see that others miss.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of Nahj 3.403 from Qutbuddin p. 810]" source="Nahj al-Balagha"}
God's special devotees are those who perceive the world's reality when others are deceived by her appearance. They occupy themselves with what is coming when others occupy themselves with the present. [@nahj, 3.403]
:::

Other people look at the surface of the world; the friends of God look at what lies inside it. This does not mean turning away from the world. There is nothing wrong with pursuing material wealth in this life; the trouble is being solely occupied with it. The friends of God have families, friends, and a place in society, as we do. They think not about themselves but about God, and in their hearts they are cut off from everything else.

## What they are given, and why it is open to all

:::: {.point}
What they are given is worth having, and their rank is open to every believer.

1. The Qur'an names three things they are given. *(Reader told: "three".)*
    a. Nearness: they are "brought near" (Q 56:10–11), a veil removed.
    b. A good life (Q 16:97): clean, above the worries that occupy everyone else.
    c. Freedom from fear and grief (Q 10:62).
2. This is not only for prophets: Q 4:69 names four ranks, and the fourth, the righteous, is a doorway open to us.
3. We are made for it: "we are not yet human beings", but God created us able to become His friends.
4. So far: the Qur'an invites more, what stops us can be repaired, and beyond the repair lie nearness, a good life and peace, open to any believer.
5. Therefore: what they have is worth having, and within our reach.
::::

Of the three gifts the Qur'an names for these people, nearness comes first. Surah al-Waqiʿa sorts people on the Last Day, and at the top it places the Foremost Ones.

::: {.ayah ref="56:10-11"}
And the Foremost Ones are the foremost ones: they are the ones brought near [to Allah],
:::

The Arabic for "brought near" is *muqarrabun*. God is not far from anyone. Being brought near means that a veil between the person and God has been removed, so that they understand far more.

Surah al-Nahl promises the second, a different kind of life, to whoever acts righteously, man or woman, while having faith.

::: {.ayah ref="16:97" words="1-12"}
Whoever acts righteously, [whether] male or female, should he be faithful, We shall revive him with a good life
:::

This good life (*hayat tayyiba*), the life the mystics seek, is a clean life. Its people live in the world without being soiled by it, rising above the worries that occupy everyone else.

The third, peace, is promised in the verse of Surah Yunus that names the friends of God.

::: {.ayah ref="10:62"}
Look! The friends of Allah will indeed have no fear nor will they grieve
:::

For the muqarrabun, who have no fear and do not grieve, the terms are entirely different from those of ordinary believers.

None of this would matter to an ordinary reader if it were reserved for prophets. Surah al-Nisaʾ answers that concern.

::: {.ayah ref="4:69"}
Whoever obeys Allah and the Apostle—they are with those whom Allah has blessed, including the prophets and the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous, and excellent companions are they!
:::

In this verse the Sheikh finds four ranks of those God has blessed: the prophets; the truthful (*siddiqin*); the *shuhadaʾ*, which the translation used here renders "martyrs" and which he takes as "the witnesses"; and the righteous (*salihin*). The first three ranks are beyond us; the fourth is a doorway. The salihin are those able to receive this favour, and from among them God chooses the truthful and the prophets. The way in is righteous action, as the verse on the good life also says.

That doorway is open to people like us. It is difficult, but we are made for it. We call ourselves human beings, yet, in the Sheikh's words, "we are not yet human beings": we begin with the capacity to become human in the full sense that the prophets are, and God wants us to reach it.

This is the case for pursuing ʿirfan. The Qur'an invites more than the minimum, and what stands in the way is a faculty in us that can be repaired. On the other side of that repair lie nearness to God, a good life, and freedom from fear and grief. That rank is open to any believer who obeys God and the Prophet and acts righteously.

## The aim is God, not powers

:::: {.point}
The repair is training the soul, and that training must aim at God alone, never at powers.

1. Repair means training the soul, and soul-training can aim at powers: being in two places at once, seeing the future.
2. The Sheikh: ʿirfan is not about powers; seeking them is shirk.
3. The great Shia teachers agree, each in his own words. *(Reader told: "each in his own words"; a nuanced point, so three scholars, TONE 43.)*
    a. Tabatabaʾi: the traveller has no right to seek powers; whoever does worships his own self. *(Who's Who box.)*
    b. Khomeini, from his teacher Shahabadi: exercises meant for powers are invalid.
    c. Mutahhari: any goal but God is a kind of shirk, because the goal has become something other than God.
4. Therefore: the scholars differ in emphasis but agree that the aim is God alone, not powers. *(Short closing paragraph, kept by the user.)*
::::

Pursuing ʿirfan means training the soul, and soul-training is not unique to Islam. People take up the inner life for different reasons. Some train the soul in an attempt to gain power: to be in two places at once, to see the future or the past, or to master the hidden world of the soul. This kind of mysticism must be held apart from ʿirfan. Islamic mysticism is about catching the signs of Allah: becoming sensitive to them, and learning to hear and see.

Sheikh Bahmanpour ends his first lecture more strongly. ʿIrfan, he says, is not about gaining power over the soul to work the wonders of the saints (*karamat*) or the miracles of the prophets (*muʿjizat*). Such powers are *shirk*: the sin of setting up a partner beside God.

The great teachers of the Shia tradition make closely related points, each in his own words. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi tells the traveller that he "has no right to seek occult experiences and miraculous qualities", nor to perform austerities or recite invocations to gain powers such as crossing great distances in a moment or reading the thoughts of others. Whoever does so "does not move on the path of the Beloved's pleasure and his worship is not for the sake of God". It is his own self that he worships, "though he may not admit it by word of mouth" [@kernel, pp. 28–29].

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi"}
Sayyid Muhammad Husayn Tabatabaʾi is best known as the author of *al-Mizan*, a commentary on the Qur'an in twenty volumes [@self_knowledge, p. 30]. His student Sayyid Muhammad Husayn Husayni Tihrani describes him as having flown "with the two wings of knowledge and action". He travelled the path in both philosophy and gnosis under the guidance of the gnostic Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, and after years of philosophical argument he "came to settle down at the sacred threshold of the Qur'an" [@kernel, p. 8]. *Kernel of the Kernels* (*Lubb al-Lubab*) is Tihrani's record of the lectures on ethics and gnosis that Tabatabaʾi gave to students in Qum in 1949–50 [@kernel, p. 9]. He also wrote the preface to *Light Within Me* [@light, p. 4], from which several quotations in this chapter come.
:::

Imam Khomeini reports the test his teacher Ayatullah Shahabadi used: the measure of true spiritual effort is how much selfishness is in it. If a traveller's "spiritual exercises are meant for acquiring powers for worldly ends, his efforts are rendered invalid" [@forty_hadith, p. 61].

Murtada Mutahhari uses the same word as Sheikh Bahmanpour. The ʿurafaʾ, he writes, consider it "a kind of shirk (polytheism) for one's goal in life and particularly in worship to be something other than God Himself", and he recalls Imam ʿAli (as) saying that he worshipped God not from fear of His Fire or desire for His Paradise but because he found Him worthy of worship [@light, p. 44].

The scholars differ in emphasis but agree that powers are not the aim. Mutahhari also explains why seeking them would be shirk: the goal has become something other than God.

## Seeking, and nothing else

:::: {.point}
ʿIrfan is seeking the knowledge and love of God and nothing else, and it is worth pursuing.

1. With powers set aside, the Sheikh's definition remains: ʿirfan "is actually seeking and nothing else": the knowledge of God and the love of God. *(Word Study box.)*
2. Therefore ʿirfan is worth pursuing: the training of the soul to perceive and love God, open to every believer, and sought for God alone.

Close: "If the aim is to know and love God, what kind of knowing is that?"
::::

With powers set aside, what remains is the definition Sheikh Bahmanpour gives at the end of his first lecture: ʿirfan "is actually seeking and nothing else". It seeks the knowledge of God (*maʿrifah*) and the love of God (*mahabba*).

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: ʿIrfan and maʿrifah"}
*ʿIrfan*, *maʿrifah*, *ʿarif* and its plural *ʿurafaʾ* belong to one family of words about knowing. An ʿarif, as Sheikh Bahmanpour puts it, is "someone who knows". "Mystic" is used of Christians, Hindus, and Buddhists alike; ʿarif is the Islamic word, and it makes a precise claim. Murtada Mutahhari draws the line. The philosopher wants a correct picture of the world in his mind. The ʿarif "wishes to reach the very kernel and reality of existence, God, to become connected to it and witness it" [@light, p. 12]. *Lantern of the Path*, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), says that there is no way to God except through knowledge [@lantern, p. 9]. What kind of knowledge that is, and how it differs from what we learn from books, is the subject of Chapter Two.
:::

ʿIrfan is therefore worth pursuing: it is the training of the soul to perceive and love God, open to every believer, and sought for God alone.

If the aim is to know and love God, what kind of knowing is that?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour builds his first lesson on the four signs of Surah al-Rum. For one week, read Q 30:21–24 slowly once a day. After each verse, ask yourself what he found in its ending: whether you reflect, whether you know, whether you listen, and whether you use your reason. Then, at some point in the day, notice one of the four signs where it occurs in your life: the affection in your home, the languages and colours of the people you meet, your own sleep and the work that earns your living, or the rain.
:::
