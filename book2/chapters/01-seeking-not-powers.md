## A mysterious subject

Few words in religious life are as easily misunderstood as "mysticism". The lecture series behind this book opens by admitting as much. Mysticism, it says, is very mysterious: no one really knows what it is, and even the mystics themselves are often unsure what they are doing, "except the real mystics". So this first chapter does not yet teach the method of ʿIrfan. It asks an earlier question. What is Islamic mysticism after? And why should a believer who already prays, fasts and gives what is due care about it at all?

The Islamic name for the subject is *ʿIrfan*. In this book it means the discipline of knowing God through spiritual experience and the purification of the soul. The word shares its root with *maʿrifah*, a word most readers will know from majalis and du'as: knowing God, and recognising Him. The Word Study below looks at the family of words more closely. For now it is enough to notice that the name itself points to knowledge, not to power, and that this is the theme of the whole chapter.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: ʿirfan and maʿrifah"}
The Arabic root *ʿ-r-f* carries the sense of knowing and recognising something, often a thing one comes to know by acquaintance rather than by description [VERIFY: Arabic lexicon, e.g. Lane's Lexicon, entry ʿ-r-f]. From it come *maʿrifah* (knowledge, recognition), *ʿarif* (one who knows) and its plural *ʿurafaʾ*, and *ʿIrfan*, the name of the discipline. The same root appears in the Quran when it says that God made human beings into peoples and tribes "so that you may come to know one another" [VERIFY: Q 49:13, Qaraʾi translation]. The lectures define ʿIrfan simply as "possessing maʿrifah", the knowledge of God, while knowledge of worldly matters is called *ʿilm*. They add that religious texts often use the two words interchangeably; the finer distinction between them is taken up in Chapter 2. An *ʿarif*, then, is not someone who knows many facts about God. It is someone who knows God.
:::

Part of the difficulty is that mysticism is not limited to Islam. There are Christian, Hindu and Buddhist mystics, and even some people with no religion at all feel drawn to it. People also come to mysticism for very different reasons. Some want extraordinary powers. They want, for example, to be in two places at the same time, and so they set out to explore the hidden world of the human soul in order to master the powers it contains. The lectures accept that this kind of mysticism exists in other traditions. Their point is that it must be clearly told apart from the real thing, and that this is "very important".

So the first questions are basic ones. What are we talking about? Who are the mystics? What do they want from a journey that the lectures describe as "exhausting, time consuming, life consuming"? The answer comes from two places: the Quran, and the words of the Imams (ʿa), especially their supplications. There, the lectures argue, we meet a kind of mysticism "very different" from what is found in other religions. This chapter follows that answer. Its conclusion can be given at the start, in the lecture's own closing words: Islamic mysticism "is actually seeking and nothing else".

## Why go beyond the minimum?

The lectures begin with an honest challenge. Someone once came to the Prophet ﷺ and said, in effect: I don't have the eagerness to pray *salat al-layl* (the recommended night prayer) or to keep the recommended fasts. If I pray my five daily prayers, fast in Ramadan, pay my zakat and go to hajj once, will I go to *janna* (Paradise)? The Prophet said yes. If you do these things, you will go to janna [VERIFY: locate this hadith and its collection].

The lecture presses the point. If it is so easy to reach janna just by performing our *wajib* (obligatory) duties, why should anyone work so hard? Why take on the long, difficult striving that mysticism asks for? Why bother with what is *mustahabb* (recommended), with night vigils and inward training, if the door to Paradise is already open to those who do the minimum?

This is not just a thought experiment. The lectures note that some Muslim jurists and activists are "completely against mysticism". Their argument is simple: follow what the *shariʿa*, the outward religious law, has told you and you will reach janna; "there is no other way". On this view mysticism is something silly, invented by certain mystics "according to their vibes and wishes".

For most believers, religious life is organised around the shariʿa: knowing what is wajib and what is forbidden, and doing one's best to keep within those limits. That is no small thing, and the lectures never belittle it. But they ask whether it is the whole story. Their method of answering is worth noticing. They do not appeal to the authority of famous mystics or to anyone's experiences. They ask: "What does the Quran say about mysticism? Let's see."

## Signs, but not for everyone

The lecture turns to Surah al-Rum (chapter 30 of the Quran). It chooses a few verses and notes that "many other verses in the same line" could have been chosen. Each verse in this passage begins with the refrain "And among His signs…" and ends by naming a particular kind of person for whom the sign is meant. That ending is the key.

> And among His signs is that He created mates for you from yourselves, that you may dwell in tranquillity with them, and He has put love and mercy between you. Surely in that are signs for those who reflect. (Q 30:21) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation; text given here as rendered in the lecture]

What signs? the lecture asks. When we look at the relationship between a man and a woman, do we see a sign of God in it? "Usually," it says with some irony, "we will see the sign of shaitan." The signs are certainly there, but they are not for everyone. They are for "the people who reflect".

> And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your tongues and your colours. Surely in that are signs for those who know. (Q 30:22) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

Here too the lecture asks what we actually see. When we look at the variety of peoples and languages, we tend to see ethnic division, argument and even racism, not a sign of God. Yet the verse says there are signs in this "for the knowledgeable", "for the learned. Not for everyone." The lecture then adds a remark that cuts close to home: not most people, "not even most pious people", and not even most of those who are very strict about the shariʿa find signs in this.

> And among His signs is your sleep by night and by day, and your seeking of His bounty. Surely in that are signs for those who hear. (Q 30:23) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

"Aren't we hearing? Do we hear?" If we truly heard, the lecture asks, why are we not broken down when we meet the signs of Allah, in the way the prophets and the *awliyaʾ Allah*, the friends of God, break down?

> And among His signs is that He shows you the lightning, arousing fear and hope, and sends down water from the sky, and with it revives the earth after its death. Surely in that are signs for those who use reason. (Q 30:24) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

Put the four endings side by side: those who reflect, those who know, those who hear, those who use reason. The lecture draws a startling conclusion: "Actually Quran is insulting us." Read honestly, these verses tell us that we do not understand, do not hear, do not reflect and are not learned. "I don't know about you," the lecture says. "I feel that Quran is insulting."

Elsewhere the Quran says something similar even more sharply. The next lecture of the series returns to the point with a verse that most Quran readers will have met many times:

> They have hearts with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. They are like cattle; rather, they are more astray. It is they who are the heedless. (Q 7:179) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

"Like animals, and even worse." It is hard to imagine stronger language. But the verse also contains a puzzle, and the lectures use that puzzle to introduce the central image of the whole series.

## The inner machine

The puzzle is this. Animals have eyes and ears. Do they not see and hear? Of course they do. An animal sees where it walks, and when it hears a sound it runs away. So what is the Quran comparing? If the people described in the verse are like animals, it cannot be because their eyes and ears have stopped working.

The lecture's answer is that the ear is only an instrument. Its job is to catch the sound, and it does that job perfectly well. "However there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is rust and doesn't see these signs." What we see and hear with our senses stays on the surface; it is "superficial". To reach the reality of what we see and hear, something inside the human being has to translate it. Animals see and hear, but they do not have that inner machine. Human beings do have it, but in most of us it is not working. It is, in the lecture's words, "dead and blind".

The lectures give an example that sharpens the point. Those who reject faith, the *kuffar*, also see and hear. If we tell them that they have eyes but do not see, they will laugh. They will say they see better than we do, in more detail and more artistically. And in one sense they are right. The Quran agrees that they see; what it denies is that they understand. The lectures promise that this "enigma of having and not having, listening and not listening, being and not being" will come up again and again in the course.

Most of us have heard the Surah al-Rum verses many times. Some of us have even felt, while hearing them, that there is something in them we are not seeing. Usually we accept them out of obedience: the Quran says there are signs here, so there are signs here. That is fine as far as it goes. But the real question is what we must do to *catch* the sign: to hear it, see it and reflect on it. Here the lectures give their first definition of the subject:

> Mysticism is all about making one's soul hear, see and reflect. I think this is the summary of mysticism… the summary of Islamic mysticism.

The rest of the series, the lecture says, is about how this can be done, "as it's very difficult". The verse it gives at this point shows how much is at stake:

> And how many a sign in the heavens and the earth do they pass by, while they turn away from them! (Q 12:105) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

"We don't see. Something is wrong." The lecture now names the machine directly: it is the soul. The soul is broken; it does not work, and it has to be fixed so that it can catch the signals of God. "And this is not of course a joke and not an easy path. It needs lots of training."

For a reader new to ʿIrfan, this image may be the most useful thing to carry through the rest of the book. Each later chapter, whether on the soul, the stages of the path, repentance, night prayer, silence or love, can be read as part of one long answer to a single question: how is the inner machine repaired?

The lectures add one more point about this repair. It is often said that mysticism has an end, a final goal at which the journey stops. The lectures disagree. Mysticism "has a beginning and no end". The beginning is the moment the machine starts working, "and then of course you see the wonders of this world". What follows has no limit.

## Not a search for powers

Self-training, the lectures observe, is practised for many different purposes. Many mystics train themselves to gain powers, or to see the future or the past. "But Islamic mysticism is not about this." Islamic mysticism is about catching the signs of Allah: becoming sensitive to them, and learning to hear and see them. The lecture calls the next verse "very moving":

> Say: "Tell me, if Allah were to make the night perpetual over you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than Allah could bring you light? Will you not then listen?" (Q 28:71) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

"Are you not hearing?" The question at the end of the verse is addressed to the inner hearing, not the outer. What the soul catches, once it is working, are the signals that God is sending to us all the time.

What happens after that cannot be predicted. When the machine is repaired and the soul is lifted up, "god knows what type of signals you can see and hear". It is not possible for us to reckon what the mystics see and hear. At the very peak of such people, the lecture says, stand the Imams. [QUERY: the transcript at L01 ¶14 reads "the things that the kaafir see and the imam sees of course they are the peak". Was a different word than "kaafir" intended, such as *kamil* or *awliyaʾ*?]

This distinction matters a great deal for anyone setting out to learn about ʿIrfan. Curiosity about wonders, hidden powers and extraordinary experiences is a natural human thing, and it is often the first reason people take an interest in mysticism. The lectures do not deny that remarkable things may come to a purified soul. What they insist on is that such things are not the goal. A seeker who is after powers is still looking at something other than God.

## Those brought near

The lectures now turn to the Quran's own vocabulary for the people they have been describing. There are, they say, two groups of people. In one group the soul works; in the other it does not, or hardly works at all. The first are the *ʿurafaʾ*, "those who know", the plural of *ʿarif*. The others are those who do not know, who do not yet have the capacity to know and understand.

The lecture is careful about who it means. It is not talking about the prophets and the Imams, who are "very special ʿurafaʾ". Nor is it talking about those who reject faith. Ordinary believers, the *muʾminun*, certainly have understanding. But there are some people, not prophets and not Imams, who have a special understanding beyond that of ordinary believers, and the Quran mentions them.

The first name the lectures give them is *muqarrabun*, "those who are brought near". This raises an obvious question. Allah is not far away; He is very near. Indeed, the Quran says He is nearer to a person than their jugular vein [VERIFY: Q 50:16, Qaraʾi translation]. So in what sense can anyone be "brought near"? The lecture's answer is that some veil that stood between ordinary people and Allah has been taken away. The muqarrabun understand far better; "this is the meaning of muqarrab". Nothing has moved except the veil.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: muqarrabun"}
*Muqarrabun* is a passive form from the root *q-r-b*, "to be near". Literally it means "those who have been brought near", which implies that the nearness is given by God, not seized [VERIFY: Arabic lexicon, root q-r-b]. The same root gives *qurb* (nearness), a word that will return often in this book. The lectures point to Surah al-Waqiʿa as the place where the Quran separates the muqarrabun from ordinary believers. That surah describes people divided into three groups: the people of the right hand, the people of the left hand, and "the foremost", who are called the ones brought near (Q 56:7–11) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation and verse numbers]. Near the end of the surah the three groups appear again, with the muqarrabun named first (Q 56:88–94) [VERIFY: verse range]. The lectures add that the muqarrabun are among the awliyaʾ Allah, the friends of God.
:::

Earlier teaching by {{LECTURER}}, outside this series, had said that the muqarrabun fall into four categories. The second lecture identifies these with a verse from Surah al-Nisaʾ:

> Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger: they are with those whom Allah has blessed, among the prophets, the truthful, the witnesses and the righteous. (Q 4:69) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation; the lecture quotes only the opening of the verse and then names the four groups]

"What is favouring?" the lecture asks. "Certainly it is not wealth or a strong body. It is something else." The four groups are the prophets (*nabiyyin*), the truthful (*siddiqin*), the witnesses (*shuhadaʾ*) and the righteous (*salihin*). The first three are very special, and "we cannot reach that level". The muqarrabun include the prophets, the Imams and the siddiqin.

The fourth group, the salihin, is different. The lectures describe them as "very good" and at the bottom of the category of the muqarrabun. The salihin are those who deserve this favour, or who have the capacity to receive it. It is from among the salihin, the lecture says, that Allah chooses the siddiqin, and from the siddiqin that He chooses the prophets. About some of the prophets, the lecture says, Allah explains that He made them prophets because they were among the righteous. The example given is the prophet Lut:

> And We admitted him into Our mercy; indeed he was one of the righteous. (Q 21:75) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

The mercy here, the lecture explains, is a "special mercy", and Lut deserved it because he belonged to this category. The salihin are people "who understand better than us, who see and hear better than us".

This is where ordinary people come in. The higher ranks are closed to us, but "ordinary people also can enter into the realm of muqarrabun". Entering it makes them "completely different from other people", and the Quran distinguishes them from other believers. It is "very difficult to get into that last category". "And that is what Islamic Mysticism is about."

The lectures then draw the contrast with ordinary faith more sharply. What we usually understand is that religion is only the shariʿa: follow it, succeed, and go to janna. "Ok that's fair enough. That is something for ordinary mominoon." For the muqarrabun, however, "the whole terms and conditions are different". They are after something else. They are given janna in this world itself, and in the next world they seek something beyond it. They become members of the awliyaʾ Allah, and they become "the proof of Allah". "These are actually the true ʿurafaʾ or mystics."

Here the lectures pause on a matter of words. "Mystic" is a very general term, used for Christian, Hindu and Buddhist mystics alike. *ʿArif*, by contrast, is "a very nice term which we use in Islam when someone becomes true ʿarif". This book follows that preference: it speaks of ʿIrfan and the ʿarif wherever it can, and uses "mysticism" only as an English gloss.

## A special class of believers in the Quran

If the idea of a special class of believers still sounds like something the mystics invented, the second lecture of the series answers with a series of verses. Each describes believers whose inner life is unlike that of ordinary believers, and each adds something to the picture.

The first is from Surah al-Anfal:

> The believers are only those whose hearts tremble when Allah is mentioned. (Q 8:2) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation; the lecture quotes the opening of the verse]

"Is there anyone here whose heart trembles when he or she hears the name of Allah?" the lecturer asks the class, and then adds with a smile: "Well, even if we have we are not going to reveal this secret." The serious point follows. What kind of person trembles at a name? You only tremble at something if you already have some idea or experience of it. If someone mentions a thing that frightens you, you go numb because you already know what it is. So these believers tremble at the name of Allah because they *know* Allah. They are "not ordinary mominoon".

The second verse opens Surah al-Muʾminun:

> Certainly the believers have attained salvation: those who are humble in their prayers. (Q 23:1–2) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

"It is not me certainly," the lecture says, "and many of you might not be like this." The word translated "humble" is *khashiʿ*, and the lecture explains that it means more than humility. A khashiʿ is someone who is broken: someone who has experienced something that brings them down inside themselves. When such believers stand before Allah in salah, they break down, and they do so "because of their experience that they have of Allah".

That brings the lecture to what it calls the heart of the matter: "Can Allah be experienced at all? This is what mysticism is all about. Can we have a way or a canal or a path to Allah?"

The next verse goes higher. The lecture calls it one of the great verses of the Quran, one that "only people who have high knowledge can understand":

> Allah bears witness that there is no god except Him, and so do the angels and those who possess knowledge, maintaining justice. There is no god except Him, the All-mighty, the All-wise. (Q 3:18) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

Allah has knowledge of His own oneness, His *tawhid*, and He bears witness to it. We also have some knowledge of tawhid, but it cannot be compared with His. The angels bear witness too, and so do certain human beings: *ulu al-ʿilm*, "those who possess knowledge". This, the lecture says, is "another expression of ʿarif". The ʿurafaʾ, those who have maʿrifah, bear witness to the tawhid of Allah.

This is why the lectures insist that tawhid is "not as simple as just saying that there is no god but Allah". That is its simplest meaning, the one we all understand. To understand it more deeply, we cannot rely on the intellect alone. "We have to start and ignite that machine inside us, the soul. It's only that part of us that can grab tawhid." The lecture recalls a teaching that each verse of the Quran has seven layers of depth, beneath each of those seventy, and beneath each of those seven hundred [VERIFY: source of this tradition; the lecture introduces it with "we have heard"]. Whatever the exact numbers, the point is clear: the same verse, the same concept, is understood at different depths by different people.

Then comes Surah Fatir:

> Only those of His servants who have knowledge fear Allah. (Q 35:28) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation; the lecture quotes part of the verse]

All of us fear Allah in some way, the lecture observes, but most of us fear His punishment more than we fear Him. There are some people who fear Him and not His punishment, with a kind of fear "which we cannot explain. It's not fear of punishment. It is something else."

The lectures name two more Quranic expressions for such people. One is *ulu al-albab*, "those who have inner intellect". The other is the *awliyaʾ Allah*, the friends of God:

> Look! The friends of Allah will indeed have no fear, nor will they grieve. (Q 10:62) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

Again, this is a group of people who are different from us. How does someone become a friend of Allah and connect to Him so closely? The answer, the lecture says, depends partly on how the word *wali* is translated.

The last verse in this series is from Surah al-Nahl:

> Whoever acts righteously, whether male or female, should he be faithful, We shall revive him with a good life. (Q 16:97) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

The lecture notices that "acts righteously", *ʿamila salihan*, comes from the same root as salihin. Whoever does righteous deeds as a believer becomes one of the salihin, the lowest of the four ranks that were open to ordinary people. To such people Allah promises a *hayat tayyiba*, a "good life". The lecture explains *tayyiba* as clean and pure: pure "from the filth of this world". Such people "live in this world but they do not live in the filth of this world". They rise above the worldly concerns that infect everyone else. "And this hayat tayyiba is of course what mystics are after."

This, the lecture concludes, is enough of an introduction to show who the mystics are, and why they put themselves through "so much hardship, lots of training and purification to find a glimpse and experience of Allah". They do it because love of Him "has been put in their heart and that's why they are glad to seek".

The Quran, then, does not leave the question of mysticism to the mystics' "vibes and wishes". It describes believers whose hearts tremble, who break down in prayer, who bear witness to tawhid with knowledge, who fear God rather than His punishment, and who live a pure life in the middle of the world. The jurists who say that the shariʿa is enough are right that the shariʿa leads to janna. The lectures' reply is that the Quran itself points to something more.

## Words too big for us: the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah

After the Quran, the lectures turn to a second witness: the supplications of the Imams (ʿa). If we want to know what the ʿurafaʾ want, they say, we can look at what the Imams asked God for. The first text they choose is one that many readers will know well: the *Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah*, the intimate prayer of the month of Shaʿban. A *munajat* is an intimate, whispered prayer addressed directly to God.

::: {.box-history title="The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah is traditionally reported from Imam ʿAli (ʿa), and the report adds that all the Imams used to recite it in the month of Shaʿban [VERIFY: Iqbal al-Aʿmal of Sayyid Ibn Tawus, citing Ibn Khalawayh]. It is printed among the practices of Shaʿban in *Mafatih al-Jinan*, the widely used prayer manual compiled by Shaykh ʿAbbas Qummi [VERIFY: Mafatih al-Jinan, practices of Shaʿban]. In Shia communities, including Khoja jamaʿats, it is commonly recited during Shaʿban, the month that leads up to Ramadan, and many people know its opening lines by heart [VERIFY: confirm the details of Khoja practice with the user, e.g. when and where it is recited in jamaʿat]. Imam Khomeini is often quoted as giving it special praise among the supplications of the Imams [VERIFY: source of this statement]. The lecture's own advice is simple: recite it in Shaʿban "as much as you can".
:::

The lecture admits to a certain puzzlement. "Sometimes I question why we are told to recite such words." Giving these words to ordinary believers, it says, is "like if you tell a primary school boy to read books of university". These words come from the peak of the understanding and longing of the muqarrabun, and yet we are asked to say them. The lecture then reads through some of the lines, giving its own translations. The renderings below follow the lecture [VERIFY: check each line against Mafatih al-Jinan, Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, and a published translation].

The first request is: *My God, grant me complete severance from everything else, towards You.* The Arabic word for this severance is *inqitaʿ*: cutting oneself off. Is it good, the lecture asks, to cut oneself off completely from everything else? "Well, in the heart yes." The Quran says the same in Surah al-Muzzammil, which the lecture renders as an instruction to cut oneself off from everything and devote oneself to Him wholeheartedly (Q 73:8) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]. The Quranic word for this is *tabattul*. This severance, the lecture notes, goes beyond the sensitivity to signs described in Surah al-Rum.

The next line asks: *and enlighten the eyes of our hearts with the light of their looking at You.* Here the machine image returns. If our physical eyes work, why do we not see the signs? Because there is another mechanism, the heart, which has to take in what the eyes receive, and it does not, "because it's blind". The du'a asks for the eyes of the heart to be given light.

This raises an obvious objection, and the lecture states it plainly. "What do the mystics mean, looking at God? Is it something possible at all? Quran says that you can never see God." The verse usually cited is the one that says that vision does not perceive Him [VERIFY: Q 6:103, Qaraʾi translation]. People who are not familiar with the language of the mystics, the lecture says, sometimes conclude that such wording is a mistake. But the du'a itself gives the answer: the eyes in question are the eyes of the *heart*. Shia teaching has always rejected any physical sight of God [VERIFY: a held source on Shia kalam regarding ruʾya, e.g. a Tabatabaʾi or Mutahhari text]. A well-known report from Imam ʿAli (ʿa) makes the same distinction. Asked whether he had seen his Lord, he is reported to have said that eyes do not see Him but hearts perceive Him through the realities of faith [VERIFY: Nahj al-Balagha, the sermon in reply to Dhiʿlib al-Yamani; paraphrase only, wording to be supplied from a held source]. The lectures promise to explain later what "looking at the face of Allah" means.

The du'a then goes further still: *until the eyes of the hearts pierce the veils of light.* The mystics speak of veils, *hujub*, both of light and of darkness. A seeker must pass through them until they reach the very source of Allah's grandeur, His *ʿazama* and *kibriyaʾ*. And so the du'a continues: *and reach the source of grandeur.* "This is of course something which we cannot understand," the lecture admits. When we speak of Allah, we speak of something beyond our understanding. Yet this is exactly what the ʿurafaʾ want: to know Him, to look at Him, and to be drawn towards Him. And to do that, they must first be cut off from everything else. The sequence of the du'a is therefore an order of steps. First comes severance; then the eyes of the heart are given light; then they pierce the veils of light and look towards the source of grandeur.

Then: *and our souls become suspended by the glory of Your sanctity.* What does it mean for a soul to hang suspended from the glory of God's holiness? The lecture does not claim that we can grasp it. But it adds a principle worth remembering for every difficult text in this book: "If we don't know what they are talking about it doesn't mean that it is senseless and meaningless."

Another line asks: *My God, make me one of those whom You call and they respond, and at whom You glance and they are thunderstruck by Your majesty.* This, the lecture says, is "something like prophets do. You call and they respond." And when God looks into their souls, they are thunderstruck by the majesty, the *jalal*, that they see. "This is munajat-e-Shaʿbaniyyah. We cannot say that this is not good."

The lecture then gives its advice. All the Imams recited this prayer, and we are told to recite it in the month of Shaʿban, and the lecture recommends reciting it "as much as you can". Of course we do not understand many of its meanings. "But we have been honoured." These words are "not of our level", and yet we have been allowed to say them. It is as if Allah says: come and recite this, and perhaps, if you are sincere, I will grant it to you. "What the Imams wanted, Allah granted them."

Most of the other lines of the munajat, the lecture says, are like these. One more is singled out: *My God, place me among the people of Your wilaya.* The word *wilaya* is translated in many ways: friendship, closeness, guardianship. Here the lecture draws out two meanings. One is maʿrifah: understanding God, knowing Him, looking at Him. The other is love for Him. Maʿrifah and love are "the true meaning" of wilaya, the two wings "by which they can fly", and they are exactly what the du'a asks for: a love for Allah that never stops, and a maʿrifah that keeps increasing. This, the lecture adds, can only happen after the machine has been repaired and has begun to show the signals of Allah.

The line continues: *in the station of one who hopes for an increase in Your love.* The person described is one who has found a passionate love of remembering God, so that he remembers nothing but Him. The lecture quotes the line that follows, which asks for exactly this ardent longing for His remembrance, but leaves its full explanation for another time.

## Can a human being reach this?

Having read words like these, it is natural to ask whether any of it is realistic. The lecture links the munajat back to Surah al-Waqiʿa and its separation of believers from the muqarrabun, and then asks directly: is it possible for a human being to reach that level of understanding, knowledge and love of Allah?

The answer begins with a hard saying. "We wrongly call ourselves human beings. We are not yet human beings. We are animals who have potential to become human beings." We were created as animals with the capacity to become human, like the prophets, who are "real human beings". This echoes the verse from Surah al-Aʿraf about those who are "like cattle", and it gives that verse a hopeful turn. The animal state is where we begin, not where we must stay.

Then comes the hopeful part. "A human being has the capacity to become a friend of God. Allah has created us at this strength and he wants us to reach that strength." The tragedy is what we have done with that capacity: instead of becoming His friends, we have become His enemies.

What makes the awliyaʾ Allah different? How do they manage to repair the machine in the middle of ordinary life? The lectures note that they use *awliyaʾ Allah*, "the friends of God", as a synonym for the ʿurafaʾ, the mystics. They then turn to a description of the awliyaʾ by Imam ʿAli (ʿa) in *Nahj al-Balagha*. In the lecture's paraphrase, the Imam says that the friends of God are those who look at the inward side of the world while other people look at its outward side, at "what is on the surface". The awliyaʾ look deeper, and that is why they please Allah [VERIFY: Nahj al-Balagha; the lecture calls it "sermon 432", but this is probably Saying 432 in the Subhi al-Salih numbering].

Why can they do this when we cannot? The transcript is unclear here. It says that the awliyaʾ "busy themselves with 29:49" while other people busy themselves with outward things. Q 29:49 speaks of the Quran as clear signs in the hearts of those who have been given knowledge [VERIFY: Q 29:49, Qaraʾi translation]. [QUERY: L01 ¶26 gives only the verse number "29:49" and contrasts it with "the imaging". What contrast was intended?]

The lecture's example is clearer. It is not wrong according to the shariʿa to be rich. There is nothing wrong with wealth in itself. The problem is getting busy with it. If you are preoccupied with such things, "you can never reach that stage of awliyaʾ Allah". The awliyaʾ "never think about themselves. They always think about God." This does not mean they abandon the world. Like everyone else they have families, friends and a place in society. But in their hearts they are cut off from all of it, aloof from what preoccupies other people. The lecture recommends reading the rest of Imam ʿAli's description directly, calling it "a very nice prescription" for becoming one of the awliyaʾ Allah.

## Not a hermit: the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers

The second supplication the lectures read is from the *Sahifa Sajjadiyya*, the collection of supplications associated with Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (ʿa). It belongs to the group known as the Fifteen Whispered Prayers, the *Munajat al-Khamsa ʿAshar*. The lines quoted are from the one usually called the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers, *Munajat al-Muhibbin* [VERIFY: Sahifa Sajjadiyya, Munajat al-Muhibbin; the lecture names only "Munajat Khamsa ʿAshar"].

ʿAli ibn al-Husayn (ʿa), known as Zayn al-ʿAbidin ("the adornment of the worshippers") and al-Sajjad ("the one who prostrates much"), is the fourth Imam. He was the son of Imam al-Husayn (ʿa) and was present at Karbala, where illness kept him from the battle. After the tragedy he was taken captive with the women and children to Kufa and Damascus [VERIFY: a standard Shia biographical source, e.g. al-Mufid, Kitab al-Irshad]. He spent the rest of his life in Medina, and he is buried in the cemetery of al-Baqiʿ [VERIFY: date of death, usually given as 94 or 95 AH]. Much of his teaching survives in supplications. The *Sahifa Sajjadiyya* gathers them, and it has been called "the Psalms of the Household of Muhammad" [VERIFY: Sahifa, translator's introduction]. The Fifteen Whispered Prayers are usually printed with it as an appendix and transmitted separately [VERIFY: transmission of the Fifteen Munajat, e.g. via Bihar al-Anwar].

The lecture begins with a thought experiment. If you read this prayer without knowing who wrote it, you might think it came from someone who had left society, lived in a cave and spent his whole life devoted to nothing but Allah. Yet it is from an Imam who lived among people, with family and community. So the prayer shows what the lectures mean when they say the awliyaʾ are cut off from the world "in the heart". Here are the lines as the lecture renders them [VERIFY: each line against the Sahifa, Munajat al-Muhibbin, and a published translation].

*My God, who is there who has tasted the sweetness of Your love and then wanted something else in its place?* If you are still after other things, the lecture says, then you have not yet tasted the sweetness of His love.

*And who is there who has grown familiar with Your nearness and then sought to turn away from You?* Someone who has known the intimacy of being near to God has no wish to go anywhere else.

The prayer then asks to be placed among a particular group of people, and it describes them line by line: *those whom You have chosen for Your nearness and Your wilaya; whom You have purified for Your affection and love; whom You have made eager to meet You and content with Your decree; to whom You have given the gift of looking at Your face.* Here again is the language of "looking", and again the lecture defers the explanation: "Now it seems very meaningless, but when we go through the course of the lesson we will know what is looking at the face of Allah."

The prayer goes on: *whom You have given refuge from the pain of separation from You.* The lecture pauses here on the difference between two kinds of people at prayer. For some of us, when we call on Allah, He seems completely absent. "We want to cry into the sky because we are very far away from him." For others, when they call Him, they feel His presence and His response so strongly that they break down and faint, as the Imams did.

The prayer continues: *whom You have chosen for Your maʿrifah, made worthy of Your worship, and whose heart You have made lovesick with longing for You.* The verb here comes from *hayam*, a word for a love so intense it never runs dry. The lecture explains it as "love which never ever is quenched". It connects the word with a tradition about angels called the *muhayyamin*, angels lost in the love of Allah, who look at Him in such a way that they cannot turn their faces away [VERIFY: source of the tradition of the muhayyamin angels]. Elsewhere, the lecture adds, there is mention of angels standing in rows, so absorbed in watching God that when other angels ask them anything, they cannot hear and cannot see. They are completely absorbed in Him.

Some mystics have felt something of this state. Some, after finding it, were never able to speak again for the rest of their lives. The lecture does not hold this up as an ideal. "I don't say it is good, because if they have enough capacity they can come back, like the Prophet goes to *miʿraj* and comes back." The Prophet ﷺ rose in his ascension to the highest nearness and then returned to his people to guide them. That return requires enormous capacity, and it is the Prophet's example, not the silent mystic's, that the lectures hold up. The lecture quotes one more phrase from the prayer before moving on: *whose heart You have set burning with the love of seeking You.* [GAP: at L01 ¶31 the lecturer says, "InshaAllah I will find the translation and put it here"; the fuller translation he intended to provide is not in the transcript.]

## Seeking, and nothing else

"This is how mystics work," the lecture concludes. "It is actually seeking and nothing else." It is not about any kind of power, and it is not about strengthening the soul so that it can perform wonders. The lectures name these wonders: *karamat*, the marvels attributed to saints, and *muʿjizat*, the miracles of the prophets. To seek such powers for oneself, the lecture says bluntly, is *shirk*, associating something else with God. [QUERY: L01 ¶32 reads "to do karamat and mojezat. These are shirk." Confirm the intended sense. The Quran affirms the miracles of the prophets, so the lecturer presumably means that *seeking* such powers as a goal is shirk, not that the miracles themselves are.] What Islamic mysticism wants is maʿrifah and the love of Allah. How the mystics pursue it, and what they find, is the subject of the rest of the course.

It is worth looking back at the path this chapter has taken, because the later chapters build on it.

- It began with a fair challenge: the obligatory duties are enough for janna, so why do more?
- It answered from the Quran. The signs of God are everywhere, but they are recognised only by "those who reflect", "those who know", "those who hear" and "those who use reason". Most of us, by that measure, are among those who pass the signs by.
- It located the problem inside us. The eyes and ears work, but the inner machine, the soul, is rusted and blind. ʿIrfan is the long training by which that machine is repaired, so that the soul can "hear, see and reflect".
- It set Islamic ʿIrfan apart from power-seeking mysticism. Powers are not the goal, and seeking them is a form of shirk.
- It showed that the Quran itself speaks of a special class of believers: the muqarrabun and the salihin, those whose hearts tremble, the khashiʿ, those who possess knowledge, the awliyaʾ Allah, and those granted a pure life. The lowest of these ranks is open to ordinary people, though it is very hard to enter.
- Finally, it let the Imams (ʿa) describe the goal in their own words, through the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah and the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers: severance from everything else, the eyes of the heart filled with light, love that is never quenched, and maʿrifah that never stops growing.

For readers from a community that recites these prayers every year, the chapter's most important point may be the simplest one. The texts of ʿIrfan are not foreign to us. Many of us already recite them, in Shaʿban and in the quiet of our own homes. What the lectures offer is a way of reading them as what they are: the words of people who were seeking God and nothing else, given to us so that we might learn to seek Him too.

::: {.box-reflect title="Reflection"}
The lecture compares reciting the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah to a primary school child reading university books, and yet it calls being allowed to recite it an honour. This Shaʿban, or the next time you open the Sahifa, choose a single line from the prayers in this chapter. You might choose "grant me complete severance from everything else", or "who has tasted the sweetness of Your love and wanted something else in its place?" Say it slowly, once a day, and ask yourself honestly: what, at this moment, am I "after" instead of Him? The question is not meant to bring despair. The lecture's promise is that the capacity is already there, because Allah "has created us at this strength and he wants us to reach that strength".
:::
