Many of us know Abu Dharr al-Ghifari from the majalis. After the Prophet ﷺ was gone, he spoke out against the injustice of the rulers, and Murtada Mutahhari records the cost. "This led him to suffer torture and exile, and finally it was in exile, deserted and alone, that he passed away from this world" [@light, p. 20]. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's account the Prophet had said Abu Dharr would live alone, die alone, and be raised alone. <!-- src: L11 ¶35 -->

"A number of orientalists have raised the question of what motivated Abu Dharr to act as he did," Mutahhari goes on [@light, p. 20]. Western books ask the same of the road we have walked, and Mutahhari sets out the choice. Either ʿirfan grew from material Islam supplied, as jurisprudence did, or it came from outside, as medicine did [@light, p. 16]. This chapter's answer is the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams (as) of his Ahl al-Bayt. <!-- src: L11 ¶3-4 -->

::: {.words}
*Sufi*: a name for the early mystics, from *suf*, wool · *zuhd*: renunciation of the world · *tawakkul*: reliance on God · *muhasaba*: self-accounting, going over one's own actions
:::

## The Qur'an, the companions and the Imams

:::: {.point}
The road's material was in Islam from its first generation: in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him.

1. Some picture early Islam as simple, in three parts; Mutahhari rejects the picture. *(Reader told: three parts, each answered in turn.)*
    a. The Qur'an's God is with everything (Q 2:115), and its own words name the road already walked.
    b. The Prophet's life, words, and prayers were full of ecstasy, and the early figures show "burning spiritual love".
    c. Mystics lived in the Prophet's time; Sunni and Shia name three most often. *(Who's Who box.)*
2. Imam ʿAli's sayings hold the teaching most fully, and the chains run to him.
3. The Imams after him taught the same (Schimmel on Imam al-Sadiq, as the Sheikh reports her).
4. An ordinary believer meets that teaching in the prayers we already recite.
5. Therefore: the road's material was in Islam from its first generation.
::::

If the road grew inside Islam, its material should be there from the first generation, and some writers hold that it is not. In their picture of early Islam, God stands outside His world as a builder stands outside a house, piety is giving up this world's pleasures from fear of hell and hope of paradise, and the companions the mystics claim as forerunners were no more than pious men [@light, pp. 16–17]. Mutahhari records this picture and rejects it in one line. "In reality this view can in no way be endorsed," he writes, and the three parts of the picture fail in turn [@light, p. 17].

The Qur'an never pictures God as a builder standing outside His house. It "identifies God as the Creator of the world, stating at the same time that His Holy Essence is everywhere and with everything", as Mutahhari puts it [@light, p. 17]. The first verse he gives for this is in Surah al-Baqarah.

::: {.ayah ref="2:115" words="1-8"}
To Allah belong the east and the west: so whichever way you turn, there is the face of Allah!
:::

Far from standing outside His house, God is there whichever way we turn. Mutahhari then lists the Qur'an's own words for the road we have walked: the meeting with God (*liqaʾ Allah*); the three states of the self, which Chapter Five described; the purification of the soul; and love of God as a passion above every other love [@light, p. 18].

The picture's second part, a piety made only of fear and hope, does not fit the Prophet ﷺ himself. His life, words, and prayers "are rich with spiritual enthusiasm and ecstasy", Mutahhari writes, and the ʿurafaʾ often rely on his supplications for their views [@light, p. 19]. One young companion told him after the dawn prayer that he could see the people of paradise and the people of hell, as we saw in Chapter Three. Mutahhari finds "burning spiritual love" in the lives of the early figures as well [@light, p. 19].

The picture's third part, companions who were no more than pious men, fails on the names the sources give. The lectures count a great number of the companions as mystics in the full sense of the word. Three from the Prophet's own time are named most often in Sunni and Shia sources alike: Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, Salman al-Farsi, and Uways al-Qarani. <!-- src: L11 ¶35, ¶44 -->

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Abu Dharr, Salman, and Uways"}
Abu Dharr al-Ghifari is remembered for his renunciation of the world, above all after the Prophet ﷺ was gone. Salman al-Farsi was a man of great learning when he came to Islam. Once he met the Prophet he gave all of it up to follow him, and after that nothing more is heard of Salman's learning. He later fought beside Imam ʿAli (as). Uways al-Qarani of Yemen is called a sign of divine grace, because the love of the Prophet fell into his heart before he had heard a single verse of the Qur'an. He never met the Prophet, and the lectures do not vouch for the stories told of him in the lives of the saints. He became one of Imam ʿAli's closest disciples and was with him at Siffin, and Chapter Eleven told of his nights of bowing and prostration. <!-- src: L11 ¶35, ¶38-41 -->
:::

Among the companions, the teaching is held most fully in the words of Imam ʿAli (as). "His sayings on this subject are a treasure of knowledge," writes ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, whereas the sayings of the other companions that have come down to us "do not contain enough material on this subject" [@light, pp. 4–5]. The lectures find *Nahj al-Balagha* full of the love of God, and companions with no tie to Imam ʿAli do not speak this way. Most Sunni and Shia mystics trace the chains of their masters back to him through companions such as Salman and Uways [@light, p. 5], and Chapter Fifteen returns to those chains. <!-- src: L11 ¶60-61 -->

The Imams after Imam ʿAli taught the same. The Western historian of Islamic mysticism Annemarie Schimmel accepts that Imam al-Sadiq (as) was one of the greatest teachers of early mysticism. Sheikh Bahmanpour reports her view and would date the teaching back to the earlier Imams and to Imam ʿAli himself. <!-- src: L11 ¶45-46, ¶59-60 -->

We meet that teaching in the prayers already in our hands, which Chapter Three found full of the road's own requests. Mutahhari names them in a single sentence. "The Du'a' Kumayl, the Du'a' Abi Hamzah, the supplications of al-Sahifat al-Kamilah and the group of supplications called Sha'baniyyah, all contain the most sublime spiritual ideas" [@light, p. 20]. Many of us recite the first on Thursday evenings and the second in the nights of Ramadan [@mafatih, pp. 129, 282].

From its first generation Islam therefore held the road's material: in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him.

## The first seekers and the Imams above them

:::: {.point}
The first seekers after the companions learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and stood below them.

1. The next century's ascetics were trained by Imam ʿAli's companions, and in each of Chapter Eleven's three temperaments a well-known seeker learned from an Imam of his day. *(Reader told: one seeker of each temperament.)*
    a. Fear: Fudayl reported abundantly from Imam al-Sadiq; *Lantern of the Path* (*Misbah al-Shariʿa*) is tied to his name, though some name Shaqiq as its author.
    b. Renunciation: Ibrahim ibn Adham, taught by Imam al-Sadiq; his highest level of *zuhd* is found in Imam ʿAli's words (Nahj 3.221).
    c. Love: Maʿruf al-Karkhi, said to have become a Muslim at Imam al-Rida's hands; Rabiʿa is the exception, with no master.
2. Most famous names are nonetheless Sunni, for two reasons.
    a. Under the Umayyads, the Imams' names were dangerous to say (Hasan al-Basri).
    b. The Imams' own followers spoke only of the Imams.
3. The seekers learned from the Imams without ever standing level with them: "an echo".
4. Story: Shaqiq and the young man on the road to Mecca, who was Imam al-Kazim (Q 20:82); for the Sheikh, it shows the mystics "completely different" in calibre from the Imams.
5. Therefore: the first seekers learned from the Imams and stood below them. *(Who's Who box: three more early names.)*
::::

In the century after the companions the teaching passed to men such as Ibrahim ibn Adham, whom the people took for holy men. "They did not talk openly of gnosis or mysticism, though they conceded that they were introduced to spiritualism by the first group and trained by it," writes ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi [@light, p. 5]. The first group is the companions of Imam ʿAli (as), so the line runs from him through his companions to these men. Chapter Eleven sorted the early seekers into those of fear, of renunciation, and of love, and in each group a well-known seeker learned from an Imam of his day.

Fudayl ibn ʿIyad, whose turning from highway robbery Chapter Nine told, was known for his great fear. After his repentance, the lectures say, he reported abundantly from Imam al-Sadiq (as). *Lantern of the Path*, quoted since Chapter One as the book attributed to that Imam, is tied to Fudayl's name as well. Its Arabic title is *Misbah al-Shariʿa*, and Mutahhari writes of Fudayl: "The book Misbah al-Shariah is attributed to him and it is said to consist of a series of lessons that he took from al-'Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq" [@light, p. 25]. The lectures report that some name Shaqiq al-Balkhi as its author instead. <!-- src: L12 ¶10, ¶19-21 -->

Renunciation (*zuhd*) has its master in Ibrahim ibn Adham, who ruled Balkh [@light, p. 24] until he left his palace with no provision. He learned from Imam al-Sadiq while young and became a byword for poverty and reliance on God (*tawakkul*). He is credited with the first classification of renunciation, which has three levels. At the first, the seeker gives up the world's pleasures, and at the second the glad feeling of having done so. At the third, the world seems too slight to renounce. The lectures trace these ideas to the Imams, and the third level is found in a saying of Imam ʿAli (as) in *Nahj al-Balagha*. <!-- src: L11 ¶78; L12 ¶11-16 -->

::: {.quote ar="والله لدنياكم هذه أهون في عيني من عِراق خنزير في يد مجذوم" source="Nahj al-Balagha"}
By God, this world of yours is worth less in my eyes than the bones of a pig in the hand of a leper. [@nahj, 3.221]
:::

We renounce only what has value, and nobody speaks of renouncing a pig's bone in a leper's hand. In Imam ʿAli's eyes the world weighs less than that bone, so there is nothing left to renounce. That is Ibrahim's third level. <!-- src: L12 ¶15 -->

Love has its early voice in Maʿruf al-Karkhi. Mutahhari passes on the report that he became a Muslim at the hands of Imam al-Rida (as) and learned much from him [@light, p. 25]. He was among the first to teach that love is a "divine gift and not an acquisition", so no one can learn it. Chapter Eleven made the same point about the love that carries the traveller. Rabiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya, whose torch and water that chapter told, is the exception among these seekers. As far as is known she studied under no master. <!-- src: L12 ¶38-39, ¶55 -->

Most of the famous early names are nonetheless Sunni; ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi grants that most mystic leaders in the biographies "belonged to the Sunni school of thought" [@light, p. 5]. For seekers who learned from the Imams this needs explaining, and the lectures give two reasons. <!-- src: L11 ¶57 -->

Under the Umayyads, the Imams' names were dangerous to say, and anyone who reported from Imam ʿAli put himself at risk. Hasan al-Basri never saw the Prophet ﷺ, yet he reported many hadith from him without naming the companion in between. Asked who that companion was, he said that it was Imam ʿAli whenever he named none. <!-- src: L11 ¶66-67 -->

In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the Imams' own followers "never spoke of themselves". They passed on only what Imam al-Sadiq or Imam al-Baqir (as) had said, as Salman had fallen silent beside the Prophet. Seekers raised among Sunnis repeated what they heard in their own language, and people were drawn to them. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi finds that the accounts of the early gnostics' visions that have come down to us mostly contain what is found in the sayings and teachings of Imam ʿAli and the other Imams [@light, p. 5]. <!-- src: L11 ¶56-57 -->

The seekers learned from the Imams without ever standing level with them. Much of what the early mystics said was, in the lectures' word, "an echo" of what Imam al-Baqir and Imam al-Sadiq had taught. Rabiʿa's verses do not compare with the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers of Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as), which Chapters Three and Eleven quoted. Before Rabiʿa, the lectures say, love was missing from the mystics' way of fear and hardship. The Sheikh sets the Imams' teaching apart from that judgement. Later mystics repeat Imam al-Sadiq's definition of love as a divine fire that consumes a person whole. <!-- src: L11 ¶51, ¶58-59; L12 ¶38, ¶56 -->

Sheikh Bahmanpour tells a story from the Sunni scholar Ibn al-Jawzi that shows how far below the Imams the mystics stood. Shaqiq al-Balkhi was the pupil of Ibrahim ibn Adham [@light, p. 24] and a master of renunciation in his own right. In 149 AH, the year after Imam al-Sadiq died, he set out for hajj. <!-- src: L12 ¶30-31 -->

At the resting place of al-Qadisiyya, Shaqiq saw a handsome young man who sat apart from everyone in the woollen dress of the Sufis and without proper shoes. He took him for one of the idlers who dressed as Sufis and lived off the caravan, and he went over to rebuke him. Before he could speak, the young man called him by name and gave him a warning that is also in the Qur'an (49:12): "Avoid much suspicion; indeed some suspicions are sins." <!-- src: L12 ¶31-32 -->

The words answered what Shaqiq had been thinking, and he resolved to follow the young man, who slipped away from him. At the next stop, Waqisa, Shaqiq found him praying with his whole body shaking and tears running down his face. Shaqiq went to apologise, and the young man finished his prayer and recited a verse of Surah Ta Ha. <!-- src: L12 ¶32-33 -->

::: {.ayah ref="20:82"}
Indeed I forgive those who repent, become faithful, act righteously, and follow guidance.
:::

Twice now the young man had answered what was in Shaqiq's heart. In Mecca Shaqiq saw him again by the spout of the Kaʿba, where he wept and prayed through the night and circled the Kaʿba at dawn. Shaqiq followed him to a great house full of relatives, slaves, money, and property. People crowded round him there for his blessing, and one of them told Shaqiq that he was Imam Musa ibn Jaʿfar al-Kazim (as). Mutahhari, too, relays a report of their meeting [@light, p. 24]. For the Sheikh, the mystics were "completely different in terms of their calibre of understanding and how they behave". <!-- src: L12 ¶34-37 -->

The first seekers therefore learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and the Imams stood above them.

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: three more early names"}
Murtada Mutahhari begins his history of ʿirfan with Hasan al-Basri (d. 110/728), and some orders trace their chains through him to Imam ʿAli (as) [@light, p. 23]. Sheikh Bahmanpour finds him harder to read, since his loyalties in the civil wars of Imam ʿAli's time were ambivalent, and it was Hasan who reported from the Imam without naming him. Al-Harith al-Muhasibi of Basra (d. 243/857) was "called 'al-muhasibi' due to his great diligence in the matter of self-observation and self-reckoning (muhasabah)" [@light, p. 26]. The lectures call this practice "still persistent in our mysticism". Dhu'l-Nun al-Misri lived in Egypt, where the English orientalist R. A. Nicholson thought Neoplatonic ideas had touched him. Yet Dhu'l-Nun taught that the sign of the lover of God is to follow the Prophet ﷺ in his morals, his deeds, his commands, and his customs. In Annemarie Schimmel's view, as the lectures report it, the thoughts of Imam al-Sadiq, and perhaps of other early mystics, "must have been at work beneath the surface" until they appeared in Sufis such as Dhu'l-Nun and al-Muhasibi. <!-- src: L11 ¶58, ¶64, ¶67; L12 ¶4, ¶63-64 -->
:::

## Not brought in from outside

:::: {.point}
The road was not brought in from outside; some techniques may have been borrowed, and the later theory drew on outside ideas.

1. Western books looked elsewhere because they doubted that a religion of law could produce mystics, and they named three candidates. *(Reader told: three candidates, each answered in turn.)*
    a. Christian monks: similar practices and the wool, though any borrowing would be of methods, not ideas; the Qur'an calls monasticism an innovation (Q 57:27), and for the Sheikh "this is not the way"; celibacy and lodges differ.
    b. Neoplatonism: it came late, with the theory, which outside currents, especially philosophy, influenced.
    c. Buddhism: real arguments, but the concepts differ; whether renunciation came from it is hard to tell, since the Qur'an has it; some techniques may have been borrowed.
2. Mutahhari is certain of the source, Nicholson and Massignon admit it, and Jurdaq's pitcher answers the orientalists over Abu Dharr: "the great ocean of Islam".
3. Therefore (section and chapter): the road began in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams (as); its first seekers learned from the Imams and stood below them; from outside it may have taken some techniques and, later, ideas for its theory.

::::

Many Western books looked elsewhere all the same, because their authors, in the lectures' words, "cannot believe that Islam as a religion of law and jurisprudence can produce mystics". Chapter Six showed the law and the inner path to be one road. Over time those books proposed three sources: Christian monks, Neoplatonism, and Buddhism. <!-- src: L11 ¶3-4, ¶12, ¶21 -->

The case for Christian monks rests on practices found in both: abstinence, celibacy, the recitation of God's name, and above all the woollen dress. The early Sufis wore coarse wool, and their name comes from it, since *suf* is Arabic for wool [@light, p. 22]. Even on this view, the lectures note, what came over would be methods rather than ideas. The Qur'an had already named the monks' way as their own invention. <!-- src: L11 ¶5-6 -->

::: {.ayah ref="57:27" words="19-27"}
But as for monasticism, they innovated it—We had not prescribed it for them—only seeking Allah’s pleasure.
:::

In the lectures' reading, those who began monasticism wanted more than ordinary worship, so they left the world for the pleasure of God and made a method God had not written for them. The verse goes on to say that God rewarded the faithful among them. Even so, in the Sheikh's words, "this is not the way", and on his reading a road grown from the Qur'an had no need to copy what the Qur'an calls an innovation. Celibacy, honoured among Christian monks, did not sit well with the Muslim mystics. Their lodges are unlike monasteries too, which makes a Christian origin "very difficult to prove". <!-- src: L11 ¶6, ¶10-12 -->

Neoplatonism, the second candidate, drew on the ideas of Plato, Aristotle, and Pythagoras [@light, p. 16]. It reached the mystics only centuries later, when they set out to build ʿirfan into a theory; the seekers of the second, third, and fourth centuries did not have it. Mutahhari records a third view of ʿirfan's origins, between the two this chapter opened with. In it ʿirfan took its material from Islam, and in giving that material a structure it was influenced by outside currents and especially by philosophy [@light, p. 16]. <!-- src: L11 ¶20-21 -->

The case for Buddhism has real force. Many early renunciants came from Khurasan, whose borders then reached India and China. Ibrahim ibn Adham, the prince of Balkh, is often compared with the Buddha [@light, p. 24]. Yet the concepts of the two differ too much for one doctrine to have come from the other, as Chapter Twelve found with annihilation and nirvana. Whether even renunciation came from Buddhism is hard to tell, the Sheikh grants, since the Qur'an already has it. Ibrahim himself had learned from Imam al-Sadiq (as). Some techniques may have been borrowed, but not the doctrine. Julian Baldick, in *Mystical Islam*, holds the same view. <!-- src: L11 ¶21-23, ¶74; L07 ¶51; L12 ¶11 -->

Whatever the techniques, Mutahhari is certain that ʿirfan "has derived its basic sources of inspiration from Islam itself and from nowhere else" [@light, p. 16]. Nicholson and Louis Massignon, he adds, "have expressly admitted that the principal sources of 'irfan are the Quran and the Prophet's Sunnah" [@light, pp. 20–21]. The Lebanese Christian George Jurdaq had answered the orientalists over Abu Dharr. They are like people who see a man by a sea with a full pitcher, he wrote, and ignore the sea to search for the pool he filled it from [@light, p. 20]. In ʿirfan, Mutahhari finds, they "completely overlook the great ocean of Islam" [@light, p. 20].

The road we have walked therefore began in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams (as). Its first seekers learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and stood below them. From outside it may have taken some techniques, and its theorists drew on outside ideas only later.

Where did those travellers arrive?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
Self-accounting (*muhasaba*) gave al-Harith al-Muhasibi his name. Fix a set time each day to go over the past day's actions. Where you find a failure, ask God's forgiveness, and where you find none, thank Him [@kernel, p. 68]. *Kernel of the Kernels* roots the practice in a saying of Imam Musa al-Kazim (as), the young man Shaqiq met on the road to Mecca. Whoever does not take account of his own soul once every day, the Imam said, is not one of us [@kernel, p. 68].
:::
