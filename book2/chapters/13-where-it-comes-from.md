Many of us know Abu Dharr al-Ghifari from the majalis. After the Prophet ﷺ was gone, he spoke out against the favouritism and injustice of the rulers of his time, and Murtada Mutahhari records the cost. "This led him to suffer torture and exile, and finally it was in exile, deserted and alone, that he passed away from this world" [@light, p. 20]. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's account the Prophet had said that Abu Dharr would live alone, die alone, and be raised alone. Abu Dharr did die alone in a faraway desert. <!-- src: L11 ¶35 -->

"A number of orientalists have raised the question of what motivated Abu Dharr to act as he did," Mutahhari goes on, and they searched for something foreign to Islam to explain him [@light, p. 20]. Western books on Islamic mysticism ask the same question of the whole road we have walked from repentance to annihilation. Either ʿirfan grew from material Islam itself supplied, as jurisprudence and Qur'an commentary did, Mutahhari writes, or it came in from outside, as medicine and mathematics did [@light, p. 16]. This chapter sets out where the road comes from. Its answer is the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams (as) of his Ahl al-Bayt. <!-- src: L11 ¶3-4 -->

::: {.words}
*Sufi*: a name for the early mystics, from *suf*, wool · *zuhd*: renunciation of the world · *tawakkul*: trust in God · *muhasaba*: self-accounting, going over one's own actions
:::

## The Qur'an, the companions and the Imams

:::: {.point}
The road's material was in Islam from its first generation: in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him.

1. Some picture early Islam as simple, in three parts; Mutahhari rejects the picture. *(Reader told: three parts, each answered in turn.)*
    a. The Qur'an's God is with everything (Q 2:115), and its own words name the road already walked.
    b. The Prophet's life, words, and prayers were full of ecstasy, and the early figures show "burning spiritual love".
    c. Mystics lived in the Prophet's time; Sunni and Shia name three most often. *(Who's Who box.)*
2. Imam ʿAli's sayings hold the teaching most fully, and the chains run to him.
3. The Imams after him taught the same (Schimmel on Imam al-Sadiq, as the Sheikh reports her).
4. An ordinary believer meets that teaching in the prayers we already recite.
5. Therefore: the road's material was in Islam from its first generation.
::::

If the road grew inside Islam, its material should be there from the first generation, and some writers hold that it is not. In their picture of early Islam, God stands outside His world as a builder stands outside a house, piety is giving up this world's pleasures from fear of hell and hope of paradise, and the companions the mystics claim as forerunners were no more than pious men [@light, pp. 16–17]. Mutahhari records this picture and answers it in one line. "In reality this view can in no way be endorsed," he writes, and the three parts of the picture fail in turn [@light, p. 17].

The Qur'an never pictures God as a builder standing outside His house. It "identifies God as the Creator of the world, stating at the same time that His Holy Essence is everywhere and with everything", as Mutahhari puts it [@light, p. 17]. The first verse he gives for this is in Surah al-Baqarah.

::: {.ayah ref="2:115" words="1-8"}
To Allah belong the east and the west: so whichever way you turn, there is the face of Allah!
:::

Whichever way we turn in prayer or anywhere else, the face of Allah is there, and that nearness is what the road has rested on from its first step. Mutahhari then lists the Qur'an's own words for the road we have already walked: the meeting with God (*liqaʾ Allah*); the commanding self, the self-reproaching self, and the self at peace, which Chapter Five described; the purification of the soul; and love of God as a passion above every other love [@light, p. 18].

The picture's second part, a piety made only of fear and hope, does not fit the Prophet ﷺ himself. His life, words, and prayers "are rich with spiritual enthusiasm and ecstasy", Mutahhari writes, and the ʿurafaʾ often rely on his supplications for their views [@light, p. 19]. One young companion told him after the dawn prayer that he could see the people of paradise and the people of hell, as we saw in Chapter Three. Mutahhari finds "burning spiritual love" in the lives of the early figures as well [@light, p. 19].

The picture's third part, companions who were no more than pious men, fails on the names the sources give. The lectures count a great number of the companions as mystics in the full sense of the word. Three of them, all from the Prophet's own time, are named most often in Sunni and Shia sources alike: Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, Salman al-Farsi, and Uways al-Qarani. <!-- src: L11 ¶35, ¶44 -->

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Abu Dharr, Salman, and Uways"}
Abu Dharr al-Ghifari is remembered for his renunciation of the world, above all after the Prophet ﷺ was gone. Salman al-Farsi was a man of great learning when he came to Islam. Once he met the Prophet he gave all of it up to follow him, and after that nothing more is heard of Salman's learning. He later fought beside Imam ʿAli (as). Uways al-Qarani of Yemen is called a sign of divine grace, because the love of the Prophet fell into his heart before he had heard a single verse of the Qur'an. He never met the Prophet, and the lectures do not vouch for the stories told of him in the lives of the saints. He became one of Imam ʿAli's closest disciples and was with him at Siffin, and Chapter Eleven told of his nights of bowing and prostration. <!-- src: L11 ¶35, ¶38-41 -->
:::

Among the companions, the teaching is held most fully in the words of Imam ʿAli (as). "His sayings on this subject are a treasure of knowledge," writes ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, whereas the sayings of the other companions that have come down to us "do not contain enough material on this subject" [@light, pp. 4–5]. The lectures find *Nahj al-Balagha* full of the love of God, and companions with no tie to Imam ʿAli do not speak this way. Most Sunni and Shia mystics trace the chains of their masters back to him through companions such as Salman and Uways [@light, p. 5], and Chapter Fifteen returns to those chains. <!-- src: L11 ¶60-61 -->

The Imams after Imam ʿAli taught the same. Annemarie Schimmel, a Western historian of Islamic mysticism, accepts that Imam al-Sadiq (as) was one of the greatest teachers of early mysticism, as Sheikh Bahmanpour reports her, and he would date the teaching further back still, to the earlier Imams and to Imam ʿAli himself. <!-- src: L11 ¶45-46, ¶59-60 -->

We meet that teaching in the prayers already in our hands, which Chapter Three found full of the road's own requests. Mutahhari names them in a single sentence. "The Du'a' Kumayl, the Du'a' Abi Hamzah, the supplications of al-Sahifat al-Kamilah and the group of supplications called Sha'baniyyah, all contain the most sublime spiritual ideas" [@light, p. 20]. Many of us recite the first on Thursday evenings and the second in the nights of Ramadan.

From its first generation Islam therefore held the road's material: in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him.

## The first seekers and the Imams above them

:::: {.point}
The first seekers after the companions learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and stood below them.

1. The next century's ascetics were trained by Imam ʿAli's companions, and the best known master of each temperament learned from an Imam of his day. *(Reader told: one master of each of Chapter Eleven's three temperaments.)*
    a. Fear: Fudayl; *Lantern of the Path* belongs to a second-century seeker and carries Imam al-Sadiq's name.
    b. Renunciation: Ibrahim ibn Adham, taught by Imam al-Sadiq; his highest level of *zuhd* is found in Imam ʿAli's words (Nahj 3.221).
    c. Love: Maʿruf al-Karkhi, a Muslim at Imam al-Rida's hands; Rabiʿa is the exception, with no master.
2. Most famous names are nonetheless Sunni, for two reasons.
    a. Under the Umayyads, the Imams' names were dangerous to say.
    b. The Imams' own followers spoke only of the Imams.
3. The seekers learned from the Imams without ever standing level with them: "an echo".
4. Story: Shaqiq and the young man on the road to Mecca, who was Imam al-Kazim (Q 20:82).
5. For the Sheikh, the story shows the mystics "completely different" in calibre from the Imams.
6. Therefore: the first seekers learned from the Imams and stood below them. *(Who's Who box: three more early names.)*
::::

In the century after the companions, the teaching passed to men such as Ibrahim ibn Adham, whom the people took for holy men and who seemed only ascetics. "They did not talk openly of gnosis or mysticism, though they conceded that they were introduced to spiritualism by the first group and trained by it," writes ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi [@light, p. 5]. The first group, on the same page, is the companions of Imam ʿAli (as), so the line runs from him through his companions to these men. Chapter Eleven sorted the early seekers into those of fear, of renunciation, and of love, and the best known master of each learned from an Imam of his day.

The master of fear is Fudayl ibn ʿIyad, whose turning from highway robbery Chapter Nine told. *Lantern of the Path*, quoted since Chapter One as the book attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), is tied to his name. "The book Misbah al-Shariah is attributed to him and it is said to consist of a series of lessons that he took from al-'Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (A)," Mutahhari writes of Fudayl [@light, p. 25], and the lectures report that some name Shaqiq al-Balkhi as its author instead. On either account a seeker of the second century set down the teaching under Imam al-Sadiq's name. <!-- src: L12 ¶10, ¶21 -->

Renunciation (*zuhd*) has its master in Ibrahim ibn Adham, who ruled Balkh [@light, p. 24] until he left his palace with no provision. He learned from Imam al-Sadiq while still young and became a byword for poverty and for trust in God (*tawakkul*). He is credited with the first classification of renunciation, which has three levels. At the first, the seeker gives up the world's pleasures, and at the second he gives up the glad feeling of having done so. At the third, the world seems too slight to renounce. The lectures trace these ideas to the Imams, and the third level is found in a saying of Imam ʿAli (as) recorded in *Nahj al-Balagha*. <!-- src: L11 ¶78; L12 ¶11-16 -->

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of Nahj 3.221; the nahj extract has no Arabic for this saying]" source="Nahj al-Balagha"}
By God, this world of yours is worth less in my eyes than the bones of a pig in the hand of a leper. [@nahj, 3.221]
:::

We renounce only what has value, and nobody speaks of renouncing a pig's bone in a leper's hand. In Imam ʿAli's eyes the world weighs less than that bone, so there is nothing left to renounce, and that is Ibrahim's third level. <!-- src: L12 ¶15 -->

Love has its early voice in Maʿruf al-Karkhi, who "became a Muslim at the hands of al- 'Imam al-Rida (A), learning much from him" [@light, p. 25]. He was among the first to teach that love is a "divine gift and not an acquisition", so no one can learn it. Chapter Eleven made the same point about the love that carries the traveller. Rabiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya, whose torch and water that chapter told, is the exception among these seekers, since as far as is known she studied under no master. <!-- src: L12 ¶38-39, ¶55 -->

Most of the famous early names are nonetheless Sunni; ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi grants that most mystic leaders in the biographies "belonged to the Sunni school of thought" [@light, p. 5]. For seekers who learned from the Imams this needs explaining, and the lectures give two reasons. <!-- src: L11 ¶57 -->

Under the Umayyads, the Imams' names were dangerous to say, and anyone who reported from Imam ʿAli put himself at risk. Hasan al-Basri never saw the Prophet ﷺ, yet he reported many hadith from him without naming the companion in between. Asked who that companion was, he said that it was Imam ʿAli whenever he named none. Shaqiq al-Balkhi likewise reported from the Ahl al-Bayt without naming them, because in his town those who reported from the Ahl al-Bayt did not name them. <!-- src: L11 ¶66-67; L12 ¶24 -->

The Imams' own followers, for their part, said nothing in their own name. The Shia "never spoke of themselves", in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, and passed on only what Imam al-Sadiq or Imam al-Baqir (as) had said, as Salman had fallen silent beside the Prophet. Seekers raised among Sunnis repeated what they heard in their own language, and people were drawn to them. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi finds that what has come down to us of the early gnostics' visions mostly holds what the sayings of Imam ʿAli and the other Imams hold [@light, p. 5]. <!-- src: L11 ¶56-57 -->

The seekers learned from the Imams without ever standing level with them. What the early mystics said was, in the lectures' word, "an echo" of what Imam al-Baqir and Imam al-Sadiq had taught. Rabiʿa's verses do not compare with the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers of Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as), which Chapters Three and Eleven quoted. Love itself, the lectures say, was missing among the mystics before Rabiʿa, and they set the Imams' teaching apart in the same breath. Later mystics repeat a definition of love as a divine fire that consumes a person whole. It is the Imams' own, from *Lantern of the Path*, and Chapter Eleven quoted it. <!-- src: L11 ¶51, ¶58-59; L12 ¶38, ¶56 -->

Sheikh Bahmanpour tells a story, reported by the Sunni scholar Ibn al-Jawzi, that shows how far below them the mystics stood. Shaqiq al-Balkhi was the pupil of Ibrahim ibn Adham [@light, p. 24] and a master of renunciation in his own right. In 149 AH, the year after Imam al-Sadiq died, he set out for hajj. <!-- src: L12 ¶30-31 -->

At the resting place of al-Qadisiyya, Shaqiq saw a handsome young man who sat apart from everyone in the woollen dress of the Sufis and without proper shoes. He took him for one of the idlers who dressed as Sufis and lived off the caravan, and he went over to rebuke him. Before he could speak, the young man said, "Shaqiq," and told him in the Qur'an's own words (49:12): "Avoid much suspicion; indeed some suspicions are sins." <!-- src: L12 ¶31-32 -->

The words answered what Shaqiq had been thinking, and he resolved to follow the young man, who slipped away from him. At the next stop, Waqisa, Shaqiq found him praying with his whole body shaking and tears running down his face. Shaqiq went to apologise, and the young man finished his prayer and recited a verse of Surah Ta Ha. <!-- src: L12 ¶32-33 -->

::: {.ayah ref="20:82"}
Indeed I forgive those who repent, become faithful, act righteously, and follow guidance.
:::

Twice now the young man had answered what was in Shaqiq's heart before he could say it. In Mecca Shaqiq saw him again by the spout of the Kaʿba, where he wept and prayed through the night until dawn. When dawn came, he prayed and circled the Kaʿba. Shaqiq followed him out to a great house full of relatives, slaves, money, and property. People crowded round him there to take his blessing. Shaqiq asked one of them who the man was and was told that he was Imam Musa ibn Jaʿfar al-Kazim (as). Mutahhari, too, records that Shaqiq met this Imam and told of his great station [@light, p. 24]. <!-- src: L12 ¶34-37 -->

For the Sheikh, the story shows that the mystics stood apart from the Imams, "completely different in terms of their calibre of understanding and how they behave". Shaqiq, himself a master of renunciation, had judged the Imam by his woollen dress and been answered twice from the Qur'an. <!-- src: L12 ¶37 -->

The first seekers therefore learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and the Imams stood above them.

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: three more early names"}
Hasan al-Basri (d. 110/728) is where Murtada Mutahhari begins the history of ʿirfan, and some orders trace their chains through him to Imam ʿAli (as) [@light, p. 23]. Sheikh Bahmanpour finds him harder to read, since his loyalties in the civil wars of Imam ʿAli's time were ambivalent, and it was Hasan who reported from the Imam without naming him. Al-Harith al-Muhasibi of Basra (d. 243/857) was "called 'al-muhasibi' due to his great diligence in the matter of self-observation and self-reckoning (muhasabah)" [@light, p. 26]. The lectures call this practice "still persistent in our mysticism". Dhu'l-Nun al-Misri lived in Egypt, where Nicholson thought Neoplatonic ideas had touched him. Yet Dhu'l-Nun taught that the sign of the lover of God is to follow the Prophet ﷺ in his morals, his deeds, his commands, and his customs. Annemarie Schimmel, as the lectures report her, names Dhu'l-Nun and al-Muhasibi among the Sufis in whom the Imams' teaching, at work "beneath the surface", came into view. <!-- src: L11 ¶58, ¶64, ¶67; L12 ¶4, ¶63-64 -->
:::

## Not brought in from outside

:::: {.point}
The road was not brought in from outside; at most, some techniques were borrowed, and the later theory drew on outside ideas.

1. Western books looked elsewhere because they doubted that a religion of law could produce mystics, and they named three candidates. *(Reader told: three candidates, each answered in turn.)*
    a. Christian monks: the Qur'an itself calls monasticism an innovation (Q 57:27), and the Muslim mystics' practice differs.
    b. Neoplatonism: it came late, with the theory, which outside currents influenced.
    c. Buddhism: the concepts differ, renunciation is in the Qur'an, and the prince of Balkh learned from Imam al-Sadiq; at most some techniques.
2. Mutahhari leaves the extent of influence to separate study and is certain of the source.
3. The best Western scholars came round: "Surely the seeds of mysticism are here."
4. Jurdaq's pitcher answers the orientalists over Abu Dharr, and Mutahhari applies it to ʿirfan: "the great ocean of Islam".
5. Therefore (section and chapter): the road began in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams (as); its first seekers learned from the Imams and stood below them; from outside it took at most some techniques and, later, ideas for its theory.

::::

Many Western books looked elsewhere all the same, and the lectures give the reason. Their authors "cannot believe that Islam as a religion of law and jurisprudence can produce mystics". Chapter Six showed the opposite, that the law and the inner path are one road. Over time those books proposed three sources: Christian monks, Neoplatonism, and Buddhism. <!-- src: L11 ¶3-4, ¶12, ¶21 -->

The case for Christian monks rests on dress. The early Sufis wore coarse wool, and their name comes from it, since *suf* is Arabic for wool [@light, p. 22]. The Qur'an, which this chapter has shown to be the road's source, had already named the monks' way as their own invention. <!-- src: L11 ¶5 -->

::: {.ayah ref="57:27" words="19-27"}
But as for monasticism, they innovated it—We had not prescribed it for them—only seeking Allah’s pleasure.
:::

A road grown from the Qur'an had no warrant to copy monasticism, so the wool proves little. The lectures keep the verse's balance. Those who began monasticism wanted more than ordinary worship, so they left the world for the pleasure of God and made a method God had not written for them. The verse itself goes on to say that God gave the faithful among them their reward. Even so, in the Sheikh's words, "this is not the way". Celibacy, honoured among Christian monks, did not sit well with the Muslim mystics. Their lodges are unlike monasteries too, which makes a Christian origin "very difficult to prove". <!-- src: L11 ¶6, ¶10-12 -->

Neoplatonism, the second candidate, drew on the ideas of Plato, Aristotle, and Pythagoras [@light, p. 16]. It reached the mystics late, only when, centuries on, they set out to build ʿirfan into a theory. The seekers of the second, third, and fourth centuries did not have it. Mutahhari records a third view of ʿirfan's origins. In it ʿirfan took its material from Islam, and in giving that material a structure it was influenced by outside currents and above all by philosophy [@light, p. 16]. <!-- src: L11 ¶20-21 -->

Buddhism makes the strongest case. Many early renunciants came from Khurasan, whose borders then reached India and China. Ibrahim ibn Adham, the prince of Balkh who left his palace, is often compared with the Buddha [@light, p. 24]. Yet the concepts of the two traditions differ too much for one doctrine to have come from the other, as Chapter Twelve found annihilation to differ from nirvana. The attitude of renunciation is in the Qur'an itself, and the prince of Balkh learned his renunciation from Imam al-Sadiq (as). At most some techniques were borrowed, and the doctrine was not. Julian Baldick, author of *Mystical Islam*, holds the same view. <!-- src: L11 ¶21-23, ¶74; L07 ¶51; L12 ¶11 -->

Mutahhari leaves the question of how far outside influence went to separate study. The source itself he holds beyond doubt. "But that which is certain is that 'irfan has derived its basic sources of inspiration from Islam itself and from nowhere else" [@light, p. 16].

The best of Western scholarship came round to the same answer. The English orientalist Nicholson and the French orientalist Louis Massignon, after long study of ʿirfan, "have expressly admitted that the principal sources of 'irfan are the Quran and the Prophet's Sunnah" [@light, pp. 20–21]. The lectures count Annemarie Schimmel among those who accept that it came from the Qur'an and hadith. After setting out the Qur'an's verses on God's light, His face, and His nearness, Nicholson writes: "Surely the seeds of mysticism are here" [@light, p. 21]. <!-- src: L11 ¶4 -->

The question about Abu Dharr with which this chapter opened was answered by the Lebanese Christian writer George Jurdaq. Jurdaq likened the orientalists to people who see a man standing by a sea with a full pitcher and then ignore the sea to go in search of the pool he filled it from [@light, p. 20]. Mutahhari draws the same line for ʿirfan: "The orientalists are in search of a non-Islamic source of inspiration of 'irfan, while they completely overlook the great ocean of Islam" [@light, p. 20].

The road we have walked therefore began in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams (as). Its first seekers learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and stood below them. From outside it took at most some techniques, and its theorists drew on outside ideas only later.

Al-Harith al-Muhasibi was named for self-accounting (*muhasaba*), and the lectures call the practice "still persistent in our mysticism". Fix a time each night to go over the day's actions. Where you find a failure, ask God's forgiveness, and where you find none, thank Him [@kernel, p. 68]. *Kernel of the Kernels* roots the practice in a saying of Imam Musa al-Kazim (as), the young man Shaqiq met on the road to Mecca.
