::: {.quote ar="وَلاَ يَغْفُلُ عَنْ شُكْرِكَ وَلاَ يَسْتَخِفُّ بِأَمْرِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
who do not fail to show You their gratitude and do not take Your orders lightly [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §126–127]
:::

These words come near the end of the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. The one who prays asks to be among those who always remember God, never fail to thank Him, and never take His commands lightly. Remembering God and keeping His commands are asked for in the same breath, as parts of one life.

In our own prayers the two often come apart. We stand for salah, raise our hands, and say *Allahu akbar*, and within moments the mind is asking whether the car was locked. The law judges the outward form of the prayer, and the form was correct, so the prayer counts even though the remembering was lost [@forty_hadith, p. 293].

That gap can suggest a conclusion. The journey towards God is an inward one, worked out in the self. If the remembering is what matters, and the rules can be kept without it, perhaps the rules are an outer layer to be left behind as the traveller goes deeper. Some mystical traditions have taught exactly this: train the heart, and do not trouble over the details of the law.

Most of the masters of the path in Islam, Shia and Sunni alike, taught the opposite. This chapter sets out why there is no path outside the law. The rules we keep and the remembering we lose are one religion at three depths. The deeper ones are reached only through the law, and the law is kept to the end of the road.

::: {.words}
*shariʿa*: the sacred law; what the Prophet ﷺ taught us to do · *tariqa*: the path; the inward way of doing what the law asks · *haqiqa*: the truth that the path leads to · *karamat*: wonders worked by those close to God
:::

## Shell, core, and kernel

::: {.point}
The law, the path, and the truth are one religion at three depths, reached in order.

1. The law tells us what to do and how to do it validly.
2. We keep the law to draw near to God, by a path that leads to the truth.
3. A jurist can judge only the outward form of an act.
4. The path and the truth lie inside that form: in how the act is done within, and in what is seen by doing it.
5. The Prophet ﷺ summed this up: his words are the law, his actions the path, his states the truth.
6. The three are therefore one reality, differing only in depth.
7. A single prayer holds all three depths.
8. The order runs one way: the law can be kept without the path, but the path cannot be walked without the law.
9. Therefore: the depths are reached in order, and none can be skipped.
:::

Let's start with the three words the ʿurafaʾ use for the depths of the religion. The first is the law (*shariʿa*): how and when to pray and to fast, and what each act needs before it is valid. It is what the Prophet ﷺ taught us to do, and it is what most of us mean by practising the religion.

The law is not kept for its own sake. We pray and fast to draw near to God, and the way from the law to that nearness is called the path (*tariqa*). The path leads in turn to the truth (*haqiqa*), the reality that is seen once the path has been walked.

The path is not a second set of acts beside the law. Suppose we prayed beside the Prophet ﷺ and copied every movement he made. A jurist would rule both prayers valid, having checked the standing, the bowing, the words recited, and the time. The outward form of an act is all a jurist can judge, and both forms were the same.

What a jurist cannot judge is what passed in the heart. Imam Khomeini draws the same line: "there is no necessary relation between the (legal) validity of ʿibadah and its acceptability" [@forty_hadith, p. 293]. The Prophet's prayer and ours would differ in the inward way each was performed, and that inward way is the path. The outward frame of the act is still the law. What the Prophet saw by praying as he prayed is the truth.

A saying attributed to the Prophet ﷺ puts the three in one line: "The shariʿah is my words, tariqah my actions, haqiqah my states" [@inner_secrets, p. 32]. His words tell us what to do. His actions show how it is done from within, and his states are what he saw by doing it.

If the path and the truth lie inside the law, the three are not separate roads. Mulla ʿAbd al-Samad Hamadani, a Shia ʿarif who had a hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt (as) ready for every point of the path, made this his teaching. He held that the law, the path, and the truth are different names for one reality seen from different sides. They differ only in depth: the law is the shell, the path the core, and the truth the kernel.[Murtada Mutahhari gives the same order: "One is outward shell, another is inward kernel, and the third is the kernel of the kernel." @light, p. 15]

Hamadani found all three depths in a single salah. As service to God (*khidma*), the prayer belongs to the law. As a drawing near (*qurb*), it belongs to the path, and as an arrival (*wusul*), it belongs to the truth.[Sayyid Haydar Amuli records the saying: "Surely prayer is service, a coming closer and an arrival." @inner_secrets, p. 32] A prayer like the Prophet's does all three at once: it serves God, it draws near to Him, and it arrives.

The order between the depths runs one way only. The law can be kept without the path, as the prayer with the wandering mind shows: it was valid, and it served, yet it did not draw near. The path cannot be walked without the law, because the path is the inside of the law's own acts and has nothing else to work on.[Sayyid Haydar Amuli puts the order as a rule: "shariʿah is possible without tariqah although tariqah is not possible without shariʿah." @inner_secrets, p. 48]

Each depth is therefore reached only through the one before it, and none can be skipped. Sheikh Bahmanpour gives an image for this, which he credits to Najm al-Din Kubra, the head of one of the Sufi orders. The law is a ship, the path is a sea, and the truth is a pearl in that sea. The pearl "cannot be found except by sailing through the sea, and you cannot sail without a ship". Whoever abandons that order will not find the pearl. This is why the ʿurafaʾ say that the beginning of ʿirfan is the shariʿa.

## The inner never cancels the outer

::: {.point}
Nothing inward can stand in for the law, so the inner work is done inside it, beginning with attention in prayer.

1. Anyone who works wonders but abandons one command of God is lying.
2. The wonders may be real; the lie is the claim to know God.
3. The law cannot be bypassed, because it purifies the character that leads to knowing God.
4. Knowing God comes through a purified self, not through study, so dropping the law breaks the chain at its first link.
5. The light of knowing God does not descend on a heart that sets the law aside.
6. What comes without the law is easily mistaken for that light.
7. Even the experiences that come on the path are only a preliminary to a lasting change in the self.
8. That lasting change is made by keeping the law for a whole life.
9. That lifelong keeping is where the inner work begins, at the first words of the prayer.
10. Knowing what we say in prayer needs attention, and attention cannot be held by force.
11. Standing before God ought to hold the mind, as standing at a cliff edge would.
12. It does not, because presence needs knowledge and knowledge needs attentive practice: a circle.
13. The faculty of attention is not missing, since a book can hold it.
14. Therefore: the inner work is done inside the law, by bringing attention to prayer a degree at a time.
:::

If the truth is reached only through the law, someone who claims the truth while setting the law aside has left the ship. Kubra draws this conclusion from his own image. Suppose a person is seen flying through the air, walking on the sea, or eating fire. If they have abandoned "even one injunction of God", Kubra says, "you should know that they are liars".

"Liars" does not mean that the feats are faked, because such things can be done. The lie is in the claim to be a true ʿarif, one who knows God. The person works these wonders for their own desires, and what they do is not a wonder of the friends of God (*karama*) but magic (*sihr*).

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: karamat, muʿjiza, and sihr"}
Three words name wonders that can look alike. A miracle (*muʿjiza*) is worked only by a prophet, by God's permission. A *karama* (plural *karamat*) is a wonder at the hands of someone close to God. Sheikh Bahmanpour describes it as worked through the strength of the human soul. Magic (*sihr*) produces effects that can look the same by other means, usually with the help of the jinn. To an onlooker, a flight through the air looks the same whoever performs it. Kubra's test therefore looks past the feat to the person's keeping of the law, and one abandoned command of God marks the feat as magic.
:::

The law cannot be bypassed because of what it does to the one who keeps it. Ayatullah Muhammad Bahari, an Iranian ʿarif, set out the order. Jurisprudence, he taught, is "an introductory means" to purifying one's character. Good character, in turn, is the way into knowledge of God's oneness (*tawhid*).

Each link in that order needs the one before it. Theology can be learned in a classroom, where a student can set out how Muslim theology differs from Christian theology. The reality of God, by contrast, is found only through purifying the self, so without good character there is no knowledge of God. Without the law lived in oneself there is no good character. A traveller who claims to be an ʿarif with no further need of the law, as if it were meant for ordinary people, has broken the chain at its first link.

Imam Khomeini reaches the same conclusion through the image of light: "Without adhering to the apparent teachings of Islam it is not possible that the light of the Divine Knowledge could descend upon his heart" [@forty_hadith, p. 35]. Knowledge of God comes to the heart as a light, and a heart that has set the law aside is not made ready to receive it.

What does come without the law is easily mistaken for that light. Sheikh Bahmanpour warns that anyone of any belief will find something if they go forty days on less and less food. The knowledge of God is so unfamiliar to us that whatever is found is taken for God.

Even the experiences that come on the true path are not the goal. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, in the lectures recorded in *Kernel of the Kernels*, speaks of the states a traveller passes through and the lights that may appear from time to time. All of these, he writes, are "a preliminary to an enduring condition" [@kernel, p. 30]. An experience comes and goes. What counts is a lasting change in the self, and "the smallest lapse in wayfaring and spiritual jihad would bring him down again to the lower world" [@kernel, p. 31].

That lasting change is made by keeping the law, a harder discipline than any forty-day exercise. It asks for worship every day and night and at every dawn. It asks for watch at every moment over a tongue that might backbite and ears that might listen to what is vile. A forty-day exercise ends, whereas the law is kept for a whole life, and the real ʿarif is the one who keeps it without a break.

That lifelong keeping is also where the first inner work is done, inside the prayers we already pray. The path is the purifying of the way we practise the law, giving each act more depth every time it is done. It begins with the first words of the prayer: at *Allahu akbar*, we have to know what we are saying. Once we do, everything inside changes as the words are said.

Knowing what we are saying needs attention, and attention cannot be held by force. We can close our eyes and keep our tongue still. Attention still moves elsewhere the moment we try to turn it towards God, and the thought about the car arrives.

Reason says this should not happen. Someone standing at the edge of a cliff would not wonder whether the car was locked, because the danger is in front of them. Standing before God ought to hold the mind in the same way. The logic is sound, but no one can bring that sense of presence to prayer simply by deciding to.

The difficulty is a circle. To say the words from the heart, we need a knowledge that comes only after the words have been practised and taken in. That practice needs the same attention that keeps slipping. The lectures call this a vicious circle, and it is the whole difficulty of the path. It is why anyone can keep the law, while not everyone can walk the path.

The circle does not mean that the faculty of attention is missing. Imam Khomeini asks, "How is it that the study of a book often absorbs man's entire attention, but prayer does not?" [@light, p. 135]. A book can hold the whole mind, so the mind can be held. What is missing is the habit of bringing that attention to prayer.

That habit is built a degree at a time, inside the law. In *Forty Hadith* Imam Khomeini names two things that bring attention to worship: "possessing unoccupied time and an unoccupied heart", and "making one's heart understand the importance of worship" [@forty_hadith, p. 375]. The path gives each prayer a little more depth than the last, and Chapter Ten takes up the remembrances that carry this attention beyond the prayer.

## To the last breath

::: {.point}
The law binds the traveller to the end of the road, where keeping it becomes thanks to God.

1. Some claim that an advanced traveller is released from the law; ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi calls this a lie.
2. The Prophet ﷺ kept every precept to the last moments of his life, so no station is beyond the law.
3. Anyone who claims the path while departing from the law is to be counted a hypocrite.
4. The light, once received, makes the keeping stricter, not looser.
5. What can change at the end is the reason for worship: it is no longer for growth.
6. The Prophet ﷺ himself gave the new reason: to be a grateful servant.
7. For a soul that has arrived, keeping God's commands is itself the thanks.
8. The last stage of the road is lived among people.
9. Therefore: there is no path outside the law, from the first step to the last.
10. If the law marks out the road, the next question is who walks it beside the traveller.
:::

The law holds at the start of the road, and it holds at the end. Some have claimed that a traveller who reaches the higher stations is spared the obligations of the law. Tabatabaʾi rejects the claim outright: "from the beginning of wayfaring to its final stage the wayfarer must abide by all the precepts of the luminous Shari'ah" [@kernel, p. 30]. The claim of release, he writes, is "a big lie and slander" [@kernel, p. 30].

His proof is the Prophet ﷺ. Though he was the noblest of all creation, he kept every precept of the law until the last moments of his life [@kernel, p. 30]. If the one who reached furthest was never released from the law, no traveller reaches a station where it stops applying.

Tabatabaʾi's test matches Kubra's. Anyone who claims to be a wayfarer while departing from the law even slightly is to be considered a hypocrite. He allows only for a lapse that comes from error, from an excuse, or from forgetfulness [@kernel, p. 30].

Imam Khomeini adds that the light, once it comes, makes the keeping stricter. A person to whom the truth has been unveiled acts "with greater observance of the manifest forms of religion" [@forty_hadith, p. 35]. He rejects the claim of release as the view of "pseudo spiritualists, who say … that after acquiring inner excellences, necessity of observing outward forms does not remain obligatory". It is, he writes, "a mistaken conception born of ignorance about the stages of worship" [@forty_hadith, p. 35].

Worship does have stages, and Tabatabaʾi finds one true sense in the talk of release, though not the sense its proponents intend. Acts of worship develop the soul, bringing its capacities "from potentiality to actuality" [@kernel, p. 30]. Worship is the means of growth for a soul still growing towards its perfection, whereas for a soul that has reached it, worship is no longer for growth [@kernel, p. 30]. What changes at the end of the road is not the law but the reason for keeping it.

The Prophet ﷺ gave that reason himself. He was once asked why he took such pains in worship when God had promised him forgiveness (Q 48:2), and he answered with a question.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the Prophet's reply, Kernel of the Kernels p. 30]" source="Hadith"}
Don't you want me to be God's grateful servant? [@kernel, p. 30]
:::

For such a soul, Tabatabaʾi concludes, worship is "purely for the sake of expression of thankfulness and gratitude to God Almighty" [@kernel, p. 30]. This is what the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah asks for at the head of this chapter: to be among those who do not fail to show God their gratitude and do not take His orders lightly. In the Prophet's worship the two are one act, because keeping God's commands is itself the thanks.

The end of the road is also lived among people, not above them. The ʿurafaʾ describe the completed journey as four journeys: from creation to God; in God, by God; from God back to creation, by God; and in creation, by God. The traveller who reaches God returns to creation in the third journey and travels the fourth among people, still keeping the law, as Chapter Sixteen describes.

There is therefore no path outside the law. The law, the path, and the truth are one religion at three depths, and each depth is reached only through the one before. Nothing inward stands in for the law, and the inner work begins inside it, with attention in prayer. At the end of the road the law is still kept, now as thanks to God.

If the law marks out the road, who walks it beside the traveller?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
For one prayer each day this week, pray at the start of its time, with nothing planned straight after it. Imam Khomeini advises that one "should set aside a certain time out of his day and night hours exclusively for the sake of worship", and that salat be carried out "in the most meritorious part of its time" [@forty_hadith, p. 375]. At *Allahu akbar*, follow Sheikh Bahmanpour's advice and "know what you are saying". When the mind turns to something else, bring it back to the words of the prayer.
:::
