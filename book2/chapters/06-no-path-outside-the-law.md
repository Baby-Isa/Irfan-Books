::: {.quote ar="وَلاَ يَغْفُلُ عَنْ شُكْرِكَ وَلاَ يَسْتَخِفُّ بِأَمْرِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
who do not fail to show You their gratitude and do not take Your orders lightly [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §126–127]
:::

These words come near the end of the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. They describe the people the prayer asks to be counted among: those who remember God always, never fail to thank Him, and never take His commands lightly. Remembering God and keeping His law are asked for in one line.

Most of us know how easily the two come apart. We stand for salah, raise our hands, and say *Allahu akbar*, and within moments the mind has turned to whether the car was locked. The prayer is still valid, since every rule has been kept, yet the remembering has slipped away.

Chapter Five found the self deeper than it appears, its final shape in our hands, and asked what keeps so inward a work from going astray. This chapter's answer is the law, in which the rules we keep and the remembering we lose are one thing at different depths.

::: {.words}
*shariʿa*: the sacred law; what the Prophet ﷺ taught us to do · *tariqa*: the path; the inward way of doing what the law asks · *haqiqa*: the truth that the path leads to · *ihsan*: the highest level of faith, worship aware of God's presence · *karamat*: wonders worked by those close to God
:::

## Shell, core and kernel

::: {.point}
The law, the path, and the truth are one religion at three depths: the law is what the Prophet ﷺ said to do, the path is doing it as he did it, and the truth is what he saw by doing it. Each depth is reached only through the one before it.
:::

Let's start with the three words the ʿurafaʾ use for the depths of the religion. The first is *shariʿa*, the law: how and when to pray and to fast, and what each act needs before it is valid. This is what most of us mean by practising the religion. Yet the law is not kept for its own sake. We pray and fast to go somewhere, towards nearness to God, and the way from the law to that nearness is called *tariqa*, a path.

The path is the inward side of the law. Suppose we prayed beside the Prophet ﷺ and copied every movement he made. A jurist watching would rule both prayers valid, because the form of an act is what a jurist can judge. What passed in the Prophet's heart, and in ours, lies beyond any ruling, and that inward way of doing the act is the tariqa. It leads to *haqiqa*, the truth: becoming like the Prophet and seeing what he saw.

A saying attributed to the Prophet puts the three in one line: "The shariʿah is my words, tariqah my actions, haqiqah my states" [@inner_secrets, p. 32]. His words tell us what to do. His actions show how it is done from within, while the outward frame remains the law. His states are what he saw by doing it.

Because each depth lies inside the one before, none of them can be skipped. Sheikh Bahmanpour gives an image for this that he credits to Najm al-Din Kubra, the master of one of the Sufi orders [VERIFY: Kubra's ship, sea and pearl, Fawaʾih al-jamal]. The law is a ship, the path is a sea, and the truth is a pearl within it. The pearl "cannot be found except by sailing through the sea, and you cannot sail without a ship". This order is why the prologue counted the shariʿa as the first step of ʿirfan, and the boundary of every step after it.

Mulla ʿAbd al-Samad Hamadani, a Shia ʿarif who had a hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt (as) ready for every point of the path, puts the same order without the image. As the lectures report him, the law, the path, and the truth are "different names for the same reality seen from different perspective[s]", and they differ only in depth. The shariʿa is the shell, the tariqa the core, and the haqiqa the kernel.[Murtada Mutahhari gives the same order: "One is outward shell, another is inward kernel, and the third is the kernel of the kernel." @light, p. 15]

Hamadani found all three depths in a single prayer. Salah is service to God, *khidma*, and this is its depth of law. In drawing near, *qurb*, it reaches the depth of the path, and as an arrival, *wusul*, it reaches the depth of the truth.[Sayyid Haydar Amuli records the saying: "Surely prayer is service, a coming closer and an arrival." @inner_secrets, p. 32] The same salah holds all three, and a prayer like the Prophet's serves God, draws near to Him, and arrives.

The ʿurafaʾ give the three depths other names as well, and two sets of them show what each depth brings. The first set names three degrees of certainty. Keeping the law in the most perfect way gives *ʿilm al-yaqin*, the knowledge of certainty: being certain that God is there without seeing Him, as a person in England can be certain that Paris lies across the Channel without having been. *ʿAyn al-yaqin*, the sight of certainty, is like seeing the city's houses from the car on arriving. *Haqq al-yaqin*, the truth of certainty, is being inside the city and witnessing what goes on there.

The second set comes from the levels of faith: *islam*, *iman*, and *ihsan*. Islam is submission, doing what the law asks. Surah al-Hujurat marks the step from islam to iman, when some Bedouins claim more than they have.

::: {.ayah ref="49:14" words="1-14"}
The Bedouins say, 'We have faith.' Say, 'You do not have faith yet; rather, say, "We have embraced Islam," for faith has not yet entered into your hearts.'
:::

The Bedouins had made the outward submission, whereas faith is the meaning of that submission entering the heart. Ihsan, the highest level, is worship aware of God's presence. Surah al-Dhariyat describes the people of ihsan, the *muhsinin*, by their nights: they slept little, and at dawn they pleaded for forgiveness (Q 51:16–18). The highest level of faith is still described through acts of worship.

Each level is built on the one below it, and none stands without it.[Sayyid Haydar Amuli puts the order as a rule: "shariʿah is possible without tariqah although tariqah is not possible without shariʿah." @inner_secrets, p. 48] Whether the names are law, path, and truth, or the three degrees of certainty, or islam, iman, and ihsan, the inner depths of the religion are reached only through its outer one.

## The inner never cancels the outer

::: {.point}
Since the inner depths are reached only through the law, nothing inward can stand in for it: a wonder worked by someone who abandons one command of God is magic, not a karama, and the light of knowing God does not descend on a heart that sets the law aside. The first inner work is done inside the law, in the attention brought to the prayer already prayed.
:::

If the truth is reached only through the law, someone who claims the truth while setting the law aside has left the ship. Kubra, as the lectures report him, draws this conclusion from his own image. Suppose a person is seen flying in the air, walking on the sea, or eating fire, and yet has abandoned "even one injunction of God". Kubra's verdict is plain: "you should know that they are liars".

"Liars" here does not mean that the feats are faked, since such things can be done. The lie is in the claim to know God. The person works these wonders for their own desires, and what they do is not a *karama*, a wonder of the friends of God, but magic. Chapter One met these wonders from the other side, where training the soul to gain them was named shirk, setting up a partner beside God.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: karamat, muʿjiza, and sihr"}
Chapter One set *karamat* and *muʿjizat*, the wonders of the saints and the miracles of the prophets, side by side. The lectures add a third word. A *muʿjiza*, a miracle, is worked only by a prophet, by God's permission. A *karama* (plural *karamat*) is a wonder that appears at the hands of someone close to God; Sheikh Bahmanpour describes it as worked through the strength of the human soul. *Sihr*, magic, produces effects that can look the same by other means, usually the help of the jinn. To an onlooker, a flight through the air looks alike whoever performs it. Kubra's test therefore looks past the feat to the person's keeping of the law, and one abandoned command of God is enough to mark the feat as magic.
:::

The law cannot be skipped because of what it does to the one who keeps it. Shaykh Muhammad Bahari, one of the Shia teachers whose chain Chapter Seven follows [@light, p. 106], set out the order. Jurisprudence, he taught, is "an introductory means" to purifying one's character, and good character is the way into knowledge of God's oneness, *tawhid*.

The chain runs in one direction only. Without the law lived in oneself there is no good character, and without good character there is no knowledge of God, which comes through purifying the self and not through study. A traveller who claims to have become an ʿarif and to need the law no longer, as if it were meant for ordinary people, has broken the first link.

Chapter Two found that purification begins with practising the shariʿa, in a circle where knowledge leads to action and action brings a deeper knowledge, and that the purified heart receives the knowledge of God as a light. Imam Khomeini states the same from the side of the law. In his words, "Without adhering to the apparent teachings of Islam it is not possible that the light of the Divine Knowledge could descend upon his heart" [@forty_hadith, p. 35].

Nor does the light, once it has come, loosen the law. A person to whom the truth has been unveiled, Imam Khomeini continues, acts "with greater observance of the manifest forms of religion". This, he writes, "falsifies the claims of pseudo spiritualists, who say that the inner perfection can only be acquired by relinquishing outer accomplishments" [@forty_hadith, p. 35].[Sayyid Haydar Amuli gives the warning from the other side: "Whoever does not protect his spiritual state and path by the shariʿah will destroy that state." @inner_secrets, p. 42]

Experiences that bypass the law are far easier to come by than the law itself. Sheikh Bahmanpour warns that anyone, of any belief, who goes forty days on less and less food will find something. The knowledge of God is so unfamiliar to us that whatever is found is taken for God.

Next to such feats, keeping the law is the harder discipline. It asks for worship every day and night and at every dawn, and for watchfulness at every moment over a tongue that might backbite and ears that might listen to what is vile. A feat can be sustained for forty days, whereas the law has to be kept for a whole life, and the true ʿarif is the one who keeps it without a break.

That lifelong keeping of the law is also where the first inner work is done, inside the prayers we already pray. The tariqa is the purifying of how we practise the shariʿa, giving it more depth each time. When we say *Allahu akbar*, in the lectures' instruction, "you have to know what you are saying". What makes this hard is attention, which is "very elusive". The eyes can be closed and the tongue held still, yet attention moves elsewhere the moment it is turned towards God.

This is the thought about the car that arrives with *Allahu akbar*. A person standing at the edge of a cliff would not wonder about the car, because the danger is in front of them. The lectures grant the logic of this, and ask whether anyone can bring that sense of presence to prayer simply by deciding to. Saying the words from the heart needs a knowledge that comes only after the words have been practised and taken in, while that practice needs the very attention that keeps slipping. The lectures call this a vicious circle, and the whole difficulty of the tariqa.

Imam Khomeini asks the question this circle raises: "How is it that the study of a book often absorbs man's entire attention, but prayer does not?" [@light, p. 135]. A book can hold the mind entirely, so the faculty of attention is not missing. In *Forty Hadith* he names the two things that bring it to worship: "possessing unoccupied time and an unoccupied heart", and "making one's heart understand the importance of worship" [@forty_hadith, p. 375].

The law also supplies the practice that the circle needs. Besides the obligatory and recommended prayers, the shariʿa gives many remembrances whose purpose is to make us think more about God and less about other things, and to spend more of our time with Him. Chapter Ten returns to these disciplines.

## To the last breath

::: {.point}
The law binds the traveller from the first step to the last. The Prophet ﷺ kept it to the end of his life, so the claim that an advanced traveller is released from it is false, and at the end of the road worship is gratitude; even the road's last stage is lived among people.
:::

The inner never cancels the outer at the start of the road, and the same holds at its end. Some have claimed that a traveller who reaches the higher stations is spared the obligations of the law. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, in the lectures recorded in *Kernel of the Kernels*, rejects the claim: "from the beginning of wayfaring to its final stage the wayfarer must abide by all the precepts of the luminous Shari'ah" [@kernel, p. 30]. The claim of release he calls "a big lie and slander" [@kernel, p. 30].

His reason is the Prophet ﷺ. The noblest of all creation kept every precept of the law until the last moments of his life [@kernel, p. 30], so no traveller reaches a station where the law no longer applies.

Worship does take on another meaning at the end of the road, and Tabatabaʾi explains how. For a soul still growing towards its perfection, the acts of worship are the means of that growth, whereas for one who has reached it, they are no longer for growth [@kernel, p. 30]. The Prophet was once asked why he took such pains in worship when God had promised him forgiveness (Q 48:2), and he replied with a question.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the Prophet's reply, Kernel of the Kernels p. 30]" source="Hadith"}
Don't you want me to be God's grateful servant? [@kernel, p. 30]
:::

For such a soul, Tabatabaʾi concludes, worship is "purely for the sake of expression of thankfulness and gratitude to God Almighty" [@kernel, p. 30]. The Munajat line at the head of this chapter asks to be among those who do not fail to show God their gratitude and do not take His orders lightly. In the Prophet's worship the two are one act, since keeping God's commands is itself the thanks.

The road's last stage, too, is lived among people. The ʿurafaʾ describe the completed journey as four journeys: from creation to God; in God, by God; from God back to creation, by God; and in creation, by God. The traveller who has reached God returns to creation in the third journey and travels the fourth in its midst, and Chapter Sixteen walks all four.

There is therefore no inner path outside the shariʿa. The law, the path, and the truth are one religion at three depths, and each depth is reached only through the one before it. Nothing inward stands in for the law: wonders worked without it are magic, and the light of knowing God does not descend on a heart that has set it aside. The law also holds to the end of the road, because the Prophet kept it to the last moments of his life, as an act of gratitude.

The law is the boundary. Who walks beside the traveller?

For one prayer each day this week, pray at the start of its time, with nothing planned straight after it. Imam Khomeini advises that one "should set aside a certain time out of his day and night hours exclusively for the sake of worship", and that salat be carried out "in the most meritorious part of its time" [@forty_hadith, p. 375]. At *Allahu akbar*, follow the advice of the lectures and "know what you are saying". When the mind turns to something else, bring it back to the words of the prayer.
:::
