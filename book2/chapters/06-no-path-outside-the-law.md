::: {.quote ar="وَلاَ يَغْفُلُ عَنْ شُكْرِكَ وَلاَ يَسْتَخِفُّ بِأَمْرِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
who do not fail to show You their gratitude and do not take Your orders lightly [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §126–127]
:::

These words come near the end of the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. The words just before them ask to be among those who always remember God. This line adds two more requests: never to fail to thank Him, and never to take His commands lightly. Remembering God and keeping His commands are asked for together.

In our own prayers the two often come apart. We stand for salah, raise our hands, and say *Allahu akbar*, and within moments the mind is asking whether the car was locked. The law judges the outward form of the prayer, and the form was correct, so the prayer is valid in law.

That gap can lead to a tempting conclusion. The journey towards God is an inward one, worked out in the self. If the remembering is what matters, and the rules can be kept without it, perhaps the rules are an outer layer to be left behind as the traveller goes deeper. Some mystical traditions have taught exactly this: train the heart, and do not trouble over the details of the law.

Most of the masters of the path in Islam, Shia and Sunni alike, taught the opposite. This chapter sets out why there is no path outside the law. The rules we keep and the remembering we lose belong to one religion, seen at different depths. The deeper levels are reached only through the law, and the law is kept to the end of the road.

::: {.words}
*shariʿa*: the sacred law; what the Prophet ﷺ taught us to do · *tariqa*: the path; the inward way of doing what the law asks · *haqiqa*: the truth that the path leads to · *karamat*: wonders worked by those close to God
:::

## Shell, core, and kernel

:::: {.point}
The path and the truth are the inside of the law, so they are reached only through it.

1. The ʿurafaʾ name the depths of the religion with three words. *(Reader told: "the three words".)*
    a. The law (*shariʿa*) tells us what to do and how to do it validly.
    b. We keep the law to draw near to God: the way to that nearness is the path (*tariqa*), and what it reaches is the truth (*haqiqa*).
2. The path is not a second set of acts: a jurist would rule our copy of the Prophet's prayer as valid as his, because he judges only the outward form.
3. The two prayers differ in what the jurist cannot see, the inward way each is done: that is the path, and what is seen through it is the truth. So both lie inside the law's form.
4. The Prophet ﷺ put the three in one line: his words are the law, his actions the path, his states the truth.
5. If the path and the truth lie inside the law, the three are one reality at three depths: shell, core and kernel (Hamadani).
6. A single prayer holds all three depths: service, drawing near, arrival.
7. The order runs one way: the law can be kept without the path, but the path has nothing to work on without the law.
8. Therefore each depth is reached only through the one before: the path and the truth only through the law. *(Kubra's ship, sea and pearl.)*
::::

Let's start with the three words the ʿurafaʾ use for the depths of the religion. The first is the law (*shariʿa*): how and when to pray and to fast, and what each act needs before it is valid. It is what the Prophet ﷺ taught us to do, and it is what most of us mean by practising the religion.

The law is not kept for its own sake. We pray and fast to draw near to God, and the way from the law to that nearness is called the path (*tariqa*). The path leads in turn to the truth (*haqiqa*), the reality that is seen once the path has been walked.

The path is not a second set of acts beside the law. Suppose we prayed beside the Prophet ﷺ and copied every movement he made. A jurist would rule both prayers valid, having checked the form of each: the standing, the bowing, and the words. The outward form of an act is all a jurist can judge, and both forms were the same.

What a jurist cannot judge is what passed in the heart. Imam Khomeini draws the same line: "there is no necessary relation between the (legal) validity of ʿibadah and its acceptability" [@forty_hadith, p. 293]. An act of worship can be valid in law and still not be accepted, because acceptance turns on what the jurist cannot see. The Prophet's prayer and ours would differ in the inward way each was performed, and that inward way is the path. The outward frame of the act is still the law. What the Prophet saw through such a prayer is the truth.

A saying attributed to the Prophet ﷺ puts the three in one line. "The shariʿah is my words, tariqah my actions, haqiqah my states" [@inner_secrets, p. 32]. His words tell us what to do. His actions show how it is done from within, and his states are what he saw by doing it.

If the path and the truth lie inside the law, the three are not separate roads. Mulla ʿAbd al-Samad Hamadani, a Shia ʿarif, taught that they are different names for one reality seen from different sides. They differ only in depth: the law is the shell, the path the core, and the truth the kernel.[Murtada Mutahhari gives the same order: "One is outward shell, another is inward kernel, and the third is the kernel of the kernel." @light, p. 15]

Hamadani found all three depths in a single salah. As service to God (*khidma*), the prayer belongs to the law. As a drawing near (*qurb*), it belongs to the path, and as an arrival (*wusul*), it belongs to the truth.[Sayyid Haydar Amuli records the saying: "Surely prayer is service, a coming closer and an arrival." @inner_secrets, p. 32] Sheikh Bahmanpour adds that a prayer like the Prophet's does all three at once: it serves God, it draws near to Him, and it arrives (*wusul*).

The order between the depths runs one way only. The law can be kept without the path. The prayer with the wandering mind was valid in law, yet it did not draw near. The path cannot be walked without the law, because the path is the inside of the law's own acts and has nothing else to work on.[Sayyid Haydar Amuli puts the order as a rule: "shariʿah is possible without tariqah although tariqah is not possible without shariʿah." @inner_secrets, p. 48]

Each depth is therefore reached only through the one before it, and none can be skipped. Sheikh Bahmanpour credits an image for this to Najm al-Din Kubra, the head of one of the Sufi orders. The law is a ship, the path is a sea, and the truth is a pearl in that sea. The pearl "cannot be found except by sailing through the sea, and you cannot sail without a ship".

## The inner never cancels the outer

:::: {.point}
No wonder or experience stands in for the law.

1. If the truth is reached only through the law, a wonder-worker who abandons one command of God is lying (Kubra).
2. The wonders may be real; the lie is the claim to know God, and the feat is magic, not *karama*. *(Word Study box: karamat, muʿjiza, sihr.)*
3. The claim fails without the law because of what the law does to the one who keeps it: the law makes good character, and good character is the way into knowing God (Bahari).
4. Each link needs the one before: God is not known in a classroom, only through a purified self, so without the law the chain never starts.
5. Knowledge of God comes to the heart as a light, and it does not descend on a heart that sets the law aside (Khomeini).
6. What comes without the law is easily mistaken for that light: forty days of hunger will show anyone something, and it is taken for God.
7. Even a real light can be mistaken for His. *(The Sheikh's story, told in a prose paragraph: the master and the light of his wudu, L06 ¶67; D47.)*
8. Even the experiences of the true path are only a preliminary: what counts is a lasting change in the self (*Kernel*).
9. Therefore the lasting change comes from keeping the law for a whole life, not from any wonder or experience: nothing stands in for the law.
::::

If the truth is reached only through the law, someone who claims the truth while setting the law aside has left the ship. Kubra draws this conclusion from his own image. Suppose a person is seen flying through the air, walking on the sea, or eating fire. In the Sheikh's rendering of his words, if they have abandoned "even one injunction of God", then "you should know that they are liars".

"Liars" does not mean that the feats are faked, because such things can be done. The lie is in the claim to be a true ʿarif, one who knows God. The person works these wonders for their own desires, and what they do is not a wonder of the friends of God (*karama*) but magic (*sihr*).

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: karamat, muʿjiza, and sihr"}
Three words name wonders that can look alike. A miracle (*muʿjiza*) is worked only by a prophet, by God's permission. A *karama* (plural *karamat*) is a wonder at the hands of someone close to God, or of someone who has found the spiritual power of the soul. Sheikh Bahmanpour describes it as worked through the strength of the human soul. Magic (*sihr*) produces effects that can look the same by other means, usually with the help of the jinn. Such a feat can look the same whoever performs it. Kubra's test therefore looks past the feat to the person's keeping of the law, and one abandoned command of God marks the feat as magic.
:::

The claim to know God fails without the law because of what the law does to the one who keeps it. Ayatullah Muhammad Bahari, an Iranian ʿarif, set out the order. Jurisprudence, he taught, is "an introductory means" to purifying one's character. Good character, in turn, is the way into knowledge of God's oneness (*tawhid*).

Each link in that order needs the one before it. Theology can be learned in a classroom, but the reality of God cannot. It is found only through purifying the self, so without good character there is no knowledge of God. Without the law lived in oneself there is no good character. Some travellers claim to be ʿarifs with no further need of the law, as if it were meant only for ordinary people. On this chain of reasoning, they have broken its first link.

Imam Khomeini reaches the same conclusion through the image of light: "Without adhering to the apparent teachings of Islam it is not possible that the light of the Divine Knowledge could descend upon his heart" [@forty_hadith, p. 35]. Knowledge of God comes to the heart as a light, and a heart that has set the law aside is not made ready to receive it.

What does come without the law is easily mistaken for that light. Sheikh Bahmanpour, recalling a remark of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's, warns that anyone of any belief will find something if they go forty days on less and less food. The knowledge of God is so unfamiliar to us that whatever is found is taken for God.

Even the light of an ablution can be taken for the light of God. The Sheikh tells of a great mystic whose chain of teachers went back to Imam al-Rida (as). One day, as he was making his ritual ablution (*wudu*), his disciples suddenly fell into prostration, glorifying God. When he asked what had happened, one of them said he had seen the light of God. The master told them it was the light of his wudu. The light of God is unknown to most of us, and so they took this one for His.

Even the experiences that come on the true path are not the goal. Tabatabaʾi's lectures on the path were recorded by his student Sayyid Muhammad Husayn Tihrani in *Kernel of the Kernels*. There the states a traveller passes through, and the lights that appear from time to time, are called "a preliminary to an enduring condition" [@kernel, p. 30]. An experience comes and goes. What counts is a lasting change in the self. Until the change is lasting, "the smallest lapse in wayfaring and spiritual jihad would bring him down again to the lower world" [@kernel, p. 31].

A forty-day exercise does not make that lasting change. In the remark the Sheikh recalls, the harder discipline is practising the law without a break. It asks for worship every day and night and at every dawn. It asks for a constant watch over the tongue, which might backbite, and the ears, which might listen to what is vile. A forty-day exercise ends, whereas the law is kept for a whole life. The lasting change comes from that keeping, not from any wonder or experience, and the real ʿarif is the one who keeps the law continuously.

## The first inner work

:::: {.point}
The inner work begins inside the law, with attention in prayer.

1. That lifelong keeping is where the inner work begins: the path deepens the way we practise the law, starting with knowing what we say at *Allahu akbar*.
2. Knowing what we say needs attention, and attention cannot be held by force.
3. Standing before God ought to hold the mind as a cliff edge would, yet no one can bring that sense of presence to prayer by deciding to.
4. The difficulty is a circle: that sense of presence needs a knowledge that only practice gives, and practice needs the attention that keeps slipping.
5. The circle does not mean the faculty is missing, since a book can hold it; what is missing is the habit of bringing it to prayer (Khomeini).
6. Therefore the inner work is done inside the law, by building that habit a prayer at a time.
::::

That lifelong keeping is also where the first inner work is done, inside the prayers we already pray. The path purifies the way we practise the law, giving each act more depth each time it is done. It begins with the first words of the prayer: at *Allahu akbar*, we have to know what we are saying. Once we do, the words begin to work inside us as we say them.

Knowing what we are saying needs attention, and attention cannot be held by force. We can close our eyes and keep our tongue still, yet the moment we turn towards God, the thought about the car arrives.

Logically this should not happen, because standing before God ought to hold the mind as standing at the edge of a cliff does. Someone at a cliff's edge would not wonder whether the car was locked, because the danger is in front of them. Yet no one can bring that sense of presence to prayer simply by deciding to.

The difficulty is a circle. To bring that sense of presence to the words, we need a knowledge that comes only after they have been practised and taken in. That practice needs the same attention that keeps slipping. This circle is the whole difficulty of the path. Anyone can perform the acts the law asks for, but the path asks for this attention as well, which is why it is harder to walk.

The circle does not mean that the faculty of attention is missing. Imam Khomeini asks, "How is it that the study of a book often absorbs man's entire attention, but prayer does not?" [@light, p. 135]. The question itself shows that the mind can be held, since a book holds it. On this reading, what is missing is the habit of bringing that attention to prayer.

That habit is built inside the law. In *Forty Hadith* he names two things that bring attention to worship: "possessing unoccupied time and an unoccupied heart", and "making one's heart understand the importance of worship" [@forty_hadith, p. 375]. He is plain about what is at stake, writing that "a worship performed without an attentive heart is devoid of value" [@forty_hadith, p. 375]. The path builds the habit a degree at a time, giving each prayer a little more depth than the last, and Chapter Ten takes up the remembrances that carry this attention beyond the prayer.

## To the last breath

:::: {.point}
The law holds to the last breath; only the reason for keeping it changes.

1. No station on the road releases the traveller from the law; whoever claims the path while leaving it, even slightly, is a hypocrite (*Kernel*).
2. The Prophet ﷺ kept every precept to the last moments of his life, so no station is beyond the law.
3. Once the truth is unveiled, the law is kept more carefully, not less (Khomeini).
4. Worship has stages: for a growing soul it is the means of growth; for a soul that has arrived it is not. What changes on arrival is the reason, not the law.
5. The Prophet ﷺ gave the new reason himself: to be God's grateful servant. *(Hadith quote.)*
6. For a soul that has arrived, worship is thanks, which is what the Munajat at the head of the chapter asks.
7. The road runs past arrival, back among people, and the law binds there too.
8. Therefore there is no path outside the law: the three depths are reached in order, nothing inward stands in for the law, the inner work starts inside it, and after arrival it is kept as thanks.

Close: "If the law marks out the road, who walks it beside the traveller?"
::::

The law holds at the start of the road, and it holds at the end. Some have claimed that a traveller who reaches the higher stations is spared the obligations of the law. Tabatabaʾi's teaching, as the *Kernel* records it, rejects the claim outright: "from the beginning of wayfaring to its final stage the wayfarer must abide by all the precepts of the luminous Shari'ah" [@kernel, p. 30]. The claim of release is "a big lie and slander" [@kernel, p. 30].

The proof is the Prophet ﷺ. Though he was the noblest of all creation, he kept every precept of the law until the last moments of his life [@kernel, p. 30]. If the one who reached furthest was never released from the law, no traveller reaches a station where it stops applying.

The *Kernel*'s test is close to Kubra's. Anyone who claims to be a wayfarer while departing from the law even slightly is to be considered a hypocrite. The only allowance is for a lapse that comes from error, from an excuse, or from forgetfulness [@kernel, p. 30].

Imam Khomeini goes further. A person to whom the truth has been unveiled, he writes, acts "with greater observance of the manifest forms of religion" [@forty_hadith, p. 35]. He rejects the claim of release, the view that once inner excellences are gained the outward forms are no longer obligatory, as "a mistaken conception born of ignorance about the stages of worship" [@forty_hadith, p. 35].

Worship does have stages, and the *Kernel* finds one true sense in the talk of release, though not the sense its proponents intend. Acts of worship develop the soul, bringing its capacities "from potentiality to actuality" [@kernel, p. 30], that is, making real what was only possible. For a soul still growing towards its perfection, worship is the means of growth. For a soul that has reached perfection, worship is no longer for growth [@kernel, p. 30]. What changes on arrival is not the law but the reason for keeping it.

The Prophet ﷺ gave that reason himself. He was once asked why he took such pains in worship when God had promised him forgiveness (Q 48:2), and he answered with a question.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the Prophet's reply, Kernel of the Kernels p. 30]" source="Hadith"}
Don't you want me to be God's grateful servant? [@kernel, p. 30]
:::

For such a soul, the *Kernel* concludes, worship is "purely for the sake of expression of thankfulness and gratitude to God Almighty" [@kernel, p. 30]. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah at the head of this chapter asks for the same gratitude: to be among those who do not fail to show God their gratitude and do not take His orders lightly.

The completed journey also runs past arrival. The ʿurafaʾ describe it as four journeys: to God, in God, back from God to creation, and among creation. In the Sheikh's phrase, the last three are made "by the haqq", that is, through God, the Real, and not by the traveller's own strength. The traveller returns to creation in the third journey and travels the fourth among people, as Chapter Sixteen describes. On the *Kernel*'s rule that the law binds the wayfarer "to its final stage" [@kernel, p. 30], it binds in these journeys too.

There is therefore no path outside the law. The law, the path, and the truth belong to one religion, seen at three depths, and each depth is reached only through the one before. Nothing inward stands in for the law, and the inner work begins inside it, with attention in prayer. After arrival the law is still kept, now as thanks to God.

If the law marks out the road, who walks it beside the traveller?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
For one prayer each day this week, keep its time free, with nothing planned straight after it. Imam Khomeini advises that one "should set aside a certain time out of his day and night hours exclusively for the sake of worship", and that the prayer be carried out "in the most meritorious part of its time" [@forty_hadith, p. 375]. At *Allahu akbar*, follow Sheikh Bahmanpour's advice and "know what you are saying". When the mind turns to something else, bring it back to the words of the prayer.
:::
