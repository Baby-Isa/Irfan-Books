Many of us recite Duʿa Tawassul, the supplication for intercession reported from the Imams (as) [@mafatih, p. 170]. In it we turn to God through the Prophet ﷺ, then Imam ʿAli and Lady Fatima (as), and then each Imam by name down to the Imam of our time [@mafatih, pp. 170–171].

A disciple in a Sufi order receives a list of names too. Sayyid Haydar Amuli records a licence in which the Prophet ﷺ instructs ʿAli (as) in the way of remembrance (*dhikr*), "who then transmitted it to Hasan al-Basri". From him the remembrance passed from master to master, through Maʿruf al-Karkhi and Junayd of Baghdad among others, until a shaykh in Konya handed it on to the writer of the licence in 731 AH [@inner_secrets, pp. 12–13].

Both lists reach back to the Prophet ﷺ, and in both the first name after his is ʿAli's. Shia ʿirfan has no institution of authority beside the Imam, and the prologue promised to show how it differs from the Sufi orders. If the two lists meet at Imam ʿAli, the difference lies further along the chain. This chapter sets out how Sufis and Shia share one road and one source, and why the road's authority belongs to one Imam.

::: {.words}
*tariqa*: a path to God; also a Sufi order · *khirqa*: the patched robe a master gives his disciple · *samaʿ*: listening to music and poetry as a spiritual practice · *ijaza*: a licence to teach or to instruct · *majdhub*: one drawn to God by divine attraction
:::

## What an order is

:::: {.point}
A Sufi order is a path that, centuries after the first seekers, became an institution of chain, method and obedience.

1. A *tariqa* is a path to God, built round an elder whose method marks out the stations, with a chain back to the Prophet ﷺ. *(Reader told: three things make a path an order.)*
2. It came late: no Sufis by name in the first century, and the great orders centuries after.
3. The Sufis say they found methods that ease the journey and claim divine sanction for them; their leaders added rites until "a vast and independent system" stood, total obedience first (Tabatabaʾi).
4. The obedience is total: the disciple lies before his shaykh like a body before the one who washes it.
5. The chain branched and was contested: disputed heirs (as after the Imams), orders within orders, and, in the Sheikh's account, honorary licences.
6. Therefore: the path became an institution; of its three supports, the chain comes first.
::::

Let's start with the word. *Tariqa* means a path, and a Sufi tariqa is a path to God. The English "brotherhood" and "order" are Western comparisons, "order" a term from the monasteries, so Sheikh Bahmanpour prefers to keep the Arabic. As Chapter Seven noted, each order is built round an elder with his own method and traces a chain of masters (*silsila*) back to the Prophet ﷺ. The method marks out the stations a traveller should pass through and makes the road easier to walk. Three things, then, make a path an order: its chain, its method, and what it asks of the disciple. <!-- src: L14 ¶3-7 -->

Orders of this kind came late in the road's history. Murtada Mutahhari writes that throughout the first century of Islam there "existed no group amongst the Muslims known as 'urafa' or sufis", and that the name *sufi* was first used in the second [@light, p. 22]. The early seekers whom Chapter Thirteen met walked the road without a tariqa. The orders arose centuries later, when people wanted instructors to tell them what to do instead of relying on their own practice. Most of the great orders date from then, among them the Qadiriyya, the Suhrawardiyya, the Naqshbandiyya, and the Chishtiyya. <!-- src: L14 ¶7, ¶25 -->

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, in his preface to *Light Within Me*, describes what the institution added to the road. Some Sunni books state that the outward form of the law does not explain how to make the spiritual journey. The Sufis, he reports, say they have discovered methods that ease the journey and claim divine sanction for them, as monasticism once had.

The Sufi leaders added whatever rites they saw fit and asked their disciples to keep them. In Tabatabaʾi's words: "Gradually a vast and independent system came into being. This system included such items as total obedience, liturgy, special robes, music and ecstasy and rapture at the time of repeating the liturgical formulas." Some orders went as far as separating the path from the law [@light, p. 6], which Chapter Six has ruled out.

The first item on that list, total obedience, asks the disciple to submit to his shaykh in everything. Sheikh Bahmanpour reports the image the orders use for it. A body given its ritual washing (*ghusl*) before burial lies powerless before the one who washes it, and he turns it and washes it however he chooses. A disciple should lie before his shaykh in the same way, "just like a dead person, before someone who performs ghusl". Obedience of that kind puts a great deal in one man's hands, and the last section of this chapter asks who may hold it. <!-- src: L14 ¶20-21 -->

The chain, for its part, branched and was contested. A shaykh usually appoints his heir, yet "there are lots of conflicts in every silsilah or tariqa about who should be the heir to the previous sheikh", much as rival claimants arose after each of the Imams. A forceful disciple could found "a path within another path". One order allowed listening to music and poetry (*samaʿ*) while another forbade it, and a Muslim might belong to both. The Sheikh knows of people who hold licences to instruct (*ijaza*) from almost every order, given, he says, as honorary compliments, and then "the whole concept of tariqa goes away". <!-- src: L14 ¶10-19 -->

In the orders, then, the path became an institution. Of its three supports, the chain comes first.

## One source

:::: {.point}
Almost every order runs its chain back to Imam ʿAli (as), so Sufi and Shia seekers draw on one source.

1. The chains lead to Imam ʿAli: Tabatabaʾi says ʿirfan derives from him, and all the orders culminate in him.
2. The orders' own records show how far this holds, and where it stops. *(Reader told.)*
    a. Suhrawardi claimed descent from Abu Bakr, yet his robe runs through Maʿruf, said to have become a Muslim at Imam al-Rida's hands, to the Imam.
    b. The Naqshbandiyya is the exception.
3. The chain itself carries the Shia idea of an heir to the Prophet's inner knowledge (the Sheikh; Ch 7). *(Who's Who box: al-Ghazali and Fayd Kashani.)*
4. Therefore: one source.
::::

Almost without exception, the chains lead to Imam ʿAli (as). ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures put the matter at its root: "The reality of 'irfan derives from Amir al-Mu'minin Ali Ibn abi Talib ('a)." More than a hundred orders handed this reality down from master to pupil, he goes on. Their main branches number no more than twenty-five, "and all of these orders culminate in Hadrat Ali Ibn Abi Talib ('a)" [@kernel, p. 87]. Many of the chains pass through his companions, such as Salman, Uways, and Kumayl [@light, p. 5].

The rule holds even where an order's head traced his own descent to Abu Bakr. Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, author of the Sufi manual *ʿAwarif al-maʿarif*, "claimed descent from Abu Bakr" [@light, p. 34]. Yet Sayyid Haydar Amuli preserves the licence Suhrawardi gave his disciples, and it traces his patched robe (*khirqa*) through Maʿruf al-Karkhi to Imam al-Rida (as) and on to the Prophet ﷺ. Maʿruf is said to have become a Muslim at the hands of Imam al-Rida and to have learned much from him [@light, p. 25]. As the introduction to *Inner Secrets of the Path* sums it up, Suhrawardi's chain reaches the Commander of the Faithful "in three different paths of transmission" [@inner_secrets, p. 13]. <!-- src: L14 ¶36 -->

The exception, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's count, is the Naqshbandiyya. Its founder, Bahaʾ al-Din Naqshband, is said to descend from Imam al-Sadiq (as), yet the order takes its chain through Abu Bakr to the Prophet. <!-- src: L14 ¶37, ¶63, ¶79 -->

The chain itself carries what the Sheikh calls "mainly a Shia idea": that the Prophet's inner knowledge passes to an heir. As Chapter Seven noted, the orders keep that idea without holding that the knowledge passed to the Imams. <!-- src: L14 ¶8-9 -->

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: al-Ghazali and Fayd Kashani"}
Abu Hamid al-Ghazali headed the Nizamiyya Academy in Baghdad, the highest post open to a scholar of his age. Feeling that neither his knowledge nor his position could satisfy his soul, he withdrew from public life and spent ten years in Palestine. Afterwards he wrote his famous *Ihyaʾ ʿulum al-din*, "Reviving the Sciences of Religion" [@light, p. 31]. Sheikh Bahmanpour counts it a deeply influential book by a sincere and learned man, who leaned too often on anecdotes and hadith that cannot be relied on. Fayd Kashani, a pupil of Mulla Sadra who belonged to no Sufi order [@light, p. 40], praised al-Ghazali as a great man and set about refining his book. He removed the anecdotes and hadith he did not trust and added hadith from the Imams (as) that al-Ghazali had not brought in, and the result was *al-Mahajja al-bayda* ("the clear path"). <!-- src: L14 ¶40-45 -->
:::

Sufi and Shia seekers therefore draw on one source, Imam ʿAli (as). With the source shared, the next question is whether the path is shared too, or whether Shia walk it by a method of their own.

## One path, many channels

:::: {.point}
The path is shared, but no single method fits every soul, so a traveller needs a guide who has walked the road more than an order's technique.

1. Shia travellers share the orders' path: the same remembrance and the same books.
2. What sets one order apart is its technique, and two orders show how far apart they run. *(Reader told.)*
    a. The Suhrawardiyya: *la ilaha illa Allah*, long, in a dark room, in a set posture and rhythm.
    b. The Naqshbandiyya: silent remembrance, company over retreat, fidelity to the law.
3. A person can be an ʿarif either way, so there is no single method.
4. Each soul is its own channel to God, no two alike, and the grace that comes through takes each soul's shape.
5. The ways are as many as the souls, but within Islam.
6. Within Islam, only what the law allows: Akhund Hamadani's line did not take the Naqshbandi stone exercise; the objection is to the exercise, not to the order's loyalty to the law.
7. So a traveller needs someone who has walked the road. *(Story: Hasanzadeh and Tabatabaʾi.)*
8. Therefore: a shared path, no single method, and a guide who has walked it.
::::

Shia travellers share the orders' path, their remembrance, and their books. The Suhrawardiyya are best known for repeating *la ilaha illa Allah*, and the practice is popular among Shia mystics too. One reason they give is that others cannot tell when it is being said. It is a hidden remembrance (*dhikr khafi*), whereas anyone nearby knows when someone is saying *subhan Allah*. <!-- src: L14 ¶57-58 -->

The books are shared as well. Ayatullah Khomeini studied the *Manazil al-saʾirin* of "the Hanbali Sufi, Khwaja 'Abdullah Ansari" with his teacher Shahabadi. The ethics class he later taught in Qum, the introduction to his *Forty Hadith* records, "consisted in the first place of a careful reading of Ansari's Manazil al-Sa'irin" [@forty_hadith, p. 8].

If the path is shared, what sets one order apart from another is its technique. Sheikh Bahmanpour sees the orders above all as the techniques they teach. Two of them show how far apart those techniques can run. In the Suhrawardiyya, the disciple says *la ilaha illa Allah* for long stretches in a dark room. He sits in a set posture and keeps a set rhythm until the remembrance passes from the tongue to the heart and becomes a lasting habit of the soul. The order also keeps the law strictly and stresses the daily prayers and the fast of Ramadan. <!-- src: L14 ¶58-60 -->

The Naqshbandiyya run the other way. They keep the remembrance silent instead of repeating a formula aloud. They prefer keeping pious company (*suhba*) to retreating into seclusion (*khalwa*), and they ask for fidelity to the law in social, political, and devotional life. Their members are known for living among people rather than apart from them. <!-- src: L14 ¶64, ¶76-77 -->

A person can become an ʿarif while living among people or while keeping apart, and while saying the remembrance aloud or in the heart. "If all these methods work," Sheikh Bahmanpour concludes, "it means that there is no one single method for practicing irfan." For this reason Shia ʿirfan does not prescribe whether the remembrance is said aloud or in the heart. <!-- src: L14 ¶65 -->

Such different methods all reach God because, as the Shia ʿurafaʾ teach, each soul is its own channel to Him, and no two channels are alike. People differ as their faces differ, and in the Sheikh's words "we are not photocopies of each other". At a certain station some Sufis heard a sound in their hearts like a dropped coin ringing on, and others grew anxious when it never came to them. It need not come, because no traveller's experiences repeat another's. <!-- src: L14 ¶66-67 -->

What comes through each channel is God's grace, and once the channel is clear the grace takes the traveller's own shape. In the Sheikh's words, "Grace is one but it has come into different types of containers". In each container it finds its own shape and colour, so that in paradise, God willing, no two people's grace will look alike. <!-- src: L14 ¶67-69 -->

If each soul receives grace in its own shape, there are as many paths to God as there are souls. Sayyid Haydar Amuli reports a saying of the Prophet ﷺ to that effect: the paths to Allah are "as many as the souls of creation" [@inner_secrets, p. 195]. Some take this to mean that any religion will do, and Sheikh Bahmanpour calls that a wrong reading. The ways are many within Islam. The salah and the Qur'an are the same for everyone, and each person finds his own way to God through them, as two people eating the same fruit each taste it differently. <!-- src: L14 ¶70 -->

Within Islam, too, the law sets a boundary on method. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures summarise a treatise whose method is that of "the aware ones who have attained to the ultimate goal". It has the traveller control stray thoughts by fixing his gaze on a stone or a piece of wood for forty days if he can. That method, Tabatabaʾi notes, "is derived from the Naqshbandi orders" [@kernel, p. 86].

Akhund Hamadani's line did not take it up. He and his disciples did not consider thought control practical without remembrance, and as Chapter Seven told, they turned the traveller's attention to the self [@kernel, pp. 86–87]. Thought control, the lectures add, belongs inside an act of worship, "for it is not permissible in the sacred Shari'ah to concentrate on a stone or piece of wood" [@kernel, p. 87]. The objection concerns this one exercise rather than the Naqshbandiyya's fidelity to the law.

Since no method fits every soul, each traveller needs someone who has walked the road to read his own, as Chapter Seven found. Sheikh Bahmanpour tells of one such meeting from the writings of Ayatullah Hasanzadeh Amuli, whom Chapter Fourteen met. Hasanzadeh writes that on the road he met many experiences he did not understand. He went to ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi and told him, "this is how I feel", and Tabatabaʾi simply explained what it meant and what he should do next. He needed a man who had passed that way rather than a technique or a licence, and the one he went to belonged to no order. <!-- src: L14 ¶23-24 -->

Shia travellers therefore share the orders' path, its remembrance, and its books, and keep no single method, so what a traveller needs inside the law is a guide rather than a technique. How far such a guide's authority reaches is the last question.

## Authority belongs to the Imam

:::: {.point}
Shia masters followed shaykhs and gave them real standing, but no chain of authority beside the Imam.

1. Obedience is where the roads part, and only in part: a teacher, yes; a chain of authority beside the Imams, no. The Prophet's inner knowledge passed by inheritance to the Imams, each appointed by the one before (L14 ¶6, ¶8–10; Ch 7).
2. From the 10th/16th century the Shia masters stood outside the orders, learned in the law and loyal to it (Mutahhari).
3. Yet they used the orders' forms and gave a shaykh real standing; three scholars, side by side. *(Reader told; D20.)*
    a. Sayyid Haydar Amuli: a contract with a shaykh and the Sufi robe; the soul at rest accepts "the jurisdiction, authority, instruction and guidance of the Prophet, the Imam or the shaykh".
    b. Tabatabaʾi assumes a pledge to an aware shaykh, kept to the end; Ayatullah Khomeini was Shahabadi's murid.
4. Sayyid Haydar, a majdhub, credits his arrival to God rather than to the forms.
5. The Imam accompanies the traveller at every rank, the most important condition of the road (Tabatabaʾi).
6. The general teacher is left behind and the Imam never, so a shaykh stands under the Imam (Ch 7; `kernel` pp. 80–81); the names of Duʿa Tawassul are that company (the book's join).
7. Therefore (section, chapter and Part Four): one road, one source, a teacher under the Imam, and the authority with the Imams.

Close: a hand-off clause, the Imam's company after arrival (`kernel` p. 81), into Chapter Sixteen. *(Try this box.)*
::::

The third feature, obedience, is where the Shia road parts from the orders, though only in part. Shia masters followed shaykhs and gave them real standing, but kept no chain of authority beside the Imams. Chapter Seven gave the reason. The inner knowledge of the Prophet ﷺ passed by inheritance to the Imams (as), so a guide stands under them and never in their place, and for Sheikh Bahmanpour the Imamate leaves no room for an order's chain. Rival claimants arose after the Imams too, as the first section noted, but Shia hold that each Imam appointed the one after him. <!-- src: L14 ¶6, ¶8-10 -->

In practice, the Shia masters of the road came to stand outside the orders. Murtada Mutahhari dates the change to around the beginning of the sixteenth century, when the masters of the orders "were no longer possessed of the academic prominence of their forerunners" [@light, p. 40]. Mulla Sadra and his pupil Fayd Kashani belonged to no order and surpassed them in the theory of ʿirfan [@light, p. 40]. Alongside them appeared travellers of high spiritual standing outside any formal order. These ʿurafaʾ were also "learned in law and jurisprudence", and among their marks Mutahhari names "a perfect loyalty to the shari'ah" [@light, p. 41]. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's teachers were among them [@light, p. 105].

Outside the orders, Shia masters still used some of their forms, and three scholars show how far. Sayyid Haydar Amuli made a contract with the Sufi shaykh Nur al-Din Tihrani and spent nearly a month in his company, "until he gave me the real patched garment of the Sufis to wear" [@inner_secrets, p. 8]. He reports that the shaykhs agree a traveller should not set out "on his own without a shaykh of perfection". He reads the verse's call to the soul at rest to enter among God's servants (Q 89:27–30) as meaning that travellers should "accept the jurisdiction, authority, instruction and guidance of the Prophet, the Imam or the shaykh without any further argument" [@inner_secrets, p. 192].

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures take a pledge of this kind for granted. Their detailed account of the road begins once the traveller has made an oath of allegiance (*bayʿa*) to an aware shaykh who is a friend of God, has passed through annihilation, and has reached remaining in God [@kernel, p. 60]. Among the traveller's duties they count loyalty, which includes keeping to the end "the promises and pledges one has given to one's aware shaykh" [@kernel, p. 64].

Ayatullah Khomeini's bond with his teacher Shahabadi, as Chapter Four told, was "that of a murid with his murshid" [@forty_hadith, p. 8]. Each of the three gives a shaykh real standing, and in Sayyid Haydar's words that standing includes authority.

What Sayyid Haydar reached at the end, he credits to God rather than to those forms. After setting out the chains of the robe he held, he writes: "My arriving at the Truth and my unveilings were not dependent on the above." He counts himself a *majdhub*, one drawn by divine attraction, and says he reached the goal "through the care and grace of God, not through any action on my part nor through any knowledge I possessed" [@inner_secrets, p. 14]. He ranks the robe with his own efforts and his learning.

If what the traveller receives comes from God, the question is through whom it comes and where the road's authority lies. Chapter Seven found that the traveller never leaves the company of the Prophet and the Imams even after he arrives. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures describe that company on the road. The traveller "travels through the planes of the Imam's luminosity and whatever degree he may ascend to and whatever rank he may attain, the Imam ('a) too possess that rank and accompanies the wayfarer at that degree and rank". This company of the Imam, in his words, "is the most important" of all the conditions of the road [@kernel, p. 81].

The general teacher of Chapter Seven, a learned guide anyone may consult, is needed only at the beginning of the road, and his company can be left once the visions come. The company of the special teachers, "the Apostle of Allah and his true successors", can never be left [@kernel, p. 80]. A shaykh may therefore be trusted and followed, as Sayyid Haydar, Tabatabaʾi, and Ayatullah Khomeini followed theirs, but he stands under the Imam and never in his place. As this book reads it, the names we call on in Duʿa Tawassul are those of the special teachers.

Shia travellers therefore share the orders' source and their path, and like the orders' disciples they may follow a shaykh who has walked it. The authority the orders give to a chain of masters belongs, in Shia teaching, to the Imams (as), whose line runs from the Prophet ﷺ to the Imam of our time. The road's roots lie in the teachings of Islam and the lives of the Prophet and the Imams of his Household [@light, pp. 4–5, 22]. The Imam's company, Tabatabaʾi adds, is needed even after the traveller arrives [@kernel, p. 81], and so it stays with those who have reached the end of the road.

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
For one week, notice which of the acts of worship you already keep (the salah, the Qur'an, or a duʿa) is the one in which your heart is most present. Then give that act a fixed time each day. Sheikh Bahmanpour's advice, for a road on which no two travellers are alike, is simple: "Everyone should find his or her own suitable type of practice and worship." If no single act stands out, say *la ilaha illa Allah* to yourself through the day, where no one else can tell, as many Shia mystics prefer. <!-- src: L14 ¶57-58, ¶71 -->
:::
