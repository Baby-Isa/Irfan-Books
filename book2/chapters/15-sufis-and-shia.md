Many of us recite Duʿa Tawassul, the supplication for intercession, which has come down from the Imams (as) [@mafatih, p. 170]. In it we turn to God through the Prophet ﷺ, then Imam ʿAli and Lady Fatima (as), and then each Imam by name down to the Imam of our time [@mafatih, pp. 170–171].

A disciple in a Sufi order receives a list of names too. Sayyid Haydar Amuli records a licence in which the Prophet instructs ʿAli (as) in the way of remembrance (*dhikr*), "who then transmitted it to Hasan al-Basri". From him the remembrance passes from master to master, through Maʿruf al-Karkhi and Junayd of Baghdad among others, until a shaykh in Konya handed it on to the writer of the licence in 731 AH [@inner_secrets, pp. 12–13].

Both lists reach back to the Prophet, and in both the first name after his is ʿAli's. The prologue promised to show how Shia ʿirfan, which has no institution of authority beside the Imam, differs from the Sufi orders. If the two lists meet at Imam ʿAli, the difference lies further along the chain. This chapter sets out how Sufis and Shia share one road and one source, and why the road's authority belongs to one Imam.

::: {.words}
*tariqa*: a path to God; also a Sufi order · *khirqa*: the patched robe a master gives his disciple · *samaʿ*: listening to music and poetry as a spiritual practice · *ijaza*: a licence to teach or to instruct · *qutb*: the pole, the perfect man the Sufis hold must always be in the world
:::

## What an order is

:::: {.point}
A Sufi order is a path that, centuries after the first seekers, became an institution of chain, method and obedience.

1. A *tariqa* is a path to God, built round an elder whose method marks out the stations, with a chain back to the Prophet ﷺ. *(Reader told: three things make a path an order.)*
2. It came late: no Sufis by name in the first century, and the great orders centuries after.
3. The institution added rites until "a vast and independent system" stood, total obedience first (Tabatabaʾi).
4. The obedience is total: the disciple lies before his shaykh like a body before the one who washes it.
5. The chain branched and was contested: disputed heirs, orders within orders, honorary licences.
6. Therefore: an order is chain, method and obedience; taken in turn, the chain comes first.
::::

Let's start with the word. *Tariqa* means a path, and a Sufi tariqa is a path to God. The English words "brotherhood" and "order" come from the monasteries of the West, so Sheikh Bahmanpour prefers to keep the Arabic. As Chapter Seven noted, each order is built round an elder with his own method and traces a chain of masters (*silsila*) back to the Prophet ﷺ. The method marks out the stations a traveller should pass through, from the first to the last, and makes the road easier to walk. Three things, then, make a path an order: its chain, its method, and what it asks of the disciple. <!-- src: L14 ¶3-7 -->

Orders of this kind came late in the road's history. Murtada Mutahhari writes that throughout the first century of Islam there "existed no group amongst the Muslims known as 'urafa' or sufis", and that the name *sufi* was first used in the second [@light, p. 22]. The early seekers whom Chapter Thirteen met walked the road without a tariqa. The orders arose centuries later, when people felt that they needed instructors to tell them what to do rather than relying on their own practice. Most of the great orders date from then, among them the Qadiriyya, the Suhrawardiyya, the Naqshbandiyya, and the Chishtiyya. <!-- src: L14 ¶7, ¶25 -->

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, in his preface to *Light Within Me*, describes what the institution added to the road. Some Sunni books state that the outward form of the law does not explain how to make the spiritual journey. On this basis, the Sufi leaders added whatever rites they saw fit and asked their disciples to keep them. In Tabatabaʾi's words: "Gradually a vast and independent system came into being. This system included such items as total obedience, liturgy, special robes, music and ecstasy and rapture at the time of repeating the liturgical formulas." Some orders went as far as separating the path from the law [@light, p. 6], which Chapter Six has ruled out.

The first item on that list, total obedience, asks the disciple to submit to his shaykh in everything. The orders have an image for it, which Sheikh Bahmanpour reports. A body given its ritual washing (*ghusl*) before burial lies powerless before the one who washes it, and he turns it and washes it however he chooses. A disciple should lie before his shaykh in the same way, "just like a dead person, before someone who performs ghusl". Obedience of that kind puts a great deal in one man's hands, and the last section of this chapter asks to whom it belongs. <!-- src: L14 ¶20-21 -->

The chain, for its part, branched and was contested. A shaykh usually appoints his heir, yet "there are lots of conflicts in every silsilah or tariqa about who should be the heir to the previous sheikh", as there were rival claimants after each of the Imams. A forceful disciple could found "a path within another path". One order allowed listening to music and poetry (*samaʿ*) while another forbade it, and a Muslim might belong to both. Some heads gave licences to instruct (*ijaza*) as honorary compliments, until one man held licences from almost every order and "the whole concept of tariqa goes away". <!-- src: L14 ¶10-19 -->

An order is therefore an institution built on a path and held together by its chain, its method, and the obedience it asks. The three, taken in that order, show what a Shia traveller shares with the orders and what he does not. The chain comes first, and with it the question of where it leads.

## One source

:::: {.point}
Almost every order runs its chain back to Imam ʿAli (as), so Sufi and Shia seekers draw on one source.

1. The chains lead to Imam ʿAli: Tabatabaʾi says ʿirfan derives from him, and all the orders culminate in him.
2. The orders' own records show how far this holds, and where it stops. *(Reader told.)*
    a. Suhrawardi claimed descent from Abu Bakr, yet his robe runs through Maʿruf to Imam al-Rida.
    b. Maʿruf's golden chain, and Junayd soon after the Imams, carry most of the chains.
    c. The Naqshbandiyya is the exception.
3. The chain and the pole carry the Shia idea of the Imam (Tabatabaʾi). *(Who's Who box: al-Ghazali and Fayd Kashani.)*
4. Therefore: one source, and the orders' own ideas point to the Imams.
::::

Almost without exception, the chains lead to Imam ʿAli (as). ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures put the matter at its root: "The reality of 'irfan derives from Amir al-Mu'minin Ali Ibn abi Talib ('a)." More than a hundred orders handed this reality down from master to pupil, he goes on. Their main branches number no more than twenty-five, "and all of these orders culminate in Hadrat Ali Ibn Abi Talib ('a)" [@kernel, p. 87]. Many of the chains pass through his companions, such as Salman, Uways, and Kumayl [@light, p. 5].

The rule holds even where an order's founder stood far from Shiʿism, and the records the orders kept of their own chains show how far it reaches. Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, author of the Sufi manual *ʿAwarif al-maʿarif*, "claimed descent from Abu Bakr" [@light, p. 34]. Yet the licence he gave his disciples, which Amuli preserves, traces his patched robe (*khirqa*) through Maʿruf al-Karkhi to Imam al-Rida (as) and on to the Prophet ﷺ. Amuli notes that Suhrawardi's chain reaches the Commander of the Faithful "in three different paths of transmission" [@inner_secrets, p. 13]. <!-- src: L14 ¶36 -->

Maʿruf al-Karkhi is the hinge of many such chains. He is said to have become a Muslim "at the hands of al-'Imam al-Rida (A), learning much from him". The ʿurafaʾ trace the lines of many orders through him to Imam al-Rida and the Imams before him, and so they call this line the golden chain (*silsilat al-dhahab*) [@light, p. 25]. Most chains of the robe also pass through Junayd of Baghdad, who follows two places after Maʿruf in the licence quoted at the start of this chapter. Junayd, as Amuli puts it, "lived soon after the time of the infallible Imams" [@inner_secrets, p. 13].

The exception, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's count, is the Naqshbandiyya. The descent of its founder, Bahaʾ al-Din Naqshband, is traced to Imam al-Sadiq (as), yet the order takes its chain through Abu Bakr to the Prophet. <!-- src: L14 ¶37, ¶63, ¶79 -->

That even the chains of such orders run back to Imam ʿAli shows that the chain itself carries a Shia idea. As Chapter Seven noted, the orders keep the idea of an heir to the Prophet's inner knowledge without holding that it passed to the Imams. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi goes further. The Sufis hold that the world must always have a perfect man as its pole (*qutb*), and what they say of him corresponds to the Shia doctrine of the Imam [@light, pp. 5–6]. The great Sufis, he concludes, "may be regarded as the proponents of the Shi'ah doctrine, though apparently they followed the Sunni school", and the pole they describe "does not actually exist anywhere outside the Shi'ite world" [@light, p. 6]. <!-- src: L14 ¶8-9 -->

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: al-Ghazali and Fayd Kashani"}
Abu Hamid al-Ghazali headed the Nizamiyya Academy in Baghdad, the highest post open to a scholar of his age. Feeling that neither his knowledge nor his position could satisfy his soul, he withdrew from public life and spent ten years in Palestine. Afterwards he wrote his famous *Ihyaʾ ʿulum al-din*, "Reviving the Sciences of Religion" [@light, p. 31]. Sheikh Bahmanpour counts it a deeply influential book by a sincere and learned man, who leaned too often on anecdotes and hadith that cannot be relied on. Fayd Kashani, a pupil of Mulla Sadra who belonged to no Sufi order [@light, p. 40], praised al-Ghazali as a great man and set about refining his book. He kept its path, removed the anecdotes and hadith he did not trust, and added hadith from the Imams (as) that al-Ghazali had not brought in. The result, *al-Mahajja al-bayda* ("the clear path"), took a Sufi manual back to the source its chains name. <!-- src: L14 ¶40-45 -->
:::

The orders and the Shia therefore draw on one source, Imam ʿAli (as), and the orders' own ideas of a chain and a pole point to the Imams. With the source shared, the next question is whether the path is shared too, or whether Shia walk it by a method of their own.

## One path, many channels

:::: {.point}
The path is shared, but no single method fits every soul, so a traveller needs a guide who has walked the road more than an order's technique.

1. Shia travellers share the orders' path: the same remembrance and the same books.
2. What sets one order apart is its technique, and two orders show how far apart they run. *(Reader told.)*
    a. The Suhrawardiyya: *la ilaha illa Allah*, long, in a dark room, in a set posture and rhythm.
    b. The Naqshbandiyya: silent remembrance, company over retreat, fidelity to the law.
3. Both produced ʿurafaʾ, so there is no single method.
4. Each soul is its own channel to God, and no two are alike; three things about the channel explain why. *(Reader told.)*
    a. Grace flows through it without pause; in the Sheikh's reading of Q 11:118–119, that is why we were created.
    b. Our deeds block it, and the work of ʿirfan is clearing it.
    c. Once it flows, the grace takes each soul's shape.
5. The ways are as many as the souls, but within Islam.
6. Within Islam, only what the law allows: Akhund Hamadani's line did not take the Naqshbandi stone method.
7. So a traveller needs someone who has walked the road. *(Story: Hasanzadeh and Tabatabaʾi.)*
8. Therefore: a shared path, no single method, and a guide who has walked it.
::::

Shia travellers share the orders' path, their remembrance, and their books. The Suhrawardiyya are best known for repeating *la ilaha illa Allah*, and the practice is just as popular among Shia mystics. One reason they give is that others cannot tell when it is being said. It is a hidden remembrance (*dhikr khafi*), whereas anyone nearby knows when someone is saying *subhan Allah*. The books are shared as well. Ayatullah Khomeini studied the *Manazil al-saʾirin* of "the Hanbali Sufi, Khwaja 'Abdullah Ansari" with his teacher Shahabadi. The ethics class he later taught in Qum, the introduction to his *Forty Hadith* records, "consisted in the first place of a careful reading of Ansari's Manazil al-Sa'irin" [@forty_hadith, p. 8]. <!-- src: L14 ¶57-58 -->

What sets one order apart from another is its technique, and for Sheikh Bahmanpour the orders are above all the techniques they teach. Two of them show how far apart those techniques can run. In the Suhrawardiyya, the disciple says *la ilaha illa Allah* for long stretches in a dark room. He sits in a set posture and keeps a set rhythm until the remembrance passes from the tongue to the heart and becomes a lasting habit of the soul. The order also keeps the law strictly and stresses the daily prayers and the fast of Ramadan. <!-- src: L14 ¶58-60 -->

The Naqshbandiyya run the other way. They keep the remembrance silent instead of repeating a formula aloud. They prefer keeping pious company (*suhba*) to retreating into seclusion (*khalwa*), and they ask for fidelity to the law in social, political, and devotional life. Their members are known for living among people rather than apart from them. <!-- src: L14 ¶64, ¶76-77 -->

Both techniques have worked, since each order has produced ʿurafaʾ. Travellers who lived among people have become ʿurafaʾ, and so have travellers who kept apart. The same is true of those who say the remembrance aloud and those who say it in the heart. "If all these methods work," Sheikh Bahmanpour concludes, "it means that there is no one single method for practicing irfan." For this reason Shia ʿirfan does not prescribe whether the remembrance is said aloud or silently, or in one manner rather than another. <!-- src: L14 ¶65 -->

Such different methods all reach God because, as the Shia ʿurafaʾ teach, each soul is its own channel to Him, and no two channels are alike. People differ as their faces differ, and travellers on this road find, in the Sheikh's words, that "we are not photocopies of each other". Passing a certain station, some Sufis heard a sound in their hearts like a dropped coin ringing on and on, and others grew anxious that it never came. It need not come, because no traveller's experiences repeat another's. Three things about the channel explain this: what flows through it, what blocks it, and the shape the flow takes. <!-- src: L14 ¶66-67 -->

What flows through the channel is God's grace, and it never stops. In the Sheikh's reading, this flow is the purpose of our creation, and he finds it in Surah Hud. Had God wished, the surah says, He would have made mankind one community, "but they continue to differ", and it then names the exception:

::: {.ayah ref="11:119" words="1-6"}
except those on whom your Lord has mercy—and that is why He created them
:::

In this reading, mercy is the reason God created us. The channel of each soul is there to receive it as a lasting flow of grace, knowledge, and light. <!-- src: L14 ¶67-68 -->

If the grace never stops, what keeps it from us lies on our side. Our deeds block the channel, as dirt washed into a river blocks the passage of its water. The work of ʿirfan is mostly the clearing of that blockage. The grace never pauses on God's side, and the clearing lets it come through. The fault, as with the signs in Chapter One, lies in our receiver rather than in what is sent. <!-- src: L14 ¶68-69 -->

Once the channel is clear, grace takes on the traveller's own shape. In the lectures' words, "Grace is one but it has come into different types of containers". In each container it finds its own shape and colour, so that in paradise no two people's grace will look alike. <!-- src: L14 ¶69 -->

If each soul receives grace in its own shape, there are as many paths to God as there are souls. Sayyid Haydar Amuli reports a saying of the Prophet ﷺ to that effect: the paths to Allah are "as many as the souls of creation" [@inner_secrets, p. 195]. Some take this to mean that any religion will do, and Sheikh Bahmanpour calls that a wrong reading. The ways are many within Islam. The salah and the Qur'an are the same for everyone, and each person finds his own way to God through them, as two people eating the same fruit each taste it differently. <!-- src: L14 ¶70 -->

Within Islam, too, the law sets a boundary on method. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures summarise a treatise on the road that calls its method the way of "the aware ones who have attained to the ultimate goal". The treatise has the traveller control stray thoughts by fixing his gaze on a stone or a piece of wood for forty days if he can. That method, Tabatabaʾi notes, "is derived from the Naqshbandi orders" [@kernel, p. 86]. Akhund Hamadani's line did not take it, because "it is not permissible in the sacred Shari'ah to concentrate on a stone or piece of wood". His line turned the traveller's attention to the self within an act of worship instead [@kernel, p. 87].

Since no method fits every soul, each traveller needs help in reading his own from someone who has walked the road, as Chapter Seven found. Sheikh Bahmanpour tells of one such meeting from the writings of Ayatullah Hasanzadeh Amuli, whom Chapter Fourteen met. On the road, Hasanzadeh writes, he came across many experiences he did not understand. He went to ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi and told him, "this is how I feel", and Tabatabaʾi simply explained what it meant and what he should do next. What Hasanzadeh needed was a man who had passed that way rather than a technique or a licence, and the man he went to belonged to no order. <!-- src: L14 ¶23-24 -->

Shia travellers therefore share the orders' path, its remembrance, and its books, but hold to no single method. What a traveller needs inside the law is a guide who has walked the road, and the next question is what authority such a guide holds.

## Authority belongs to the Imam

:::: {.point}
Shia masters used teacher, robe and pledge, but gave none of them the authority that belongs to the Imam.

1. The obedience is what Shia do not share: the Prophet's inner knowledge passed to the Imams, whose line was clear where the orders' chains were contested.
2. From the 10th/16th century the Shia masters stood outside the orders, learned in the law and loyal to it (Mutahhari).
3. Yet they used some of the orders' forms, and three scholars, side by side, show how far. *(Reader told; D20.)*
    a. Amuli: a contract with a shaykh and the Sufi robe; the soul at rest accepts "the Prophet, the Imam or the shaykh".
    b. Tabatabaʾi assumes a pledge to an aware shaykh; Ayatullah Khomeini was Shahabadi's disciple.
4. Amuli: his arrival came from God, not from the forms.
5. The Imam accompanies the traveller at every rank, the most important condition of the road (Tabatabaʾi).
6. So the forms sit under the Imam, and the names of Duʿa Tawassul are the chain a Shia traveller holds to.
7. Therefore (section, chapter and Part Four): one road, one source, and the authority with the Imams.

Close: "If the road's authority rests with the Imams, what does its end look like in those who reached it?" *(Try this box.)*
::::

The third feature, the obedience given to a chain of masters, is what Shia travellers do not share with the orders. Chapter Seven gave the reason. The inner knowledge of the Prophet ﷺ passed by inheritance to the Imams (as), so there is no room beside them for a chain of authority, and a guide stands under them. As the first section showed, the orders' chains branched, were contested at each succession, and were loosened by honorary licences. The line of the Imams, by contrast, was clear from the Prophet's own time. <!-- src: L14 ¶8-11 -->

In practice, the Shia masters of the road came to stand outside the orders. Murtada Mutahhari dates the change to around the beginning of the sixteenth century, when the masters of the orders "were no longer possessed of the academic prominence of their forerunners" [@light, p. 40]. Scholars outside the orders, such as Mulla Sadra and his pupil Fayd Kashani, surpassed them in the theory of ʿirfan [@light, p. 40]. Alongside them appeared travellers of high spiritual standing who belonged to no formal order. These ʿurafaʾ were also "learned in law and jurisprudence", and among their marks Mutahhari names "a perfect loyalty to the shari'ah" [@light, p. 41]. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's own line of teachers belonged among them.

Outside the orders, the Shia masters still used some of the orders' forms. Three scholars, set side by side, show how far they went. Sayyid Haydar Amuli made a contract with the Sufi shaykh Nur al-Din Tihrani and spent nearly a month in his company, "until he gave me the real patched garment of the Sufis to wear" [@inner_secrets, p. 8]. He reports that the shaykhs agree a traveller should not set out "on his own without a shaykh of perfection". He also reads the verse addressed to the soul at rest (Q 89:27–30) as a call to accept the guidance "of the Prophet, the Imam or the shaykh without any further argument" [@inner_secrets, p. 192].

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures take such a pledge for granted. Their detailed account of the road begins once the traveller has made an oath of allegiance (*bayʿa*) to an aware shaykh who is a friend of God, has passed through annihilation, and has reached remaining in God [@kernel, p. 60]. Among the traveller's duties they count loyalty, which includes keeping to the end "the promises and pledges one has given to one's aware shaykh" [@kernel, p. 64]. Ayatullah Khomeini, as Chapter Four told, was bound to his teacher Shahabadi as a disciple to his guide.

The forms served the road without carrying the authority an order claims for its chain, and Amuli says as much of his own robe. After setting out the chains of the robe he held, he writes: "My arriving at the Truth and my unveilings were not dependent on the above." He had reached the goal, he says, "through the care and grace of God, not through any action on my part nor through any knowledge I possessed" [@inner_secrets, p. 14]. He ranks the robe with his own efforts and his learning. The teacher, the robe, and the pledge served the road, and what came to him at its end came from God.

If what the traveller receives comes from God, the question is through whom it comes and where the road's authority lies. Chapter Seven found that the traveller never leaves the company of the Prophet and the Imams even after he arrives. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures describe that company on the road. The traveller "travels through the planes of the Imam's luminosity and whatever degree he may ascend to and whatever rank he may attain, the Imam ('a) too possess that rank and accompanies the wayfarer at that degree and rank". This company of the Imam, in his words, "is the most important" of all the conditions of the road [@kernel, p. 81].

The general teacher of Chapter Seven, a learned guide anyone may consult, is needed only at the beginning of the road. His company can be left once the visions come, whereas the Imam's company can never be left. Whatever forms a Shia master used therefore sit under the Imam, in the order Amuli's own words give them: the Prophet, the Imam, and the shaykh. In Duʿa Tawassul we call on the Prophet and the Imams by name. They are the special teachers of Tabatabaʾi's lectures, and theirs is the chain a Shia traveller holds to.

Shia travellers therefore share the orders' source and their path. In Shia teaching, the authority the orders give to a chain of masters belongs to the Imams (as). Their line runs from the Prophet to the Imam of our time, as the names in Duʿa Tawassul do. Others have walked this road before us. Its roots are in the teachings of the Qur'an, the Prophet, and the Imams from his Ahl al-Bayt, and its authority stays with the Imams.

If the road's authority rests with the Imams, what does its end look like in those who reached it?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
For one week, notice which of the acts of worship you already keep (the salah, the Qur'an, or a duʿa) is the one in which your heart is most present. Then give that act a fixed time each day. Sheikh Bahmanpour's advice, for a road on which no two travellers are alike, is simple: "Everyone should find his or her own suitable type of practice and worship." If no single act stands out, say *la ilaha illa Allah* silently through the day, where no one else can hear it, as many Shia mystics prefer. <!-- src: L14 ¶57-58, ¶71 -->
:::
