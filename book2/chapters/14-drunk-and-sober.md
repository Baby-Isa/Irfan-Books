Each Shaʿban, in the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, we ask God for a place among those He glanced at until they swooned before His Majesty [@duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §109–110]. Some travellers reached that swoon. Chapter Twelve followed the traveller into annihilation and back, keeping to principles rather than names. The tradition keeps the names, and what came after the swoon divided them.

The mystics have two words for the two outcomes. Intoxication (*sukr*) is losing oneself in God until nothing else is seen. Persian poetry praises the drunkard who leaves every bond to this world behind and forgets everything, and in the mystics' use the forgetting is for God. Sobriety (*sahw*) is coming back to oneself. This chapter sets out who stayed drunk and who came back sober, and the theory their greatest heir made of what they saw. It then turns to the scholars who have been wary of it all, and to the answers given to their concerns.
<!-- src: L13 ¶13-14 -->

::: {.words}
*sukr* / *sahw*: intoxication / sobriety · *shath*: an ecstatic saying · *ishara*: allusion, the veiled language of the mystics · *wahdat al-wujud*: the unity of being · *hulul* / *ittihad*: indwelling / union, God dwelling in a creature or becoming one with it
:::

## Lost in God, or back with God

:::: {.point}
Of those who reached annihilation, the drunk stayed lost in it and spoke of it, while the sober came back, kept its secret, and went further.

1. Bayazid, it is said, was the first to speak openly of annihilation; he shed his self "as a snake from its skin".
2. He reached it by renouncing this world, then the next, then all but God, until he wanted nothing.
3. In that intoxication some spoke ecstatic sayings (*shath*); Bayazid's "Praise is to me" seems to say "I am God". *(Story, part 1.)*
4. Whether a believer can say it: the Sheikh gives two readings. *(Reader told: "two readings"; story, part 2.)*
    a. God spoke from his tongue, as He spoke to Musa from the tree (Q 28:30).
    b. He no longer saw himself, only God: "there is no you anymore".
    c. On either reading no man became God.
5. Hallaj spoke openly: the jurists called him an apostate, and the ʿurafaʾ excused his words but blamed him for revealing secrets.
6. Secrets are kept because those who have not seen cannot bear them (Abu Dharr and Salman), so the sober spoke in allusion (*ishara*).
7. Junayd kept the secret: a moderate mystic who dressed like the jurists.
8. He was ranked higher for going past annihilation into remaining, a new life in God.
9. The Sheikh's river: Bayazid stopped at it, and Junayd was probably the first to cross.
10. Going further kept Junayd at the night prayer.
11. Therefore: the drunk stayed lost and spoke, while the sober came back, kept the secret, and went further. Yet their greatest heir wrote it down as a theory.
::::

The first name the tradition attaches to this experience is Bayazid Bistami, who came from the small town of Bistam in the north of Iran. It is said that he was the first to speak openly of the annihilation of the self in God, and of the self he had lost he said, "I came forth from Bayazid-ness as a snake from its skin" [@light, p. 25]. The image fits what Chapter Twelve found. A snake sheds a skin it has outgrown and goes on living, and what falls away in annihilation is a false self, while the soul remains.
<!-- src: L13 ¶16 -->

Bayazid reached annihilation by giving things up. Asked what renunciation (*zuhd*) is, he answered with his own days. "I renounced the world on the first day, and the Hereafter on the second; the third day I renounced everything other than God, and on the fourth I was asked, 'What do you want?' I said, 'I want not to want anything!'" [@kernel, p. 73]. To renounce the Hereafter, as the lectures explain it, is to desire nothing but God. *Kernel of the Kernels* sets the saying at the point Chapter Eleven reached, where even love is left at the door and the traveller "tries to forget love and the beloved" [@kernel, p. 73].
<!-- src: L13 ¶16-17 -->

A man so emptied can lose hold of his tongue, and in that intoxication some mystics spoke ecstatic sayings (*shath*), words drawn from what they had seen that make no sense to the rest of us. The saying of Bayazid's that fascinated many mystics after him runs, "Praise is to me, how great my majesty is." As statements about God, the lectures judge, such sayings are not right in themselves, though they come out of moments of ecstasy, and the ʿurafaʾ say the same of Bayazid: "he uttered these words when he was beside himself in ecstasy" [@light, p. 25]. Taken at its word, though, the saying still seems to say "I am God".
<!-- src: L13 ¶19 -->

Whether a believer can say such words and remain a believer is the question Sheikh Bahmanpour then takes up, and he gives two readings that mystics have offered. In the first, Bayazid had lost all control, and God was speaking from his tongue as He spoke to Musa from a tree in Surah al-Qasas:

::: {.ayah ref="28:30" words="11-"}
from the tree: ‘Moses! Indeed I am Allah, the Lord of all the worlds!’
:::

The words came from a tree, yet, as the lecture puts it, "the tree was not Allah. Allah was speaking from the tree." On this reading the words "Praise is to me" were God's, spoken from the tongue of a man who had stepped out of the way, and they claim nothing for Bayazid himself.
<!-- src: L13 ¶20 -->

Many mystics who had the same experiences preferred a second reading. Bayazid had come to a station where he saw only God, and he spoke when God's glory had removed him from his own sight. When he looked for himself he did not find himself there, because, in the lecture's words, "there is no you anymore".
<!-- src: L13 ¶21 -->

On either reading, no man became God. The tree did not become God when the words came from it, and what went from Bayazid's sight was the false self Chapter Twelve described, while his soul remained and God remained God. Later Sufis rejected any reading of such experiences that makes the lover and the Beloved one.
<!-- src: L13 ¶23 -->

Not everyone who spoke like this was excused. Husayn ibn Mansur al-Hallaj spoke openly about himself and his relation to God, and Sheikh Bahmanpour calls his statements "very unacceptable". In Murtada Mutahhari's account, the jurists "pronounced him an apostate", and he was executed in 309/913 [@light, p. 27]. The ʿurafaʾ excused his words as they excused Bayazid's, and they remember him as a martyr, though they "accuse him of disclosing spiritual secrets" [@light, p. 27]. Hafiz put that charge into a couplet: "He said, that friend, who was raised high on the cross, / His crime was that he used to reveal secrets" [@light, p. 27].
<!-- src: L13 ¶15 -->

What the ʿurafaʾ blamed was the telling rather than the truth told. "If they reveal their experiences ordinary people cannot tolerate it," the lecture explains, because our idea of God is too narrow to take in what they have seen. Many traditions report that had Abu Dharr known what was in Salman's heart, he would have taken him for an unbeliever, though both were companions of the Prophet ﷺ [@light, p. 23]. Abu Dharr, whose lonely death in exile opened Chapter Thirteen, was no ordinary believer. The sober therefore spoke in allusion (*ishara*), a language outsiders could not follow, and it became a custom among the early mystics.
<!-- src: L13 ¶30-31 -->

Junayd of Baghdad kept the secret, and he criticised Hallaj for speaking openly. He died in Baghdad in 298/910, more than thirty years after Bayazid, and he had been the pupil and nephew of Sari al-Saqati and a pupil of al-Muhasibi [@light, pp. 25–27]. The ʿurafaʾ call him Sayyid al-Taʾifa, the master of the community, "just as the Shi'ah jurisprudents call al-Shaykh al-Tusi Shaykh al-Ta'ifah" [@light, p. 26]. Mutahhari counts him "one of the moderate mystics", and no ecstatic sayings were ever heard from his lips. Junayd even "dressed like scholars and jurisprudents" rather than in the usual dress of the Sufis [@light, p. 27].
<!-- src: L13 ¶27-28, ¶30, ¶32 -->

The tradition ranked Junayd higher for where he went rather than for his silence. For him intoxication is a passing experience at the first steps of the road; annihilation is not the goal. Beyond it lies remaining (*baqaʾ*), which is the sober return Chapter Twelve found loftier than annihilation itself. Remaining, for Junayd, is "a new life in God", and in it a man becomes more aware of himself rather than less.
<!-- src: L13 ¶14-15, ¶28-29 -->

Sheikh Bahmanpour pictures the difference with explorers. After travelling thousands of kilometres they reach a river and take it for the end of the world, until better-equipped travellers cross it and find many other things beyond. In his account Bayazid stopped at the river, preferring intoxication because it "obliterates the human attributes", while Junayd was probably the first to cross.
<!-- src: L13 ¶28-29 -->

Going further kept Junayd at his prayers. Sayyid Haydar Amuli records his words, "Uncertainties have vanished and all signs have disappeared; the only thing of benefit to us is the prayer we perform in the depth of the night" [@inner_secrets, p. 162]. With every doubt gone and every sign behind him, what Junayd held on to was the night prayer, which any believer can keep.

Those who reached annihilation therefore divided. The drunk stayed lost in it and spoke of what they saw. The sober came back, kept its secret in allusion, held to the night prayer, and went further. Yet the greatest of their heirs wrote what they saw down as a theory, and a secret kept and a theory written seem hard to hold together.

## A theory of what they saw

:::: {.point}
Ibn ʿArabi gave what they saw a theory, the unity of being: one existence, with creation its real manifestation, neither God nor apart from Him, known by experience rather than argument.

1. The heir was Ibn ʿArabi, for Mutahhari the greatest mystic of Islam.
2. He called what they saw the unity of being: one existence, and all things its manifestations. *(History box: four views of existence, each closer than the last.)*
3. If there is one existence, creation is His manifestation: He is "the Manifest and the Hidden" (Q 57:3).
4. Then we are neither God nor nothing. *(Reader told: "neither".)*
    a. Not God: each of us has "gained existence from Him".
    b. Not nothing: creation is "more real than you think".
    c. Imam ʿAli (as) held both halves; Ibn ʿArabi made such statements a theory.
    d. Ayatullah Khomeini's sea and waves, an image he calls imperfect.
5. It is grasped by experience, above reason, so writing it down gave no secret away.
6. Only those with the experience can check it: the Sheikh found ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's analysis in Ibn ʿArabi. *(Story.)*
7. Many mystics found no better language for their own experience, and through Mulla Sadra it reached the Shia masters.
8. Therefore: one existence, creation its real manifestation, known by experience; and leaning on it drew critics.
::::

The greatest of those heirs was Ibn ʿArabi, who died in Damascus in 638/1240 [@light, p. 35]. Mutahhari calls him "certainly the greatest mystic of Islam" [@light, p. 34] and writes that "the mystics who came after him ate the crumbs from his table" [@light, p. 35]. Shia and Sunni mystics alike know him as the Greatest Shaykh. Chapter Five told how, as a sick boy of twelve, he saw Surah Yasin come to his defence.
<!-- src: L13 ¶33 -->

Ibn ʿArabi called what the sober had seen the unity of being (*wahdat al-wujud*). There is only one existence; all existing things are its manifestations, its states, and its particular forms. Others had alluded to it before him, whereas he made it a theory. The theory is clearest when it is set beside the other views of existence held in Islamic thought.
<!-- src: L13 ¶55, ¶60 -->

::: {.box-history title="History: Four views of existence"}
Islamic thought has given four answers to how the existence of God stands to the existence of everything else, and each brings the two closer than the last. The first two belong to the schools of philosophy Chapter Two named. The Peripatetics held that there are many existences, so that God has His and the sun, the earth, and every animal have their own. The Illuminationists held that there is one existence, God's, and that other beings are essences that take their existence from it. Mulla Sadra, drawing on the old Persian sages, held that there is one existence in grades from strong to weak, as the sun and a small lamp differ in strength "but all of them are light". With this gradation of being (*tashkik*) he came as close as reasoning has come to the fourth view, Ibn ʿArabi's, that there is one existence and everything else is its manifestation. The unity of being stands one step past the point where reasoning stopped.
:::
<!-- src: L13 ¶56-62 -->

One existence leaves a plain question about where creation stands, and Sheikh Bahmanpour puts it to his listeners. He asks whether what God created is outside Him, a new existence beside His, or a manifestation of Him. The theory he reports holds that "you cannot say that there's anything besides God". Surah al-Hadid names God in a way that makes room for both what we see and what stays hidden:

::: {.ayah ref="57:3" words="1-5"}
He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden
:::

Because God is the Manifest, what we see in creation is His manifestation, and because He is the Hidden, what we see is not He.
<!-- src: L13 ¶60-61, ¶64-65 -->

If nothing stands beside God, it can seem that each of us is either God or nothing. The lectures answer that we are neither. We are not God: in the lecture's words, "certainly I'm not God or a part of God". God gave existence to beings whose limits He had planned from eternity, and so each of us has "gained existence from Him". It is what we mean, as Chapter Twelve found, when we say after the prayer that there is no power and no strength except through God.
<!-- src: L13 ¶63-64 -->

Nor are we nothing. The lectures are as firm on this side: creation "is not illusion" and is "more real than you think". Its reality is that of a manifestation of God, which is not separate from Him and is still not He.
<!-- src: L13 ¶66 -->

Imam ʿAli (as) held both halves at once in the oration from which Chapter Two quoted the line on soaring thoughts:

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of Nahj 1.1.1, He is with all things but not by association; the nahj extract's Arabic on p. 121 is garbled]" source="Nahj al-Balagha"}
He is with all things but not by association. He is other than all things but not by detachment. [@nahj, 1.1.1]
:::

God is with everything, so nothing stands apart from Him, and He is other than everything, so nothing is He. The verse of Surah al-Hadid and this oration held the truth as statements. In the lectures' account, it was Ibn ʿArabi who made a theory of them, and after him readers saw his theory in their words.
<!-- src: L13 ¶64-66 -->

For both halves Ayatullah Khomeini offers the image of the sea and its waves, and he writes that "the waves of the sea are not separate from the sea itself, but still the sea is not the waves, although the waves are the sea" [@light, p. 144]. He adds at once that "even this similitude is not perfect, for no similitude can properly illustrate the relation between Allah and His creation" [@light, p. 144].

No argument fits the unity of being better than an image does. The lectures report Ibn ʿArabi's verdict, that "you can never rationalise this theory. It is above the rank of reason." The mind is not built for it; it is understood "only through hard experience", which is the line Chapter Two drew between knowing about God and knowing Him. This settles the puzzle the sober left: Ibn ʿArabi wrote the theory down, yet writing it gave no secret away, since without the experience no one can understand it. The secret the sober kept and the theory their heir wrote are one thing seen from two sides.
<!-- src: L13 ¶62, ¶66-67 -->

If only experience can grasp the theory, only those who have the experience can check it, and Sheikh Bahmanpour tells how he learned this. Reading a book of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's on the names of God, he was amazed at its analysis, until he read Ibn ʿArabi's *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* (*The Meccan Illuminations*) and found it all there. Tabatabaʾi loved Ibn ʿArabi and never claimed the knowledge as his own, and Ayatullah Hasanzadeh Amuli reports him saying that he had learned it all from him. Tabatabaʾi's own experiences "matched very much" what Ibn ʿArabi had experienced, and judging such a theory, the lecture concludes, "needs someone who has the experience".
<!-- src: L13 ¶42-43 -->

Many mystics found that the theory matched their own experience, and they "found no better language" for it than Ibn ʿArabi's. Mulla Sadra's transcendent philosophy set Ibn ʿArabi's mystical theory out in reasoned form, and through Sadra his influence runs to Ayatullah Khomeini and to the Shia masters of Iran today.
<!-- src: L13 ¶45-47, ¶50-51 -->

Ibn ʿArabi therefore gave what the sober saw a theory. There is one existence, and creation is its real manifestation, which is neither God nor apart from Him. Those who have the experience know it, whereas argument cannot reach it. Leaning on that theory, and on the sayings of the drunk before it, has drawn critics of its own.

## The critics' concerns, and the answers

:::: {.point}
The critics feared that the ʿurafaʾ were not sincere Muslims, that their God was merged with the world, and that the law was set aside; the masters' own words and lives answer each fear, and only Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties stay open.

1. Mutahhari sorts the critics, and their concerns come down to three: sincerity, God and the world, and the law. A Shia reader adds a fourth, about Ibn ʿArabi himself. *(Reader told: "three", then "a fourth".)*
2. Sincerity: mistakes, perhaps, but no evil intentions (Mutahhari).
3. God and the world has two halves: pantheism, and God dwelling in a man; the theory has answered the first.
    a. Tabatabaʾi answers the second: indwelling and union are unbelief, far from the unity of being (with Sayyid Haydar Amuli).
    b. Ayatullah Khomeini rebukes those who accuse without study, and the mystic who belittles the jurists.
4. The law: the tradition judged its own ecstatics, and words spoken beside oneself are no rule.
5. The masters, drunk and sober alike, kept the law.
6. Ibn ʿArabi's loyalties: hard to call him Shia, and some of his passages go against Shia belief.
7. The Sheikh answers in three ways: reason, not experience, settles sect; visions reflect the seer's theology; no one is accepted or rejected wholesale.
    a. Side by side: his chain runs back to Gilani; Sayyid Haydar Amuli reads the Seal as Imam ʿAli (as); the Sunni case is "much much more powerful". The theory can be taken without its author's loyalties (the book's reading). *(Who's Who box: al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi and the Seal of the Saints.)*
8. Therefore: each fear has an answer; the sober went furthest, and the theory, though contested, is neither pantheism nor licence.

Close: "How did the road become orders, and where do Shia stand?" *(Try this box.)*
::::

The prologue promised to set out why some scholars have been wary of ʿirfan. Mutahhari sorts the critics into groups. One group of traditionists and jurists held that the ʿurafaʾ "are not practically bound to Islam", and that their appeals to the Qur'an and the Sunna are "merely a ruse to deceive the simple-minded people" [@light, p. 13]. A group of modernists welcomed ʿirfan as a movement against Islam and its law [@light, p. 13]. Their concerns come down to three: whether the mystics were sincere, whether their God was merged with the world, and whether they set the law aside. A Shia reader will add a fourth concern about Ibn ʿArabi himself.

On the first, Mutahhari sides with a third group, which takes a neutral view. Its members grant that ʿirfan holds innovations, which are found more in its practice than in its theory. "It is quite possible that they have made mistakes," they allow, as theologians, philosophers, and jurists have made mistakes, but never out of bad faith [@light, p. 14]. In Mutahhari's own words, "I do not believe that the 'urafa' have had evil intentions towards Islam" [@light, p. 14]. He asks those who know both ʿirfan and the teachings of Islam to study the question with care [@light, p. 14].

The second concern has two halves. One is pantheism, which holds that the world is God. The other is the belief that God can come to dwell in a man. The theory has answered the first already, since creation is neither God nor a part of Him.

*Kernel of the Kernels* records ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's answer to the second. A traveller who imagines God united with his own ego has fallen into indwelling (*hulul*) and union (*ittihad*), "which are kufr and shirk". By contrast, the unity of being denies all plurality and otherness before God: "And how far is this from the notions of hulul and ittihad" [@kernel, p. 84]. Sayyid Haydar Amuli likewise counts the gnostics "far removed from such false assumptions and lying fantasies" [@inner_secrets, p. 163].

Ayatullah Khomeini turns the question back on the accusers. He grieved that Mulla Sadra was called a heretic and "a Sufi through and through", though Sadra had written a book refuting the Sufis [@forty_hadith, p. 402]. God may ask one who never studied the unity of being, he warns, "Then why did you blindly accuse them of unbelief and insult them?" [@forty_hadith, p. 345]. He puts the same question to a mystic who belittles the jurists, since "None of the scholars of any one of these sciences has a right to find fault with the others" [@forty_hadith, pp. 345–346].

The third concern is the one the prologue named as the chief reason for wariness, that mysticism sets the law aside, and the tradition judged its own ecstatics on this point. The ʿurafaʾ "say that the statements of al-Hallaj and Bayazid that gave the impression of unbelief were made when they were beside themselves in the state of 'intoxication'" [@light, p. 27]. Words spoken by someone beside himself are no rule for a sober believer. Chapter Twelve found unacceptable the claim that duties are lifted from one who has arrived, and such words give it no ground.

Drunk and sober alike, the masters kept the law that Chapter Six found binding to the last breath. Junayd held to the prayer of the night, as we saw. Sayyid Haydar Amuli counts Bayazid among those who prayed a great many cycles each day and night, and he records that Ibn ʿArabi "would pray two cycles of prayer for every prophet and saint, after performing all the obligatory cycles of prayers" [@inner_secrets, p. 162].

The fourth concern comes from within the Shia community, and it asks whether Ibn ʿArabi was one of us. "It is difficult to say that he was a Shia," Sheikh Bahmanpour grants. Ibn ʿArabi respected the Imams (as) and the first two caliphs as well, and some of his passages and visions go against Shia belief. Opinion on him has always been divided, since friends called him the Perfect Saint and enemies "the Killer of the Faith" [@light, p. 35].
<!-- src: L13 ¶47-49, ¶52 -->

The lectures meet this concern in three ways. Experience cannot settle which school is right, since "this Shia Sunni business should only be solved by rational arguments rather than by experiences." Visions, moreover, take the shape of the theology a person already holds; Ibn ʿArabi's visions may reflect his own. Lastly, no one can be accepted or rejected wholesale, and "certainly he had something valuable in what he wrote".
<!-- src: L13 ¶48, ¶52-53 -->

The record pulls both ways. Through a single link, the chain of Ibn ʿArabi's Sufi order runs back to ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Gilani [@light, p. 34]. On the other hand, Sayyid Haydar Amuli reads Ibn ʿArabi's own words on the Seal of the Saints as showing that the absolute Seal is Imam ʿAli (as) [@inner_secrets, p. 99]. Sheikh Bahmanpour weighed a two-volume book arguing that he was Shia against another arguing the opposite. He found the Sunni case "much much more powerful", because "we shouldn't be unjust". A Shia reader, as this book reads the lectures, can therefore take the theory without its author's loyalties.
<!-- src: L13 ¶54-55 -->

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi and the Seal of the Saints"}
The mystics' theory grew close to what Shia hold of the Imams, and then went past it. Al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi stands near the start of that story. He was a mystic and philosopher of the third century, not the compiler of the hadith collection that shares his name. Beside the early mystics' three leanings to fear, renunciation, and love, there was a fourth that put knowledge of God (*maʿrifah*) first, and Tirmidhi was its leading figure. The lectures call him "a turning point" in bringing theory into mysticism, and many of Ibn ʿArabi's ideas came from him. As there is a Seal of the Prophets, Tirmidhi spoke of a Seal of the Saints (*khatm al-awliyaʾ*), with a hierarchy of saints who keep the world in order. These are the ideas Shia hold of the Imams, taken, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "to very extreme type of concept which is not even acceptable by Shia". Ibn ʿArabi took up the Seal at length, and some read him as "talking more about himself than others". The lectures decline to judge what was meant, whereas Sayyid Haydar Amuli reads the absolute Seal as Imam ʿAli (as) [@inner_secrets, p. 99].
:::
<!-- src: L13 ¶3-12, ¶49 -->

The critics' fears therefore have answers in the masters' own words and lives. The ʿurafaʾ were sincere, though they could err. The unity of being neither makes the world God nor puts God inside a man. Ecstatic words gave no licence, since the masters, drunk as well as sober, kept the law. Only Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties stay open, and his theory can be taken without them. Among those who reached annihilation the sober went furthest. The theory of what they saw is contested, but it is neither pantheism nor licence.

Ibn ʿArabi's chain, as we saw, ran back to ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Gilani, who headed one of the great Sufi orders. How did the road become orders, and where do Shia stand?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
Surah al-Hadid is one of the Musabbihat, the surahs that open with God's glorification. When you recite or hear it this week, stop at its third verse: "He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden" (Q 57:3). Then, once a day, when something in creation stops you, such as a face, an evening sky, or an act of kindness, recall how the lectures read this verse. What you see is His manifestation, because He is the Manifest, and it is not He, because He is the Hidden.
:::
