::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي إِنْ أَنَامَتْنِيَ ٱلْغَفْلَةُ عَنِ ٱلإِسْتِعْدَادِ لِلِقَائِكَ فَقَدْ نَبَّهَتْنِي ٱلْمَعْرِفَةُ بِكَرَمِ آلاَئِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, if my disregard for preparations to meet You has put me to sleep, my knowledge of Your kind bounties has awakened me. [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §118–119]
:::

These words come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, the prayer of the month of Shaʿban. The one who prays admits that neglect has put them to sleep, unready to meet God. Knowing His generosity has woken them. The prayer uses sleep as a picture of neglect, and sets it beside the meeting with God.

The tradition also speaks of real sleep in the same breath as death. *Lantern of the Path*, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), calls sleep "the brother of death" [@lantern, p. 24]. Death is what we stand before at a graveside. There we say that the dead are now in *barzakh* (the world between this life and the resurrection), and many of us picture a waiting room somewhere far away.

Sheikh Bahmanpour teaches otherwise: at death "we don't go anywhere". The dead are not far away, and the reason lies in what the soul is. The way to God runs through the soul, the one thing we know from the inside. How deep the soul goes therefore matters. This chapter sets out the soul and its worlds: which worlds the soul lives in, how it moves between them, and how its deeds give it a form there. These three steps show that what the soul finally becomes is, by God's grace, in our hands.

::: {.words}
*ʿalam al-mulk*: the material world · *ʿalam al-mithal* or *barzakh*: the middle world of images · *ʿalam al-ʿuqul*: the world of pure intellects · *badan mithali*: the image body · *muraqaba*: watchfulness; keeping the heart's attention on God
:::

## One world at three depths

::: {.point}
The world has three depths, so the dead have gone deeper into it, not away.

1. The soul is a copy of creation, so each of its three ways of knowing samples one world.
2. The senses meet the material world; the other two worlds are unseen, not distant.
3. The imagination shows that shape and colour can exist without matter.
4. Because the soul is a copy of creation, our imagination is a sample of a created world of images that does not depend on anyone's mind.
5. The intellect grasps meanings with no image or matter, and these belong to a third world, the world of intellects.
6. The world of images links the other two, so it is called barzakh, a barrier.
7. None of these worlds is a place; each is a deeper dimension of this one, the world of intellects with some difference.
8. Imam ʿAli (as) taught that this world lies inside the hereafter, which surrounds it.
9. So far: three worlds make one reality, and none of them is far away.
10. Therefore the dead have gone deeper into our world, not away from it.

New story (D47): The churchman's question to Imam ʿAli, L04 ¶62.
:::

Let's start with a claim of the mystics: the human soul is the whole of creation in small, a compact copy of everything that exists. We know things in three ways: through the senses, the imagination, and the intellect. If the soul is a copy of creation, each of these three should give us a sample of one world. Each world can then be approached through something we already know.

The senses meet the first world, the material world (*ʿalam al-mulk*). It is made of matter: things with weight that take up space, such as a table, a hand, or the rain. Because our senses witness it, it is also called the witnessed world. The other two worlds are called unseen. They are unseen not because they are far away but because the ordinary eye does not perceive them without training.

The imagination gives us a sample of the second world. When we picture a friend's face, the picture has a shape and a colour, yet it weighs nothing and is made of nothing material. A dream does the same more vividly. The people in it have size and colour, and none of it is matter.

If the soul is a copy of creation, our imagination is a small sample of a larger world of this kind, the world of images (*ʿalam al-mithal*). Like the picture in our mind, it has shape, size, and colour, and no matter at all. The difference is that the picture in our mind belongs to us, whereas the world of images is not inside anyone's mind. It is part of God's creation, and it exists whether or not anyone imagines it.

The intellect gives us a sample of the third world. Every person we have met has a particular height, voice, and face. Yet the mind can grasp "a human being" with none of these features. No such person exists anywhere outside the mind. This kind of understanding deals in meanings, with no image and no matter. Its world is the world of intellects (*ʿalam al-ʿuqul*). The angels who live there are pure intellect, attached to nothing material.

The world of images stands between the other two. That is why it is also called barzakh, a barrier. Pure intellect and matter cannot meet directly, so a middle world is needed between them. Whatever is in the world of intellects is reflected first in the world of images, which has the shape and colour of matter without its weight. Only then does it reach the world of matter.

None of these worlds is a place we travel to. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the world of images "is another dimension of the same world in which we are living", and so, with some difference, is the world of intellects. The unseen worlds surround this one, but in depth and not in distance: each is a deeper dimension of the world we are in now.

Imam ʿAli (as) also spoke of this world as surrounded, in a story Sheikh Bahmanpour tells. A Christian churchman once came to him with questions. One was whether the hereafter lies inside this world, or this world inside the hereafter. Imam ʿAli (as) answered that this world lies inside the hereafter. This world is the smaller one, and the hereafter surrounds it. The lectures set the answer beside the verse that says hell surrounds the faithless (Q 9:49).

There are three worlds, then, and they make one reality. The senses meet matter, the imagination samples a world of images, and the intellect grasps pure meaning. Each lies deeper than the one before, and none of them is far away.

This changes the picture at the graveside. The dead are in barzakh, which is the world of images, and that world is not a distant room. It is a deeper dimension of the world we are living in. Even the resurrection is not a journey elsewhere but a move deeper still, closer to the majesty and beauty of God.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: barzakh"}
A *barzakh* is a barrier, something that stands between two things and keeps each in its place. The Qur'an uses the word for the barrier God set between two seas that He merged, one sweet and one briny (Q 25:53). It also uses it of death. One of the deniers, when death comes, asks to be sent back so that he may act righteously, and he is refused. Before such people, the verse says, is a barrier until the day they are resurrected (Q 23:99–100). The ʿurafaʾ use the same word for the world of images, which stands between matter and pure intellect. Mohammad Ali Shomali notes that philosophers compare the body of that world with the bodies we see in dreams, and "call them 'Barzakh' or 'mithali'" [@self_knowledge, p. 76]. The lectures join the two uses: the middle world is called barzakh because it stands between matter and intellect, and it is where the dead are until the resurrection.
:::

## A soul that crosses them

::: {.point}
The soul moves between the worlds, as far as the life it lives takes it.

1. The soul links the spirit God breathed into us with the body, so it has a share in every world.
2. For now the body holds the soul's attention, and that hold loosens at three times.
3. In sleep the soul touches the world of images, which is why a dream can show what has not yet happened.
4. The Qur'an sets sleep beside death: God takes the soul in both.
5. At death the soul lets go of the body completely and lives in an image body with shape but no mass.
6. The image body was with us all along, so at death the soul sets the body down and goes on in it.
7. Awake, a person can loosen the hold by turning their attention to God.
8. So far: sleep, death, and watchfulness each loosen the body's hold, and each time the soul can reach the world of images.
9. The angels, unlike the soul, each have a fixed station they cannot leave.
10. Therefore the soul, which has none, goes as far as the life it lives takes it.
:::

The soul has a share in each of these worlds, because it was made to stand between them. The Qur'an says that God breathed of His spirit into the first human being (Q 15:29). God has no spirit as part of Himself, since He is not made of parts. The spirit is called His out of honour, as the Kaʿba is called His house. It is the greatest of His creations. Pure spirit and matter cannot meet directly. The soul is the means by which that spirit reaches the body, as the world of images links intellect and matter.

For now, the body holds almost all of the soul's attention, so the soul lives in the material world. That hold loosens at three times: in sleep, at death, and in contemplation.

The first is sleep. When we sleep, the soul attends less to the body. With the senses at rest, it can touch the world of images. It does not leave the body completely; if it did, we would be dead. This explains a dream that is more than memory. A person may dream of someone doing something that later happens, and a memory cannot show the future. Time works differently in the middle world. The soul has seen there the counterpart of an event that has not yet happened here.

The Qur'an also sets sleep beside death. Surah al-Zumar names the two together.

::: {.ayah ref="39:42" words="1-10"}
Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those who have not died, in their sleep.
:::

In both, the verse says, God takes the soul. The difference between the two is one of degree. Mohammad Ali Shomali explains that in sleep the spirit is still connected with the body, "but to a lesser degree than waking". At death it is disconnected from the body and "belongs to another body which has the qualities of a material body like shape and size without the mass" [@self_knowledge, pp. 75–76]. This second body is the image body (*badan mithali*). It has our shape, but it is not made of any material elements.

At death the soul is not slipping out of a body that held it. "The body is not carrying the soul but the soul is carrying the body," Sheikh Bahmanpour says. When the appointed time comes, the soul sets the body down and goes. It does not go without a body, because a soul cannot be without one. The image body was with us all along, unseen in another dimension, and the soul goes on in it.

The third loosening can happen while we are awake. A person who turns their attention to God until they forget the body does what sleep does. They can then see in the middle world as one sees in a dream. This is the work of watchfulness (*muraqaba*): keeping the heart's attention on God. Nothing leaves the body; what turns is the attention. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi said that the more he kept up his muraqaba, the more distinctly he saw into that world. Such moments come to the mystics as an opportunity from God and not as a power to use. Only the Imams (as), the lectures add, can pass between the worlds whenever they wish.

::: {.box-story title="Story: Qadi meets himself"}
*Kernel of the Kernels*, the record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures on the path, describes what may come to a traveller who keeps up muraqaba. Lights appear, at first like flashes of lightning, and "these lights belong to the class of entities of the realm of Barzakh". In the early stages the traveller may see his own soul in bodily form: "he might see himself standing before himself." The lectures then tell of their teacher, Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, who used to say: "One day emerging from the room into the veranda, I saw myself standing silent by my side. I looked very carefully at my face, and observed a mole on it. On returning to my room when I looked into the mirror, I saw that there was a mole on my face that I had not noticed until then" [@kernel, p. 17].
:::

Sleep, death, and contemplation are three ways of loosening one hold. In sleep the hold slackens for a night, at death it lets go entirely, and in watchfulness a person loosens it on purpose. Each time, the soul can reach the world of images.

The angels have no such movement between worlds. Each has a fixed station that it cannot rise above. An angel does not change or grow, because it is made as it will always be. Surah al-Saffat gives their own words.

::: {.ayah ref="37:164"}
There is none among us but has a known place.
:::

The human soul has no known place of this kind. It can move and change, and no one knows how far it can go. The mystics say that this capacity is why the angels bowed down to Adam. A soul's reach is not fixed in advance, as an angel's station is. It expands, the lectures say, as the soul learns the names of God. How far it goes therefore depends on the life it lives.

## Our deeds give the soul a form

::: {.point}
What we do now shapes the image body we will meet after death.

1. Repeated acts shape the self, as the habit of lying shows.
2. The image body records that shaping far more than the face does: sin deforms it and piety lights it.
3. The state of the soul at death fixes the form it takes in the next world.
4. On the Last Day people will be grouped by their true natures.
5. Piety shines as light on the Last Day; Shomali holds that some rewards are the deeds themselves made visible.
6. Therefore the image body is being shaped now, and part of what we meet after death may be our own deeds.

New story (D47): Ibn ʿArabi and Surah Yasin, L13 ¶35–37.
:::

If every soul passes into the middle world in any case, it can seem that what we do here changes little. Habit shows otherwise, because repeated acts shape the self. Shomali takes the example of lying. A child telling a first lie feels uneasy and later remorseful. With repetition "the soul becomes inured to the effects" [@self_knowledge, p. 20], until lying costs little effort.

Some of that shaping shows on the face. A face can show faith or its absence to those with eyes to see it. In this world, though, the change is slow and small. It does not deform the body. The image body is far more sensitive. Sin deforms it and piety makes it shine, so that it takes the shape of what our habits have made of the soul.

Imam Khomeini describes a comparable second form in his *Forty Hadith*. Beside the body that everyone can see, each person has "another form and a different face of him, which is heavenly and invisible, which is determined by the qualities of his soul and his inner nature" [@forty_hadith, p. 41]. The same page names the moment that settles it: "in whichever state one's soul departs from the body, it assumes a form suited to it in the next world also" [@forty_hadith, p. 41]. The face a soul wears in the next world is the face it has made by the time it dies. Surah al-Nabaʾ describes people coming forth on the Last Day.

::: {.ayah ref="78:18"}
the day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will come in groups,
:::

Shomali reports the hadith that the groups are formed "according to their true natures" [@self_knowledge, p. 21]. Surah al-Hadid describes how the faithful will appear.

::: {.ayah ref="57:12" words="1-9"}
The day you will see the faithful, men and women, with their light moving swiftly in front of them and on their right
:::

The lectures read this light as the shining of the pious themselves. The body of the Last Day, they note, is not the image body. Piety makes both shine. On the Last Day that light goes ahead to light the way. Shomali states a general principle about reward and punishment: "Sometimes rewards or punishments are nothing other than the actions. They are just the realities of those actions made manifest in another universe" [@self_knowledge, p. 77]. Some rewards, on this view, are the deeds themselves, seen for what they are.

If some rewards are the deeds themselves, the form a soul meets after death may be, in part, the work of its own deeds. Shomali himself draws a related conclusion from the fact that heaven and hell already exist: "our future is now present" [@self_knowledge, p. 77]. The image body is being shaped now, by what we do.

::: {.box-story title="Story: Ibn ʿArabi and Surah Yasin"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour tells a story that the great mystic Ibn ʿArabi told of his own childhood. At the age of twelve he fell seriously ill and went into a coma. It was so serious that everyone thought he had died. In that state he saw horrible-looking people who were trying to hurt him. Then a figure breathing out a wonderful perfume defended him and drove them away. Ibn ʿArabi asked who he was. The figure answered that he was Surah Yasin, his protector. When the boy came round, he found his father at his bedside in tears. His father had just finished reciting Surah Yasin for his recovery, and the boy had not known. The lecture draws a general point from it. Even a lie or a word of comfort, which we take for mere words, has an image in the world of images, and that image is as real as the figure the boy saw.
:::

## The self can change its state

::: {.point}
The one self can rise from its lowest state to peace, so what it becomes is, by God's grace, in our hands.

1. So far: the soul lives in more than one world, and its deeds give it a form there.
2. The lowest state of the self is the one that commands to evil, when the body rules the spirit.
3. Worry ties the soul to the body as much as pleasure does.
4. The self that blames itself after giving in is a hopeful sign.
5. Self-blame, kept up, brings the self to peace.
6. These are one self under three names, so changing our attitude changes its state.
7. The remembrance of God refreshes and revives the soul.
8. The Qur'an says hearts find rest in remembrance, and its word for rest shares a root with the name of the self at peace.
9. Fasting and the night prayer loosen the soul's tie to the body.
10. These acts work because the soul takes on habits quickly, so what we make of it is ours forever.
11. Therefore, by God's grace, what the soul finally becomes is in our hands.
:::

The picture so far is this. The soul lives in more than one world, and it can pass into the world of images in sleep, at death, and in watchfulness. What it carries there is a form made by its own deeds.

If deeds give the soul its form, the self that does the deeds is not fixed either. The Qur'an gives the self (*nafs*) three names, and the lectures read them as three states. The lowest is the self that commands to evil (*nafs al-ammara*). In it the bodily side of a person overcomes the spiritual side, and the soul goes after the body's pleasures. Surah Yusuf names this self together with the mercy that can lift it.

::: {.ayah ref="12:53" words="4-11"}
the [carnal] soul indeed prompts [men] to evil, except inasmuch as my Lord has mercy.
:::

The verse admits what this self does and names what overcomes it, the mercy of God. Its pull is not only towards pleasure. Worry binds the soul to the body as well: worry over whether our children will be poor, or whether we will have a job next year. It keeps the soul occupied with the body and stops it rising towards God.

After giving in, the self may remember what it did and ask why. This is the second state, the self that blames itself (*nafs al-lawwama*). Its self-blame is a hopeful sign. It shows that we hold values that matter more to us than the pleasure we gave in to. Surah al-Qiyama swears by it.

::: {.ayah ref="75:2"}
And I swear by the self-critical soul!
:::

That God swears by this self suggests its worth. A self that keeps up its self-blame comes in time to the third state, the self at peace (*nafs al-mutmaʾinna*). Surah al-Fajr calls this self back to its Lord (Q 89:27–28). It relies on what belongs to the other world and not on this one. It is free of sin, of greed, and of a mind turned wholly towards the body.

These are not three selves. "We don't have different types of nufus," Sheikh Bahmanpour says, using the plural of nafs. The one self takes a different name according to its attitude. Because we can change the attitude, we can change the state, and with it the name.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: the layers of the soul, counted differently"}
Murtada Mutahhari writes that the ʿurafaʾ "have different words for the human soul", and he sets out four. When the soul is "dominated and ruled by desires and passions they call it nafs". When it bears divine knowledge it is the heart (*qalb*). When "the light of Divine love dawns within it", it is the spirit (*ruh*), and when it reaches witnessing it is the secret (*sirr*). Beyond these lie the hidden (*khafi*) and the most hidden (*akhfa*) [@light, p. 58]. The lectures order them differently, describing the sirr as known only to God and the khafi as hidden even from the angels. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, a Shia master, counts the states of the self differently too, with four where the lectures have three. In his *Inner Secrets of the Path* the self ascends through "commanding to evil", "reproachful", "inspired", and "at rest" [@inner_secrets, p. 192]. Imam Khomeini notes that the soul's levels are divided "sometimes into seven sections and sometimes into four", and sometimes into three or two [@forty_hadith, p. 32]. The lists count different things (names, states, and levels). Read together, they describe one soul seen in different ways. Chapter Eight takes nafs, qalb, and ruh as stages of the road.
:::

What moves the self from one state to the next are acts already familiar to us. One is the remembrance of God (*dhikr*), which the lectures describe as refreshing and reviving the soul. Surah al-Raʿd names what it gives.

::: {.ayah ref="13:28" words="7-11"}
Behold! The hearts find rest in Allah's remembrance!
:::

In Arabic, the verse's word for finding rest comes from the same root as *mutmaʾinna*, the name of the self at peace (Q 13:28; 89:27). The rest that remembrance brings is the rest of the third state.

Fasting and the night prayer work on the same tie to the body that sleep and watchfulness loosen. The soul manages the body, down to digesting a meal, so a fasting body asks less of it. In Ramadan God pours down His light everywhere. The soul of the one who fasts is less taken up with the body, and it takes in that light without knowing it. The night prayer (*salat al-layl*) likewise helps the soul free itself from the body and see the light.

These acts work because the soul takes on habits quickly. Trained to worry about the body, it worries about the body. Trained to care for higher things, it comes to care for them and to see the light. In the lectures' image, the soul is like a fine elastic thing. It can be held in the palm of a hand or stretched to the size of the universe, and which it becomes depends on what we do with it. It has been entrusted to us for a short time. Whatever we do with it, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, "would remain with us forever".

The soul is therefore deeper than it looks. It lives in the material world and in the world of images, and it reaches towards the world of intellects. It can touch the middle world in sleep, and it passes there fully at death. Its deeds give it a form that it will meet there. The self that does those deeds can move from commanding to blaming to peace. What the soul finally becomes has been entrusted to us. The drive to begin is itself God's grace, given, the lectures say, once we have shown that we deserve it. The rest of the work is in our hands.

If the work on the soul is this inward, what keeps it from going astray?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
*Lantern of the Path* gives an intention for going to sleep. For one week, as you lie down each night, make it your own: "to lighten your burden on the angels and disengage the self from its appetites, and to examine yourself by your sleep" [@lantern, p. 24]. With it, the Lantern asks you to "be aware of the fact that you are incapable and weak. You have no power over any of your movements and stillnesses, except by the judgement and measure of Allah" [@lantern, p. 24]. Let sleep be your "last affair in this world", and remember Allah "with your heart and your tongue" before it comes [@lantern, p. 25].
:::
