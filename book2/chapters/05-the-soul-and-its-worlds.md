My Lord, if my disregard for preparations to meet You has put me to sleep, my knowledge of Your kind bounties has awakened me. [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §118–119]
:::

At a graveside we say that the dead are now in *barzakh* (the world between this life and the resurrection), and we picture it as a waiting room far from the living realm. Sheikh Bahmanpour teaches the opposite: at death "we don't go anywhere". The dead are nearer than we think.

The dead are near because the next world is not far away. Creation holds more than one world, and they are layers of one reality: each is deeper than the last, not further away. Barzakh is one of these deeper worlds. Each night, in sleep, the soul already touches it. That is why *Lantern of the Path*, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), calls sleep "the brother of death" [@lantern, p. 24]. The prayer above makes the same link: it speaks of sleep and of meeting God together. At death the soul does not travel away; it goes deeper.

This chapter sets out the soul and its worlds: which worlds creation holds; how the soul passes into them; how what we do gives the soul its form there; and why that form is, by God's grace, still in our hands.

::: {.words}
*ʿalam al-mulk*: the material world · *ʿalam al-mithal* or *barzakh*: the middle world of images · *ʿalam al-ʿuqul*: the world of pure intellects · *badan mithali*: the image body · *muraqaba*: watchfulness; keeping the heart's attention on God
:::

## One world at three depths

:::: {.point}
Creation has three worlds, one inside another, and none of them is far away.

1. The mystics hold that the soul is creation in small, so the kinds of thing the soul holds show the kinds of world creation holds.
2. The soul holds three kinds of thing, one for each way it knows. *(Reader told: "taking them one at a time shows the three worlds.")*
    a. The senses meet things made of matter: the first world, the material world.
    b. The imagination holds shape and colour with no matter; creation holds the same on a larger scale, outside anyone's mind: the world of images.
    c. The intellect holds meanings with no shape and no matter: the world of intellects.
3. So creation has three worlds that the soul mirrors, and the world of images stands between the other two: barzakh, "a barrier". Higher realms encompass all three: the Footstool, the Throne and jabarut.
4. These worlds are not places but depths of the one world we live in.
5. Story: the churchman asks Imam ʿAli (as); this world lies inside the hereafter, which surrounds it.
6. Therefore: three worlds, one inside another, none far away.
::::

Let's start with a claim of the mystics: the human soul is the whole of creation in small; it is a compact copy of everything that exists. If that is so, we can use the soul as a map of creation. Each kind of thing the soul holds has its counterpart in creation on a larger scale, and each such kind makes up a world of its own. By looking at the kinds of thing the soul holds, we can learn the kinds of world that creation holds.

The soul holds three kinds of thing, because it knows in three ways: through the senses, through the imagination, and through the intellect. Each way of knowing meets its own kind of thing. Taking them one at a time shows the three worlds.

The senses meet things made of matter: things with weight that take up space, such as a table, a hand, or the rain. These make up the first world, the material world (*ʿalam al-mulk*). Because our senses witness it, it is also called the witnessed world. The other two worlds are called unseen.

The imagination holds the second kind of thing. When we picture a friend's face, the picture has a shape and a colour, yet it weighs nothing and is made of nothing material. A dream does the same more vividly. Creation holds things of this kind on a larger scale, in the world of images (*ʿalam al-mithal*). Like the picture in our mind, that world has shape, size, and colour, and no matter at all. The difference is that the world of images is not inside anyone's mind. It is part of God's creation, and it exists whether or not anyone imagines it.

The intellect holds the third kind of thing: meanings, with no shape and no matter. Every person we have met has a particular height, voice, and face. Yet the mind can grasp "a human being" with none of these features. No such featureless person exists anywhere outside the mind. Creation holds pure meanings of this kind in the world of intellects (*ʿalam al-ʿuqul*). The angels who live there are pure intellect, attached to nothing material.

Creation therefore has three worlds that the soul mirrors, one for each of its ways of knowing. The world of images stands between the other two as a middle world. That is why it is also called *barzakh*, "a barrier". Pure intellect and matter cannot meet directly. Whatever is in the world of intellects is reflected first in the world of images, which has the shape and colour of matter without its weight. Only then does it reach the world of matter. Above all three, the lectures place higher realms that encompass them: the Footstool (*kursi*), the Throne (*ʿarsh*), and the world of *jabarut*.

These three worlds are not places we travel to. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the world of images "is another dimension of the same world in which we are living", and so is the world of intellects. The two deeper worlds are unseen not because they are far away but because the ordinary eye does not perceive them without training. The unseen worlds surround this one, but in depth and not in distance: each is a deeper dimension of the world we are in now.

Imam ʿAli (as) described this world as lying inside the hereafter when a Christian churchman came to him with questions. One of them, as the lectures tell it, was whether the hereafter lies inside this world, or this world inside the hereafter. Imam ʿAli (as) answered that this world lies inside the hereafter: this world is limited, and the hereafter encompasses it. The lectures set the answer beside a verse that says hell, which belongs to the hereafter, surrounds the faithless (Q 9:49). If this world lies inside the hereafter, the hereafter is not somewhere else. It surrounds this world now, like an envelope.

The soul mirrors three worlds, then, one inside another, and together they make one reality. The senses meet matter, the imagination meets the world of images, and the intellect grasps pure meaning. Each world lies deeper than the one before, and none of them is far away.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: barzakh"}
A *barzakh* is a barrier, something that stands between two things and keeps each in its place. The Qur'an uses the word for the barrier God set between two seas that He merged, one sweet and one briny (Q 25:53). It also uses it of death. One of the deniers, when death comes, asks to be sent back so that he may act righteously, and he is refused. Before such people, the verse says, is a barrier until the day they are resurrected (Q 23:99–100). The ʿurafaʾ use the same word for the world of images, which stands between matter and pure intellect. Mohammad Ali Shomali notes that philosophers compare the body of that world with the bodies we see in dreams, and "call them 'Barzakh' or 'mithali'" [@self_knowledge, p. 76]. The tradition joins the two uses: the middle world is called barzakh because it stands between matter and intellect, and it is where the dead are until the resurrection.
:::

## A soul that crosses them

:::: {.point}
When the body's hold on the soul loosens, the soul passes into the middle world; at death it stays there.

1. The soul stands between the spirit God breathed into us and the body, so it has a share in every world.
2. For now the body holds its attention; the hold loosens at three times. *(Reader told: "sleep, death and watchfulness, one at a time.")*
    a. Sleep: the hold slackens and the soul touches the middle world, which is why a dream can show what has not yet happened.
    b. Death: the Qur'an sets it beside sleep, since God takes the soul in both (Q 39:42); the difference is degree.
    c. At death the soul lets go of the body completely and goes on in an image body it had all along.
    d. Watchfulness: awake, a person who turns their whole attention to God loosens the hold on purpose and can see into the middle world. *(Qadi box.)*
3. So each time the hold loosens, the soul reaches the middle world, and at death it stays there.
4. Therefore the dead have gone deeper into our world, not away from it (pays off the opening).
::::

The soul has a share in each of these worlds because it stands between the spirit God breathed into us and the body. The Qur'an says God breathed of His spirit into the first human being (Q 15:29). God is not made of parts, so the spirit is called His out of honour, as the Kaʿba is called His house. This spirit is the greatest of His creations. Pure spirit and matter cannot meet directly. The soul is the means by which that spirit reaches the body, as the world of images links intellect and matter. It therefore touches matter through the body and can enter the middle world.

For now, the body holds almost all of the soul's attention, so the soul lives in the material world and the deeper worlds pass unnoticed. That hold loosens at three times: in sleep, at death, and in watchfulness. Taking them one at a time shows where the soul goes when the hold loosens.

The first is sleep. When we sleep, the soul attends less to the body. With the senses at rest, it can touch the world of images. It does not leave the body completely; if it did, we would be dead. This explains how a dream can show more than memory holds. A person may dream of something that later happens, and memory cannot show the future. Time works differently in the middle world. The soul has seen there the counterpart of an event that has not yet happened here.

The second is death, which the Qur'an sets beside sleep. Surah al-Zumar names the two together.

::: {.ayah ref="39:42" words="1-10"}
Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those who have not died, in their sleep.
:::

In both, the verse says, God takes the soul. The difference is one of degree. Mohammad Ali Shomali explains that in sleep the connection with the body remains, "but to a lesser degree than waking" [@self_knowledge, p. 75].

At death the soul lets go of the body completely. Shomali writes that it then "belongs to another body which has the qualities of a material body like shape and size without the mass" [@self_knowledge, p. 76]. This second body is the image body (*badan mithali*). "The body is not carrying the soul but the soul is carrying the body," Sheikh Bahmanpour says. When the appointed time comes, the soul sets the body down. It does not go on without a body, because a soul cannot be without one. It goes on in the image body, which was with us all along, unseen in another dimension.

The third is watchfulness (*muraqaba*): keeping the heart's attention on God. It happens while we are awake. A person who turns their attention to God until they forget the body does on purpose what sleep does. They can then see into the middle world as one sees in a dream. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi said that the more he kept up his muraqaba, the more distinctly he saw into that world. Such moments come to the mystics as an opportunity from God and not as a power to use. Only the Imams (as), the lectures add, can pass between the worlds whenever they wish.

::: {.box-story title="Story: Qadi meets himself"}
*Kernel of the Kernels*, the record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures on the path, describes what may come to a traveller who keeps up muraqaba. Lights appear, at first like flashes of lightning, and "these lights belong to the class of entities of the realm of Barzakh". In the early stages the traveller may see his own soul in bodily form: "he might see himself standing before himself." Tabatabaʾi then recalls his own teacher, Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, who used to say: "One day emerging from the room into the veranda, I saw myself standing silent by my side. I looked very carefully at my face, and observed a mole on it. On returning to my room when I looked into the mirror, I saw that there was a mole on my face that I had not noticed until then" [@kernel, p. 17].
:::

Sleep, death, and watchfulness are three ways of loosening one hold. In sleep the hold slackens for a night, and in watchfulness a person loosens it on purpose. At death it lets go entirely. Each time, the soul reaches the world of images, and at death it stays there.

This changes the picture at the graveside. The dead are in barzakh, and barzakh is the world of images, the middle world the soul enters whenever the body's hold loosens. That world is not a distant room. It is a deeper dimension of the world we are living in, so the dead have gone deeper into our world, not away from it.

## Our deeds give the soul a form

:::: {.point}
Because the soul is not fixed like an angel, what we do shapes it, and it meets that shape in the middle world.

1. An angel has a fixed station and does not grow (Q 37:164); the soul has none, so it can be shaped. *(Moved from §2.)*
2. Repeated acts shape it, as the habit of lying shows.
3. In this world the shaping shows a little, in the face; the image body shows it fully: sin deforms it, piety makes it shine.
4. The state the soul leaves the body in fixes its form in the next world (Imam Khomeini).
5. The Qur'an points the same way: people come in groups by their true natures (Q 78:18); the faithful come with their light before them (Q 57:12).
6. Some rewards are the deeds themselves made visible. *(Story: Ibn ʿArabi and Surah Yasin.)*
7. Therefore the form we meet after death is being made now, by what we do.
::::

The soul's movement between worlds sets it apart from the angels of the world of intellects. Each angel has a fixed station that it cannot rise above. An angel does not change or grow, because it is made as it will always be. Surah al-Saffat gives their own words.

::: {.ayah ref="37:164"}
There is none among us but has a known place.
:::

The human soul has no known place of this kind. It can move and change, and no one knows how far it can go. The mystics say that this capacity is why the angels bowed down to Adam. A soul that is not fixed in advance can be shaped, and how far it goes depends on the life it lives.

What shapes such a soul is what it does, and above all what it does again and again. Shomali takes the example of lying. A child telling a first lie feels uneasy and later remorseful. With repetition "the soul becomes inured to the effects" [@self_knowledge, p. 20], until lying costs little effort. The act has become a habit, and the habit has become part of the soul.

Some of that shaping shows on the face. A face can show faith or its absence to those with eyes to see it. In this world, though, the change is slow and small, and it does not deform the body. The image body is far more sensitive. Sin deforms it and piety makes it shine.

The form the soul has when it leaves this world is the form it keeps in the next, as Imam Khomeini writes in his *Forty Hadith*. Beside the body that everyone can see, each person has "another form and a different face of him, which is heavenly and invisible, which is determined by the qualities of his soul and his inner nature" [@forty_hadith, p. 41]. The same page names the moment that settles it: "in whichever state one's soul departs from the body, it assumes a form suited to it in the next world also" [@forty_hadith, p. 41].

The Qur'an points the same way. Surah al-Nabaʾ describes people coming forth on the Last Day.

::: {.ayah ref="78:18"}
the day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will come in groups,
:::

Hadith reports say that the groups are formed "according to their true natures" [@self_knowledge, p. 21]. Surah al-Hadid describes how the faithful will appear.

::: {.ayah ref="57:12" words="1-9"}
The day you will see the faithful, men and women, with their light moving swiftly in front of them and on their right
:::

The lectures read this light as the shining of the pious themselves. The body of the Last Day, they note, is not the image body, yet piety makes both shine. On the Last Day that light goes ahead of the faithful to light the way. In both verses, people appear as what their souls have become.

If the light of the faithful is their own piety shining, then some rewards are the deeds themselves, made visible. Shomali states this as a general principle about reward and punishment: "Sometimes rewards or punishments are nothing other than the actions. They are just the realities of those actions made manifest in another universe" [@self_knowledge, p. 77]. A deed done here has a reality in the middle world, and Ibn ʿArabi once met such a reality face to face.

::: {.box-story title="Story: Ibn ʿArabi and Surah Yasin"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour tells a story that the great mystic Ibn ʿArabi told of his own childhood. At the age of twelve he fell seriously ill and went into a coma. It was so serious that everyone thought he had died. In that state he saw horrible-looking people who were trying to hurt him. Then a figure breathing out a wonderful perfume defended him and drove them away. The boy asked who he was. The figure answered that he was Surah Yasin, his protector. When the boy came round, he found his father at his bedside in tears. His father had just finished reciting Surah Yasin for his recovery, and the boy had not known. In this telling, the figure was the image the recitation had formed in barzakh, for anything we do here has an image there. A lie or a word of comfort seems to be only words, yet each takes a shape in barzakh, as real as the figure the boy saw.
:::

If some rewards are the deeds themselves, the form a soul meets after death is, in part, the work of its own deeds. Shomali puts it briefly: "our future is now present" [@self_knowledge, p. 77]. What we do now shapes the image body, and every prayer, lie, or word of comfort has its own image in barzakh.

## The self can change its state

:::: {.point}
The self that does the deeds can change its state, so what the soul becomes is, by God's grace, in our hands.

1. The Qur'an names three states of the self. *(Reader told: "from lowest to highest.")*
    a. Commanding to evil, when the body rules (Q 12:53); worry binds as much as pleasure.
    b. Blaming itself, a hopeful sign (Q 75:2).
    c. At peace, which self-blame kept up leads to (Q 89:27–28).
2. These are one self under three names, so changing our attitude changes its state.
3. Familiar acts make that change. *(Reader told: "two of them show how this works.")*
    a. Remembrance refreshes and revives the soul and gives the rest of the self at peace; the Qur'an's word for "rest" shares its root (Q 13:28).
    b. Fasting and the night prayer loosen the body's hold, as in §2, and that hold is what makes the self command to evil.
4. The change lasts, because the soul takes on habits quickly and what we make of it stays with us forever.
5. Therefore, by God's grace (the mercy of Q 12:53), what the soul becomes is in our hands.

Close: a short recap; "If the work on the soul is this inward, what keeps it from going astray?"
::::

The soul that does these deeds is what the Qur'an calls the self (*nafs*), and it is not fixed in one state. The Qur'an gives it three names, and the lectures read them as three states. Let's take them from lowest to highest, to see how the self can move.

The lowest is the self that commands to evil (*nafs al-ammara*). In it the bodily side of a person overcomes the spiritual side, and the soul goes after the body's pleasures. Surah Yusuf names this self together with the mercy that can lift it.

::: {.ayah ref="12:53" words="4-11"}
the [carnal] soul indeed prompts [men] to evil, except inasmuch as my Lord has mercy.
:::

The verse admits what this self does and names what overcomes it: the mercy of God. The pull of this self is not only towards pleasure. Worry binds the soul to the body as well: worry over whether our children will be poor, or whether we will have a job next year. Worry keeps the soul occupied with the body and stops it rising towards God.

After giving in, the self may remember what it did and ask why. This is the second state, the self that blames itself (*nafs al-lawwama*). Its self-blame is a hopeful sign, because it shows that we hold values that matter more to us than the pleasure we gave in to. Surah al-Qiyama swears by it.

::: {.ayah ref="75:2"}
And I swear by the self-critical soul!
:::

A self that keeps up its self-blame comes in time to the third state, the self at peace (*nafs al-mutmaʾinna*). Surah al-Fajr calls this self back to its Lord (Q 89:27–28). It relies on what belongs to the hereafter and not on this world. It is free of sin, of greed, and of a mind turned wholly towards the body.

These are not three selves. "We don't have different types of nufus," Sheikh Bahmanpour says, using the plural of nafs. The one self takes a different name according to its attitude. Because we can change the attitude, we can change the state, and with it the name.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: the layers of the soul, counted differently"}
Murtada Mutahhari writes that the ʿurafaʾ "have different words for the human soul", and he sets out four. When the soul is "dominated and ruled by desires and passions they call it nafs". When it bears divine knowledge it is the heart (*qalb*). When "the light of Divine love dawns within it", it is the spirit (*ruh*), and when it reaches witnessing it is the secret (*sirr*). Beyond these lie the hidden (*khafi*) and the most hidden (*akhfa*) [@light, p. 58]. The lectures order them differently, describing the sirr as known only to God and the khafi as hidden even from the angels. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, a Shia master, counts the states of the self differently too, with four where the lectures have three. In his *Inner Secrets of the Path* the self ascends through "commanding to evil", "reproachful", "inspired", and "at rest" [@inner_secrets, p. 192]. Imam Khomeini notes that the soul's levels are divided "sometimes into seven sections and sometimes into four", and sometimes into three or two [@forty_hadith, p. 32]. The lists count different things (names, states, and levels). Read together, they describe one soul seen in different ways. Chapter Eight takes nafs, qalb, and ruh as stages of the road.
:::

The attitude is changed by acts already familiar to us. Two of them show how this works: the remembrance of God, and fasting with the night prayer.

The first is the remembrance of God (*dhikr*), which the lectures describe as refreshing and reviving the soul. Surah al-Raʿd names what it gives.

::: {.ayah ref="13:28" words="7-11"}
Behold! The hearts find rest in Allah's remembrance!
:::

In Arabic, the verse's word for finding rest comes from the same root as *mutmaʾinna*, the name of the self at peace (Q 13:28; 89:27). The rest that remembrance brings is the rest of the third state.

The second is fasting, with the night prayer beside it. Both loosen the body's hold on the soul, as sleep and watchfulness do. It is that hold that makes the self command to evil. The soul manages the body, down to digesting a meal, so a fasting body asks less of it. In Ramadan God pours down His light everywhere. The soul of the one who fasts is less taken up with the body, and it takes in that light without knowing it. The night prayer (*salat al-layl*) likewise helps the soul free itself from the body and see the light.

A change made this way lasts, because the soul takes on habits quickly. Trained to worry about the body, it worries about the body. Trained to care for higher things, it comes to care for them and to see the light. In the lectures' image, the soul is like a fine elastic thing. It can be held in the palm of a hand or stretched to the size of the universe, and which of the two it becomes depends on what we do with it. It has been entrusted to us for a short time. Whatever we do with it, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, "would remain with us forever".

The soul is therefore deeper than it looks. It mirrors three worlds of creation, one inside another, and none of them is far away. When the body's hold loosens, the soul passes into the middle world, and at death it stays there. Its deeds give it a form that it will meet there. The self that does those deeds can move from commanding to blaming to peace. What the soul finally becomes has been entrusted to us. The drive to begin is itself God's grace, the mercy that Surah Yusuf named. The rest of the work is in our hands.

If the work on the soul is this inward, what keeps it from going astray?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
*Lantern of the Path* gives an intention for going to sleep. For one week, as you lie down each night, make it your own: "to lighten your burden on the angels and disengage the self from its appetites, and to examine yourself by your sleep" [@lantern, p. 24]. With it, the Lantern asks you to "be aware of the fact that you are incapable and weak. You have no power over any of your movements and stillnesses, except by the judgement and measure of Allah" [@lantern, p. 24]. Let sleep be your "last affair in this world", and remember Allah "with your heart and your tongue" before it comes [@lantern, p. 25].
:::
