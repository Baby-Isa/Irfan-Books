::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي إِنْ أَنَامَتْنِيَ ٱلْغَفْلَةُ عَنِ ٱلإِسْتِعْدَادِ لِلِقَائِكَ فَقَدْ نَبَّهَتْنِي ٱلْمَعْرِفَةُ بِكَرَمِ آلاَئِكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, if my disregard for preparations to meet You has put me to sleep, my knowledge of Your kind bounties has awakened me. [@light, p. 166; @duas_munajat_shabaniyah, §118–119]
:::

These words come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, the prayer of the month of Shaʿban. The one who prays admits that neglect has put them to sleep, unready to meet God, and that knowing His generosity has woken them. Sleep and the meeting with God come together again at a graveside. There we say that the dead are now in *barzakh* (the world between this life and the resurrection), and many of us picture a waiting room somewhere far away.

Sheikh Bahmanpour teaches otherwise: at death "we don't go anywhere". The dead are not far away, and the reason lies in what the soul is. The way to God runs through the soul, the one thing we know from the inside, so it matters how deep the soul goes. This chapter sets out the soul and its worlds: which worlds the soul lives in, how it moves between them, and how its deeds give it a form there. Together they show that what the soul finally becomes is in our hands.

::: {.words}
*ʿalam al-mulk*: the material world · *ʿalam al-mithal* or *barzakh*: the middle world of images · *ʿalam al-ʿuqul*: the world of pure intellects · *badan mithali*: the image body · *muraqaba*: watchfulness; keeping the heart's attention on God
:::

## One world at three depths

::: {.point}
The world has three depths, so the dead have gone deeper into it, not away.

1. The soul is a small copy of creation, so each of our three ways of knowing gives us a sample of one world.
2. The senses meet the material world; the other two worlds are unseen, not distant.
3. The imagination shows that shape and colour can exist without matter.
4. Our imagination is a sample of a world of images, which exists in its own right.
5. The intellect grasps meanings without image or matter, and its world is the world of intellects.
6. The world of images links the other two, so it is called barzakh, a barrier.
7. None of these worlds is a place; each is a deeper dimension of this one.
8. Therefore the dead have gone deeper into our world, not away from it.
:::

Let's start with a claim of the mystics: the human soul is the whole of creation in small, a compact copy of everything that exists. We know things in three ways: through the senses, the imagination, and the intellect. If the soul is a copy of creation, each of these should give us a sample of one world, and each world can be approached through something we already know.

The senses meet the first world, the material world (*ʿalam al-mulk*). It is made of matter: things with weight that take up space, such as a table, a hand, or the rain. Because our senses witness it, it is also called the witnessed world. The other two worlds are called unseen. They are unseen not because they are far away but because the ordinary eye does not perceive them without training.

The imagination gives us a sample of the second world. When we picture a friend's face, the picture has a shape and a colour, yet it weighs nothing and is made of nothing material. A dream does the same more vividly. People move and speak in it, with faces, sizes, and clothes of a certain colour, and none of it is matter.

Our imagination is a small sample of a larger world of this kind, the world of images (*ʿalam al-mithal*). Like the picture in our mind, it has shape, size, and colour, and no matter at all. The difference is that the picture in our mind belongs to us, whereas the world of images depends on no one's mind. It exists in its own right, and it is the world the dead enter.

The intellect gives us a sample of the third world. Every person we have met has a particular height, voice, and face. Yet the mind can grasp "a human being" with none of these features, though no such person exists anywhere outside it. This kind of understanding deals in meanings, with no image and no matter. Its world is the world of intellects (*ʿalam al-ʿuqul*), and the angels who live there are pure intellect, attached to nothing material.

The world of images stands between the other two, and this is why it is also called barzakh, which means a barrier. Pure intellect and matter have nothing in common, so they cannot meet directly. Whatever is in the world of intellects is reflected first in the world of images, which has the shape and colour of matter without its weight. Only then does it reach the world of matter.

None of these worlds is a place we travel to. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the world of images "is another dimension of the same world in which we are living", and so is the world of intellects. The unseen worlds surround this one as an envelope surrounds a letter. The surrounding is in depth and not in distance, since each is a deeper dimension of the world we are in now.

This changes the picture at the graveside. The dead have not been taken to a distant room. They have passed into a deeper dimension of the world we are living in. Even the resurrection is not a journey elsewhere but a move deeper still, closer to the majesty and beauty of God.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: barzakh"}
A *barzakh* is a barrier, something that stands between two things and keeps each in its place. The Qur'an uses the word for the barrier God set between two seas that He merged, one sweet and one briny (Q 25:53). It also uses it of death. When death comes, a person asks to be taken back so that he may act righteously in what he left behind. He is told that a barzakh stands before him until the day he is raised (Q 23:99–100). The ʿurafaʾ use the same word for the world of images, which stands between matter and pure intellect. Mohammad Ali Shomali notes that philosophers compare the body of that world with the bodies we see in dreams, and "call them 'Barzakh' or 'mithali'" [@self_knowledge, p. 76]. The two uses meet: the barrier between this life and the resurrection is the middle world the dead now live in.
:::

## A soul that crosses them

::: {.point}
The soul moves between the worlds, as far as the life it lives takes it.

1. The soul was made to stand between spirit and clay, so it has a share in every world.
2. For now the body holds the soul's attention, and that hold loosens at three times.
3. In sleep the soul touches the world of images, which is why a dream can show what has not yet happened.
4. Sleep is death's brother: the Qur'an says God takes the soul in both.
5. The difference is one of degree: at death the soul lets go of the body completely.
6. At death the soul sets the body down and goes on in the image body it already had.
7. Awake, a person can loosen the hold by turning their attention to God.
8. The angels each have a fixed station.
9. Therefore the soul, which has none, goes as far as the life it lives takes it.
:::

The soul has a share in each of these worlds, because it was made to stand between them. The Qur'an says that God breathed of His spirit into the first human being (Q 15:29). Pure spirit and a body of clay have nothing in common, so something that reaches both had to stand between them, as the world of images stands between pure intellect and matter. That something is the soul. Each of us has an existence here and a counterpart in the world of images, far larger than the self that walks about here.

For now, the body holds almost all of the soul's attention, so the soul lives in the material world. That hold loosens at three times: in sleep, at death, and in contemplation.

The first is sleep. When we sleep, the soul attends less to the body. With the senses no longer crowding out its other perceptions, it can touch the world of images. It does not leave the body completely; if it did, we would be dead. This explains a dream that is more than memory. A person may dream of someone doing something that later happens, and a memory cannot show the future. Time works differently in the middle world, and the soul has seen there the counterpart of an event that has not yet happened here.

Sleep and death are close kin. *Lantern of the Path*, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), calls sleep "the brother of death" [@lantern, p. 24], and Surah al-Zumar says that God takes the soul in both.

::: {.ayah ref="39:42" words="1-10"}
Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those who have not died, in their sleep.
:::

The difference between the two is one of degree. Mohammad Ali Shomali explains that in sleep the spirit is still connected with the body, "but to a lesser degree than waking". At death it is disconnected from the body and "belongs to another body which has the qualities of a material body like shape and size without the mass" [@self_knowledge, pp. 75–76]. This second body is the image body (*badan mithali*). It has our shape, but it is not made of any material elements.

At death the soul is not slipping out of a body that held it. "The body is not carrying the soul but the soul is carrying the body," Sheikh Bahmanpour says. When the appointed time comes, the soul sets the body down and goes. It does not go without a body, because a soul cannot be without one. The image body was with us all along, unseen in another dimension, and the soul goes on in it.

The third loosening can happen while we are awake. A person who turns their attention to God until they become unaware of the body does what sleep does, and can see in the middle world as one sees in a dream. This is the work of watchfulness (*muraqaba*): keeping the heart's attention on God. Nothing leaves the body; what turns is the attention. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi said that the more he kept up his muraqaba, the more distinctly he saw. Such moments come to the mystics as an opportunity from God and not as a power to use, whereas the Imams (as) can pass between the worlds at will.

::: {.box-story title="Story: Qadi meets himself"}
*Kernel of the Kernels*, the record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures on the path, describes what may come to a traveller who keeps up muraqaba. Lights appear, at first like flashes of lightning, and "these lights belong to the class of entities of the realm of Barzakh". In the early stages the traveller may see his own soul in bodily form: "he might see himself standing before himself." The lectures then tell of their teacher, Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, who used to say: "One day emerging from the room into the veranda, I saw myself standing silent by my side. I looked very carefully at my face, and observed a mole on it. On returning to my room when I looked into the mirror, I saw that there was a mole on my face that I had not noticed until then" [@kernel, p. 17].
:::

Sleep, death, and contemplation show a soul that moves between worlds. The angels have no such movement. Each has a fixed station that it cannot rise above, and it does not change or grow, because it is made as it will always be. Surah al-Saffat gives their own words.

::: {.ayah ref="37:164"}
There is none among us but has a known place.
:::

The human soul has no known place of this kind. It can move and change, and no one knows how far it can go; the mystics say that this capacity is why the angels bowed down to Adam. How far a soul travels is not settled at its creation, as an angel's station is. It depends on the life the soul lives.

## Our deeds give the soul a form

::: {.point}
What we do now shapes the image body we will meet after death.

1. Repeated acts shape the self, as the habit of lying shows.
2. The image body shows that shaping far more than the face does: sin deforms it, and piety makes it shine.
3. Imam Khomeini: the soul's state when it leaves the body fixes the form it takes in the next world.
4. On the Last Day people will appear as their true natures, some below animals and some above angels.
5. The light of the faithful is their own: a reward can be the deed itself made visible.
6. Therefore the form we meet after death is our own deeds, and it is being made today.
:::

If every soul passes into the middle world in any case, it can seem that what we do here changes little. Habit shows otherwise, because repeated acts shape the self. Shomali takes the example of lying. A child telling a first lie feels uneasy and later remorseful, but "as one repeats this behaviour though, the soul becomes inured to the effects", until lying costs little effort [@self_knowledge, p. 20].

Some of that shaping shows on the face. A face can show faith or its absence to those with eyes to see it, and a pious person's face can carry light. In this world the change is slow and small, and it never deforms the body. The image body is far more sensitive. Sin deforms it and piety makes it shine, so that it takes the shape of what our habits have made of the soul.

Imam Khomeini describes this second form in his *Forty Hadith*. Beside the body that everyone can see, each person has "another form and a different face of him, which is heavenly and invisible, which is determined by the qualities of his soul and his inner nature" [@forty_hadith, p. 41]. The soul's state when it leaves the body fixes that form: "in whichever state one's soul departs from the body, it assumes a form suited to it in the next world also" [@forty_hadith, p. 41]. Surah al-Nabaʾ describes people coming forth on the Last Day.

::: {.ayah ref="78:18"}
the day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will come in groups,
:::

The hadith, as Shomali reports them, say that the groups are formed "according to their true natures". On that day, "when the veil is finally lifted from before our eyes, we will see ourselves and others as they really are". Some "could have fallen lower than a bug, while others could have ascended higher than angels" [@self_knowledge, p. 21]. A soul with no fixed station can sink below every animal as well as rise above the angels. Surah al-Hadid describes those who rise.

::: {.ayah ref="57:12" words="1-9"}
The day you will see the faithful, men and women, with their light moving swiftly in front of them and on their right
:::

The light the faithful carry goes ahead of them and lights their way, and it is their own. Shomali writes of reward and punishment in the same terms. "Sometimes rewards or punishments are nothing other than the actions," he writes. "They are just the realities of those actions made manifest in another universe" [@self_knowledge, p. 77].

If a reward can be the deed itself made visible, the form a soul meets after death is largely its own deeds. In Shomali's words, "our future is now present" [@self_knowledge, p. 77]. The image body is being shaped today by what we do today.

## The self can change its state

::: {.point}
The one self can rise from its lowest state to peace, so what it becomes is in our hands.

1. The lowest state of the self is the one that commands to evil, when the body rules the spirit.
2. Worry ties the soul to the body as much as pleasure does.
3. The self that blames itself after giving in is a hopeful sign.
4. Self-blame, kept up, brings the self to peace.
5. These are one self under three names, so changing our attitude changes its state.
6. The first act that moves the self is the remembrance of God.
7. The rest it gives is the peace of the third state.
8. Fasting and the night prayer loosen the soul's tie to the body.
9. These acts work because the soul takes on habits quickly, and what we make of it stays with us forever.
10. Therefore what the soul finally becomes is in our hands.
:::

If deeds give the soul its form, the self that does the deeds is not fixed either. The Qur'an names the self (*nafs*) in three states. The lowest is the self that commands to evil (*al-nafs al-ammara*). In it the bodily side of a person overcomes the spiritual side, and the soul goes after the body's pleasures. Surah Yusuf names this self together with the mercy that can lift it.

::: {.ayah ref="12:53" words="4-11"}
the [carnal] soul indeed prompts [men] to evil, except inasmuch as my Lord has mercy.
:::

The body's pull is not only towards pleasure. Worry over whether our children will be poor, or whether we will have a good job next year, also binds the soul to the body. It keeps the soul occupied with the body and stops it rising towards God.

After giving in, the self may remember what it did and ask why. This is the second state, the self that blames itself (*al-nafs al-lawwama*). Its self-blame is a hopeful sign, because it shows that we hold other values that matter more to us than the pleasure we gave in to. Surah al-Qiyama swears by it.

::: {.ayah ref="75:2"}
And I swear by the self-critical soul!
:::

A self that keeps up this self-blame comes in time to the third state, the self at peace (*al-nafs al-mutmaʾinna*), which Surah al-Fajr calls back to its Lord (Q 89:27). It relies on what belongs to the other world and not on this one. It is free of sin, of greed, and of a mind turned wholly towards the body.

These are not three selves. "We don't have different types of nufus," Sheikh Bahmanpour says, using the plural of nafs. The one self takes a different name according to its attitude, and because we can change the attitude, we can change the name. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, a Shia master of an earlier age, counts four states where the lectures count three. In his *Inner Secrets of the Path*, the self ascends from "commanding to evil" to the "reproachful", from there to the "inspired", and finally to the station where it is "at rest" [@inner_secrets, p. 192]. Both counts describe one self climbing.

::: {.box-word title="Word Study: the layers of the soul, counted differently"}
The ʿurafaʾ give the one soul several names, one for each depth it reaches. Murtada Mutahhari sets out four. When the soul is "dominated and ruled by desires and passions they call it nafs". When it bears divine knowledge it is the heart (*qalb*). When "the light of Divine love dawns within it", it is the spirit (*ruh*), and when it reaches witnessing it is the mystery (*sirr*). Beyond these lie the hidden (*khafi*) and the most hidden (*akhfa*) [@light, p. 58]. The lectures order them differently, describing the sirr as known only to God and the khafi as hidden even from the angels. Imam Khomeini notes that the soul's levels are divided "sometimes into seven sections and sometimes into four", and sometimes into three or two [@forty_hadith, p. 32]. However they are counted, the names describe one soul at different depths. Chapter Eight takes nafs, qalb, and ruh as stages of the road.
:::

What moves the self from one state to the next are acts already familiar to us. The first is the remembrance of God (*dhikr*), and Surah al-Raʿd names what it gives.

::: {.ayah ref="13:28" words="7-11"}
Behold! The hearts find rest in Allah's remembrance!
:::

The rest this verse promises is the peace of the third state, and only remembrance gives it. Remembrance refreshes and revives the soul. Real meditation, on this teaching, is reflection inside the soul carried by dhikr, whereas sitting still and thinking of nothing stays shallow.

Fasting and the night prayer work on the same tie to the body that sleep and watchfulness loosen. The soul manages the body, down to the digestion of a meal, so a body that is not eating asks less of it. In Ramadan God pours down His light everywhere. The soul of the one who fasts, less taken up with the body, takes in that light without knowing it. The night prayer (*salat al-layl*) likewise helps the soul free itself from the body and see the light.

These acts work because the soul takes on habits quickly. Trained to worry about the body, it worries about the body; trained to care for higher things, it comes to care for them and to see the light. In the lectures' image, the soul is like a fine elastic thing that can be held in the palm of a hand or stretched to the size of the universe. It has been entrusted to us for a short time, and whatever we do with it, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, "would remain with us forever".

The soul is therefore deeper than it looks. It lives in the material world and in the world of images, and it reaches towards the world of intellects. It passes into the middle world a little each night in sleep, and fully at death. Its deeds give it a form that it will meet there, and the self that does them can move from commanding, to blaming, to peace. What the soul finally becomes has been entrusted to us, and it is in our hands.

If the work on the soul is this inward, what keeps it from going astray?

*Lantern of the Path* gives an intention for going to sleep. For one week, as you lie down each night, make it your own: "to lighten your burden on the angels and disengage the self from its appetites, and to examine yourself by your sleep" [@lantern, p. 24]. With it, the Lantern asks you to "be aware of the fact that you are incapable and weak" [@lantern, p. 24]. Let sleep be your "last affair in this world", and remember Allah "with your heart and your tongue" before it comes [@lantern, p. 25].
:::
