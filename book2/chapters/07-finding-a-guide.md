The Mafatih recommends reading Surah al-Kahf on Fridays [@mafatih, pp. 84–85]. Halfway through it, the Prophet Musa (as) sets out to find a servant of God whom, God says, He had taught "a knowledge from Our own" (Q 18:65). This servant is known as Khidr [@forty_hadith, p. 508]. Musa is ready to keep travelling until he reaches the meeting place of the two seas, or has spent a long time (Q 18:60). When he finds the servant, he makes one request.

::: {.ayah ref="18:66"}
Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you for the purpose that you teach me some of the probity you have been taught?’
:::

In this verse a prophet to whom God spoke directly (Q 4:164) asks to be taught. His long search shows how much time and effort a seeker must be ready to give. Travellers on the road to God can look for the same kind of help. The law marks the boundary of that road, and the next question is who walks beside the traveller on it. This chapter sets out why finding a guide helps the traveller, and how to know a true guide once found.

::: {.words}
*shaykh*: a spiritual master · *murid*: a disciple · *silsila*: a chain of masters traced back to the Prophet ﷺ · *wali* (plural *awliyaʾ*): a friend of God · *wusul*: arrival at the goal of the road
:::

## Why a traveller needs a companion

:::: {.point}
A traveller needs a companion who has walked the road, wherever scholars place the need.

1. On the road the soul passes into states the traveller does not understand, and without knowing what they mean he can lose the way.
2. Someone who has passed through those states can say what they mean and what to do next, so a guide must have travelled the road, not only studied it.
3. Scholars differ on how early this need begins. *(Reader told: "three accounts, taken in turn, show the range".)*
    a. The Sheikh: the first steps are the law; the guide who reads the soul's states comes after them.
    b. Before then a beginner needs only a teacher who says which practices to begin with, as Qadi set the Musabbihat for a year. *(Story box: ʿUnwan al-Basri; the Imam begins him on rules to start with.)*
    c. Tabatabaʾi: the general teacher is needed at the beginning of the road.
    d. Mutahhari: guidance is needed at every stage, from a guide the ʿurafaʾ call Khidr.
4. Therefore: the accounts differ on when, not on what the guide is for, and only someone who has travelled the road can do it.
::::

A guide helps because the road has turns that a traveller cannot read alone, and those turns are in the soul. On the journey the soul can pass into strange states. There may come a time when a traveller hates worship, or cannot bear to live among people. Such states arrive without explanation. A traveller who does not know what they mean, or what to do next, can lose the way.

Someone who has already passed through these states can explain them. The traveller describes the state and is told what it means and what to do next. The advice can be as exact as whether *Allahu akbar* or *la ilaha illa Allah* is the remembrance that suits that stage. This is why a guide must have travelled the road himself, and not only studied it.

How early in the journey this need begins is where scholars differ, and three accounts, taken in turn, show the range. Many who want to set out look first for a teacher and, finding none, conclude that they cannot begin. Sheikh Bahmanpour does not tell a beginner to find a guide first. In his account the first steps are the law: "First you have to comply completely with whatever Sharia tells." The guide who can read the soul's states is found after those steps, not before. The Sufi orders tell a beginner that anything done without their instruction will leave him lost, which he calls "something of an exaggeration".

What a beginner does need is narrower: a teacher who says which of the practices handed down in the hadith matter most. The Musabbihat are the five surahs that open with God's glorification, recited by the Prophet ﷺ every night before sleeping. Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, the teacher of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, had beginners recite them every night before sleep for a whole year, and only then gave them a second instruction. <!-- src: L07 ¶14-16 -->

::: {.box-story title="Story: ʿUnwan al-Basri at the door of Imam al-Sadiq"}
ʿUnwan al-Basri was an old man, near the end of his life, when he asked Imam al-Sadiq (as) to teach him. Sheikh Bahmanpour tells the hadith. The sixth Imam at first turned him away. He was busy, he said, with words of remembrance he recited at every hour of the day, and ʿUnwan should go and learn from Malik ibn Anas and the other scholars of Medina. The lectures explain that ʿUnwan was not yet at a level where the Imam could teach him. ʿUnwan did not give up. He prayed that God would soften the heart of Imam al-Sadiq (as) towards him, so that he could learn something. When he went back, the Imam received him warmly and began to advise him. Among the first counsels were rules for eating: do not eat unless you are hungry, and when you do eat, eat only what is lawful and begin in the name of God. The teacher ʿUnwan had persisted in seeking began by giving him rules to start with. <!-- src: L07 ¶18-19; L08 ¶77-79 -->
:::

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi places the need for a teacher at the beginning of the road. In the lectures recorded in *Kernel of the Kernels*, he distinguishes a general teacher, consulted as one consults anyone learned, from the teachers God has appointed. Of the general teacher he says: "The necessity of referring to the general teacher is only at the beginning of wayfaring" [@kernel, p. 80; @light, p. 100]. Once the traveller reaches the higher stations, that teacher's company is no longer needed.

Murtada Mutahhari describes the practice of ʿirfan as guided at every stage. Each stage is passed under the guidance of "a mature and perfect example of humanity who, having traveled this path, is aware of the manners and ways of each station". Without such a guide, the traveller "is in danger of going astray". The ʿurafaʾ, he reports, call this guide Khidr, and he quotes a couplet: "Leave not this stage without the company of Khidr, / There is darkness ahead; be afraid of losing the way" [@light, p. 10].

The three accounts place the need at different points, but they do not change what the guide is for: to read states the traveller has not yet passed through. Only someone who has travelled the road can do that, like the Khidr of the couplet, whose company keeps the traveller from losing the way.

## Under the Imams, not in their place

:::: {.point}
A guide stands under the Imams, never in their place, and a traveller submits to him only if he meets a high bar.

1. The Sufi orders place authority in a chain of masters who pass on the Prophet's inner knowledge.
2. For Shia that knowledge passed by inheritance to the Imams, so there is no room for such a chain of authority.
3. So where an order asks for total submission to its master, a Shia traveller looks for an instructor he can trust.
4. Tabatabaʾi's two kinds of teacher show where such an instructor stands. *(Reader told: "the general and the special".)*
    a. The instructor is a general teacher, consulted as one consults anyone knowledgeable (Q 16:43).
    b. He is submitted to only if he has reached a high rank; anyone knowledgeable may be consulted.
    c. The special teachers, the Prophet and the Imams, are the company the traveller never leaves.
5. Therefore: a guide stands under the Imams, never in their place, and is followed only if he meets that bar.
::::

If a guide is needed, the next question is what authority he holds. The Sufi orders give one answer. Each order calls itself a path (*tariqa*) and is built round an elder with his own method of instruction, and each has a chain of masters (*silsila*) that it traces back to the Prophet ﷺ. Through that chain, the orders hold, the Prophet's inner knowledge passed from the heart of each master to the next.

Shia agree that the Prophet's inner knowledge passed on, but hold that it passed by inheritance to the Imams (as). Imam ʿAli (as) received it as the Prophet's heir, not as one learned companion among others. The orders keep the idea of someone who receives that knowledge, Sheikh Bahmanpour observes. Their chains go back to Imam ʿAli (as), some of them through Imam al-Rida (as) [@kernel, pp. 87–88]. Yet they do not hold, as Shia do, that the knowledge passed by inheritance to the Imams. For Shia the Imams hold that place, and so, in his words, "We don't have such a thing as tariqa in Shia."

The orders also ask for total submission. In an order, a disciple (*murid*) submits to the master (*shaykh*) completely, whatever he says and whatever he does. As a rule, Shia travellers do not join an order. They find for themselves an instructor who comes from no order and holds no special licence to instruct. What they look for is a person they can trust, someone to whom "you could entrust your soul".

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's two kinds of teacher, the general and the special, show where such an instructor stands. The instructor the Sheikh describes fits the first, the general teacher. Such a teacher has not been specially appointed to guide anyone. Consulting him is like consulting anyone knowledgeable, as a verse of Surah al-Nahl tells believers to do [@kernel, p. 80; @light, p. 100].

::: {.ayah ref="16:43" words="9-"}
Ask the People of the Reminder if you do not know.
:::

The verse is general, but Tabatabaʾi sets a high bar for anyone a traveller submits to. A man is not a teacher "so long as the revelations of Divine Essence … are not manifested in him", and the revelations of God's Attributes and Names are not enough [@kernel, p. 82; @light, p. 101]. Short of that rank, "one may not surrender oneself to everyone and anyone and submit and obey him" [@kernel, p. 83; @light, p. 102]. Anyone knowledgeable may be consulted, but only a teacher who has reached that rank may be submitted to.

The special teacher is different. He is one of those "specially designated to guide, and they are the Apostle of Allah and his true successors": the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as) [@kernel, p. 80]. "The wayfarer cannot dispense with the company of the special teacher in any circumstance whatsoever, even if he has reached his sought homeland" [@kernel, p. 80]. The company meant is inner, and not "merely their outward company and comradeship". It lasts through every stage of the road and after its end [@kernel, pp. 80–81; @light, p. 100].

A guide therefore walks beside the traveller under the Imams (as), and never in their place. A traveller submits to him only if he has reached the rank Tabatabaʾi describes, whereas the company that never ends is that of the Prophet and the Imams. Chapter Fifteen returns to the Sufi orders and how the Shia path differs from them.

## Teachers of recent generations

:::: {.point}
Shia can name guides of this kind in recent generations, in a line that belonged to no Sufi order.

1. If Shia travellers find their own instructor, there must be instructors to find: Tabatabaʾi named his, Qadi.
2. Qadi's line runs back to Akhund Hamadani, a pupil of a great jurist, whose pupils included Imam Khomeini's first teacher in gnosis.
3. These teachers were scholars, several of them jurists, and their line belonged to no Sufi order.
4. Tabatabaʾi calls it a chain and a tariqah, but beside §2 the words name a line of teachers, not a chain of authority.
5. They taught the road this book describes, to God through the self.
6. Therefore: the guide §2 described, under the Imams and in no order, is one Shia can name in recent generations. *(Story box: the weaver at the judge's door, how Akhund Hamadani was trained.)*
::::

If Shia travellers find an instructor for themselves, there must be instructors to find. The tradition can name teachers who walked this road in recent generations. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi named his own teacher: "My preceptor was the late Haji Mirza Ali Agha Qazi" [@light, p. 106]. This is the same Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi who set beginners a year of the Musabbihat. Under him Tabatabaʾi travelled the path [@kernel, p. 8].

Qadi's line runs back two generations, to Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani. He was a different scholar from Mulla ʿAbd al-Samad Hamadani, who saw the law, the path, and the truth in one prayer. Akhund Hamadani had been one of the outstanding pupils of the jurist Shaykh Murtada Ansari before he became a guide [@light, p. 106; @kernel, p. 89]. He trained several pupils who became guides in turn, among them Qadi's teacher and Imam Khomeini's first formal teacher in gnosis and ethics [@light, p. 106; @forty_hadith, p. 8]. The box below tells how Akhund Hamadani was himself trained.

Akhund Hamadani and the teachers of Tabatabaʾi's line were scholars, several of them jurists. Of the many Sufi orders, Tabatabaʾi grants that two or three are Shia [@kernel, pp. 87–88]. His own line belonged to none of them: "we belong to none of these orders and follow the directions of the late Akhund, who had nothing to do with these orders" [@light, p. 105].

Tabatabaʾi still gives his line the names the orders use. He calls it "the chain of my preceptors" [@light, p. 106]. In *Kernel of the Kernels* he uses the word the Sheikh says Shia lack: "our tariqah is that of the marhum Akhund which does not end in any of these silsilahs" [@kernel, p. 88]. Set beside the Sheikh's point, the words name a line of teachers here, not a chain of authority beside the Imams.

These teachers taught the road this book describes, which runs to God through the self. Akhund Hamadani taught concentration on the self, and all his disciples followed "the method of gnosis (ma’rifah) of the soul, which is essential to the gnosis of the Lord" [@kernel, p. 87]. Qadi taught the same way. He asked a traveller to set a time each day or night to attend to the self, "for half an hour or a little more" [@light, p. 107]. The guide the last section described, standing under the Imams and in no order, is therefore one Shia can name in recent generations.

::: {.box-story title="Story: The weaver at the judge's door"}
More than a hundred years before ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi told this story, Sayyid ʿAli Shushtari was a leading scholar and judge in the city of Shushtar. One day a weaver knocked at his door and told him that a ruling he had given on a property was wrong. The property belonged to an orphan, the weaver said, and its deed was buried in a certain place. Shushtari found the deed where the weaver had said, and found that the witnesses had lied. The weaver returned the next night and the night after with the same warning. On that third night he told Shushtari to sell his belongings, go to Najaf, and wait for him in the Wadi al-Salam after six months. Shushtari went, followed the weaver's instructions, and "reached a position too high to be described". In Najaf he attended Shaykh Murtada Ansari's lectures on jurisprudence and took over Ansari's teaching when Ansari died. Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani, one of Ansari's pupils, had long attended Shushtari's lessons in ethics and gnosis. In the last six months of his own life, Shushtari persuaded Hamadani to take up his way [@light, pp. 105–106; @kernel, pp. 88–89]. Tabatabaʾi traces his own line of teachers back to the weaver through Shushtari, and adds that "it is not known who that weaver was" [@light, p. 106].
:::

## How to test a guide

:::: {.point}
A guide who breaks the law is ruled out; a true one is shown by long company and recognised by where he turns people, never by wonders.

1. A line of true teachers does not make every claimant true, so a guide must be tested: has he walked the road? His learning cannot show it.
2. Nor can wonders, which come early on the road, far from its goal.
3. The law can rule a guide out: anyone who works wonders while abandoning one command of God is lying.
4. Keeping the law does not show how far a guide has travelled; only long company shows that. *(Who's Who box: Maliki Tabrizi, fourteen years with his teacher.)*
5. Where long company is impossible, his teaching is weighed against the Qur'an, the Sunna and the practice of the Imams.
6. Either way care is needed, because pretenders turn people towards themselves; ʿirfan is for God alone (Ch 1), so a true guide turns them towards God.
7. The Prophet ʿIsa (as) gives this test in its simplest form: sit with one whose sight reminds you of God.
8. His three marks all ask where a person's company turns us, and a traveller can watch for them over time.
9. Therefore (section and chapter): finding a guide helps if he is tested; he stands under the Imams; wonders prove nothing; breaking the law rules him out; long company shows how far he has travelled; where he turns people shows whether he is true.

Close: "Once a guide is found, what does the road itself look like?"
::::

A line of true teachers does not make every teacher who claims a place in it true, so a guide must be tested. The question is whether he has walked the road himself. His learning cannot answer it. A teacher's writing or study does not show whether he has made the journey.

Wonders cannot answer it either. Tabatabaʾi's lectures list what cannot show that a person has reached the goal of the road (*wusul*): "extraordinary feats, knowledge of hidden things, of private thoughts of persons, passing over fire and water", and knowledge of the past and the future [@kernel, p. 81]. Such powers become possible early on the road, at "the beginning of spiritual vision" [@light, p. 101]. Yet "there is a very great distance between this point and the frontiers of wusul and perfection" [@kernel, p. 81]. A wonder may come from someone who sees what others do not, but it does not make him a friend of God (*wali*). Such feats are "not a sign of anybody's being a favorite of Allah" [@light, p. 101].

Where wonders prove nothing, the law gives a firm test. Najm al-Din Kubra, the master of one of the Sufi orders, gave a rule for the road that applies here. Someone may be seen flying in the air or walking on the sea, but if they have abandoned "even one injunction of God", then "you should know that they are liars". Their wonders may be real, but their claim to know God is false. <!-- src: L06 ¶18-20 -->

Keeping the law rules a false guide out, but only time shows how far a true one has travelled. In Tabatabaʾi's lectures, the general teacher "is not recognized except through company and being with him in public and private" [@kernel, p. 81]. A traveller must watch him closely "for a considerable time" [@light, p. 101], as anyone comes to know a person by living alongside him. One of Akhund Hamadani's pupils, whose story is in the box below, lived alongside his teacher for fourteen years.

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi"}
Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi, "The most distinguished jurisprudent and perfect mystic" in his biographer's words, studied jurisprudence in Najaf under the great jurists of his day. There he came to know Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani, and he spent fourteen years with him, learning ethics and mysticism. He became a guide in his turn: the jurist Shaykh Muhammad Husayn Isfahani, himself an authority in learning and deeds, wrote asking for his instructions in ethics and mysticism. Maliki Tabrizi later settled in Qum, where he taught jurisprudence and ethics and wrote [@journey, p. 8]. He died in 1925, and three years later his ethics class was resumed by Mirza Muhammad ʿAli Shahabadi, the teacher of Imam Khomeini. When Shahabadi left for Tehran in 1936, he gave the class to Imam Khomeini [@forty_hadith, p. 8].
:::

Not every traveller can keep long company with a teacher before trusting him, and there is a test for that case too. Where it is hard to inquire into a teacher's state, the traveller should trust in God and weigh whatever the teacher says or prescribes "in the light of the Book of God and the Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah and the practice of the Immaculate Imams" [@kernel, p. 83]. Only what agrees with them is put into practice [@light, p. 102].

Care is needed either way, because the road has many pretenders, who put their wares on display with claims to unveiling and inner sight [@kernel, p. 83]. Imam Khomeini names what they do. Those who style themselves guides of God's creatures and leaders of mystic orders "have distracted the attention of the creatures of God from Him, attracting them towards themselves" [@forty_hadith, p. 102]. ʿIrfan is sought for God alone, so the test of a guide is whether he turns people towards God or towards himself.

The simplest form of the test comes in a saying of the Prophet ʿIsa (as). It is recorded in *Lantern of the Path*, the book attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as). They asked ʿIsa with whom they should sit.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the saying of ʿIsa in Misbah al-Shariʿa (Lantern of the Path, p. 13)]" source="Lantern of the Path"}
With one the sight of whom reminds you of Allah, … and whose speech increases you in knowledge, and whose actions make you desire the next world. [@lantern, p. 13]
:::

ʿIsa's three marks all ask where a person's company turns us: towards Allah, towards knowledge, and towards the next world. They are marks a traveller can watch for over time, in the company he keeps.

Finding a guide therefore helps the traveller, as long as the guide is tested. A true guide has walked the road himself and walks beside the traveller under the Imams (as), never in their place. A guide's wonders prove nothing, and one who breaks the law is ruled out. Long company shows how far he has travelled, and the direction he turns people shows whether he is true.

Once a guide is found, what does the road itself look like?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
This week, notice the people whose company you keep: at the mosque, at a majlis, at work, and at home. Of each, ask the three questions in ʿIsa's answer. Does the sight of them remind you of Allah? Does their speech increase you in knowledge? Do their actions make you desire the next world [@lantern, p. 13]? Where you find someone of whom all three are true, seek out their company.
:::
