On Fridays many of us read Surah al-Kahf. Halfway through it, the Prophet Musa (as) sets out on a journey, telling the young man with him that he will keep going until he reaches the meeting place of the two seas, however long it takes (Q 18:60). There he finds a servant of God who has been taught knowledge from God's own presence (Q 18:65), the servant known as Khidr [@forty_hadith, p. 508]. Musa makes him one request.

::: {.ayah ref="18:66"}
Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you for the purpose that you teach me some of the probity you have been taught?’
:::

A prophet to whom God spoke asked to follow a teacher, and had been ready to travel for a long time to find him. Chapter Six argued that the shariʿa marks the boundary of the road from the first step to the last, and it ended by asking who walks beside the traveller. This chapter's answer is a guide: one who helps, and who must be tested.

::: {.words}
*shaykh*: a spiritual instructor · *murid*: a disciple · *silsila*: a chain of masters traced back to the Prophet ﷺ · *wali* (plural *awliyaʾ*): a friend of God · *wusul*: arrival at the goal of the road
:::

## Why a companion

::: {.point}
On the road the soul passes through states that a traveller cannot read alone, and someone who has walked the road can say what they mean. Scholars weigh how early that need begins: Sheikh Bahmanpour starts with the law and brings in the teacher later, while Murtada Mutahhari wants a guide at every stage.
:::

Let's start with what a guide is for. Chapter Two described the knowledge of God as a light that God places in a purified heart, and noted that this light can pass from one heart to another. Receiving it straight from God is possible but difficult, so most travellers receive it through someone who has received it already. The reason is that the road has turns a traveller cannot read alone, and the turns are in the soul itself.

On the journey the soul can pass into states that the traveller does not understand. There may come a time when a traveller hates worship, or cannot bear to live among people. Such states arrive without explanation, and a traveller who does not know what they mean, or what to do next, can lose the way. Someone who has already passed through them can explain them. The traveller describes what they feel and is told what it means and what to do next, down to which words of remembrance suit that stage, *Allahu akbar* or *la ilaha illa Allah*.

How early in the journey that need begins is where the scholars differ. Sheikh Bahmanpour places it after the start. Many who want to set out look first for a teacher, and when they cannot find one they conclude that they cannot begin. In his account the teacher is found only once a traveller has moved past the first steps towards the deeper side of ʿirfan, and the first steps are the law: "First you have to comply completely with whatever Sharia tells."

The Sufi orders, by contrast, tell the beginner to come to them and to act on nothing without their instruction, or be lost on the path, a claim he calls "something of an exaggeration".

Murtada Mutahhari weighs the need for a guide more heavily, and places it at every stage. Each stage of the path, in his account, must be passed under the guidance of "a mature and perfect example of humanity who, having traveled this path, is aware of the manners and ways of each station". Without such a guide, the traveller "is in danger of going astray" [@light, p. 10].

The ʿurafaʾ, Mutahhari adds, call this master Khidr, the name of the servant Musa followed in Surah al-Kahf. He quotes a verse of their poetry that asks for that company: "Leave not this stage without the company of Khidr, / There is darkness ahead; be afraid of losing the way" [@light, p. 10].

## A guide beside the Imam, not in his place

::: {.point}
Shia hold that the Prophet's inner knowledge passed by inheritance to the Imams, so there is no chain of masters with authority beside them, and a guide is not obeyed blindly. He is a trusted teacher, consulted as one consults the knowledgeable, whereas the inner company of the Imam is the company the traveller can never do without.
:::

If a guide is needed, the next question is what authority he holds. The Sufi orders give one answer. Each order calls itself a *tariqa*, the word Chapter Six used for the inward path, and is built round an elder with his own method of instruction. Each has a *silsila*, a chain of masters that it traces back to the Prophet ﷺ. Through that chain, the orders hold, the Prophet's inner knowledge passed from heart to heart, from each master to the next.

Shia hold that the Prophet's inner knowledge did pass on, and that belief leaves no room for the chain. It passed by inheritance to the Imams (as), which is why Imam ʿAli (as) is not counted as one companion among others, however learned the others were. The orders, Sheikh Bahmanpour observes, keep the idea of an authority who receives the Prophet's inner knowledge, without believing in the Imam. For Shia that authority is the Imam, and so "We don't have such a thing as tariqa in Shia."

The orders also ask for a particular kind of obedience: a *murid*, a disciple, submits to the *shaykh* completely, whatever he says and whatever he does. Shia travellers do not join an order. They find an instructor for themselves, one who comes from no order and holds no special licence to instruct, and what they look for is a person they can trust, someone to whom "you could entrust your soul".

Where such a teacher stands is set out by ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, who distinguishes two teachers. The first, the general teacher, has not been specially appointed to guide anyone. Consulting him is like consulting any knowledgeable person, as a verse of Surah al-Nahl tells believers to do [@kernel, p. 80].

::: {.ayah ref="16:43" words="9-"}
Ask the People of the Reminder if you do not know.
:::

Asking those who know is the traveller's relation to a general teacher, and it lasts for a stretch of the road: once the traveller is honoured with visions of God's Attributes and Essence, Tabatabaʾi writes, "his company is not necessary". The special teacher is different. He is the one "specially designated to guide, and they are the Apostle of Allah and his true successors" [@kernel, p. 80].

Of the special teacher Tabatabaʾi is categorical: "The wayfarer cannot dispense with the company of the special teacher in any circumstance whatsoever, even if he has reached his sought homeland." The company he means is not physical. It is "the inner company of the Imam with the wayfarer" [@kernel, p. 80], present at every stage of the road and after its end [@kernel, p. 81]. A guide is consulted and trusted for a stretch of the road, whereas the company that never ends belongs to the Imam. How this position differs from the Sufi orders, with their chains and their pledges, is the subject of Chapter Fifteen.

## Teachers within living memory

::: {.point}
Shia teachers who walked this road can be named, in a line that runs from Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani to ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi and Imam Khomeini. They were scholars, several of them jurists, who belonged to no order, and their method was knowledge of the self.
:::

If Shia travellers find an instructor for themselves, there must be instructors to find, and the tradition can name teachers who walked this road within living memory. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, lecturing on the path in Qum in 1949–50 [@kernel, p. 9], named his own: "My preceptor was the late Haji Mirza Ali Agha Qazi who was a pupil of Agha Sayyid Ahmad Karbalai" [@light, p. 106]. Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi is the teacher under whom, as Chapter One noted, Tabatabaʾi travelled the path [@kernel, p. 8], and his instruction was practical: set a time each day or night to attend to the self, "for half an hour or a little more" [@light, p. 107].

The line goes back further. Karbalaʾi was one of the pupils of Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani, a different scholar from the Hamadani of Chapter Six, who found the law, the path, and the truth in a single prayer. Akhund Hamadani had been a distinguished student of jurisprudence before he was a guide [@light, p. 106], and he had been trained by a judge whose own guide was a weaver no one could identify, as the box below tells. His other pupils included Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi and Shaykh Muhammad Bahari [@light, p. 106]. Maliki Tabrizi taught Imam Khomeini's first formal classes in gnosis and ethics, in Qum [@forty_hadith, p. 8], whereas Bahari gave Chapter Six its rule that jurisprudence leads to good character, and good character to the knowledge of God.

They were scholars, several of them jurists, and none belonged to a Sufi order. Tabatabaʾi is plain about it: "we belong to none of these orders and follow the directions of the late Akhund, who had nothing to do with these orders" [@light, p. 105]. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls Tabatabaʾi a symbol of Shia mysticism in our time, the mysticism practised by scholars and not by the orders.

Their method was the one Chapter Four described, in which the way to God runs through the self, because the soul is the one thing we know from within and whoever knows his own self knows his Lord. Hamadani taught concentration on the self, and all his disciples followed "the method of gnosis (ma’rifah) of the soul, which is essential to the gnosis of the Lord" [@kernel, p. 87].

::: {.box-story title="Story: The weaver at the judge's door"}
More than a hundred years before ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi wrote, Sayyid ʿAli Shushtari was a leading scholar and judge in the city of Shushtar. One day a weaver knocked at his door and told him that a ruling he had given on a property was wrong. The property belonged to an orphan, the weaver said, and its deed was buried in a certain place. Shushtari found the deed where the weaver had said, and found that the witnesses had lied. The weaver returned on the next two nights with the same warning, and on the third told him to sell his belongings, go to Najaf, and wait for him there. Shushtari went, followed the weaver's instructions, and "reached a position too high to be described". In Najaf he attended Shaykh Murtada Ansari's lectures on jurisprudence and took over Ansari's teaching after his death. In the six months he had left to live, he trained Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani [@light, pp. 105–106]. Tabatabaʾi traces his own line of teachers back to the weaver through Shushtari, and adds that "it is not known who that weaver was" [@light, p. 106].
:::

## How to test a guide

::: {.point}
A guide is not known by wonders, by learning, or by claims. He is known through long company, by keeping to the shariʿa, and by turning people to God and not to himself; where long company is impossible, what he teaches is weighed against the Qur'an, the Sunna, and the practice of the Imams.
:::

A line of true teachers does not make every teacher who claims a place in it true, so a guide must be tested. The question is whether he has walked the road himself, and his learning does not answer it. Knowledge of the road is not the kind that study yields, as Chapter Two argued, so it cannot be read from what a teacher has written or studied.

Wonders do not answer it either. Chapter Six gave Najm al-Din Kubra's rule: someone seen flying through the air or walking on the sea, who has abandoned a single command of God, is lying in his claim to know God. Tabatabaʾi goes further. No one can tell that a person has reached *wusul*, the goal of the road, from "extraordinary feats, knowledge of hidden things, of private thoughts of persons, passing over fire and water", or from knowledge of the past and the future [@kernel, p. 81]. Such powers become possible early on the road, when the soul first begins to see what is hidden [@light, p. 101], and "there is a very great distance between this point and the frontiers of wusul and perfection" [@kernel, p. 81]. A wonder shows that a person sees what others do not. It does not show that he is a *wali*, a friend of God [@light, p. 101].

The weaver who began Tabatabaʾi's line knew where an orphan's deed was buried, which looks like the kind of proof Tabatabaʾi sets aside. It was not taken as one. The weaver's knowledge showed that he saw what others could not, and what it pointed to was an injustice done under the law, which Shushtari checked against the evidence of the case.

What shows a guide is time. The general teacher, Tabatabaʾi writes, "is not recognized except through company and being with him in public and private", until the traveller knows his reality and his certainty for sure [@kernel, p. 81]. A traveller comes to know a guide as anyone comes to know a person, by living alongside him. Maliki Tabrizi spent fourteen years with his.

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi"}
Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi, "The most distinguished jurisprudent and perfect mystic" in his biographer's words, studied jurisprudence in Najaf under the great jurists of his day. There he came to know Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani, and he spent fourteen years with him, learning ethics and mysticism. He became a guide in his turn: the jurist Shaykh Muhammad Husayn Isfahani, himself an authority in learning and deeds, wrote asking for his instructions in ethics and mysticism. Maliki Tabrizi later settled in Qum, where he taught jurisprudence and ethics and wrote [@journey, p. 8]. He died in 1925, and three years later his ethics class was resumed by Mirza Muhammad ʿAli Shahabadi, the teacher of Imam Khomeini whom Chapter Four introduced. When Shahabadi left for Tehran in 1936, he gave the class to Imam Khomeini [@forty_hadith, p. 8].
:::

Long company matters because the road has many pretenders. Tabatabaʾi quotes a Persian couplet: "A thousand trap lies concealed in this wilderness, / Not one out of a million finds deliverance there from" [@kernel, p. 83]. A traveller, he warns, must not surrender himself to everyone who "exhibits his merchandise and puts out his wares on display", with claims to unveiling and inner sight [@kernel, p. 83].

Imam Khomeini describes such pretenders sharply. Those who style themselves guides of God's creatures and leaders of mystic orders, he writes, "have distracted the attention of the creatures of God from Him, attracting them towards themselves" [@forty_hadith, p. 102]. Chapter One argued that ʿirfan is sought for God alone and never for powers, and the same aim tests the guide: a false guide leaves people looking at him, whereas a true guide turns them towards God.

Not every traveller can keep long company with a teacher before trusting him, and Tabatabaʾi gives a test for that case too. Where it is hard to inquire into a teacher's state, the traveller should trust in God and weigh whatever the teacher says or prescribes "in the light of the Book of God and the Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah and the practice of the Immaculate Imams" [@kernel, p. 83]. Only what agrees is put into practice, so the measure of a guide who cannot be known in person is the Imam, whose inner company never leaves the traveller.

The simplest form of the test comes in a saying of the Prophet ʿIsa (as), recorded in *Lantern of the Path*, the book attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as). His companions asked him with whom they should sit.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the saying of ʿIsa in Misbah al-Shariʿa (Lantern of the Path, p. 13)]" source="Lantern of the Path"}
With one the sight of whom reminds you of Allah, … and whose speech increases you in knowledge, and whose actions make you desire the next world. [@lantern, p. 13]
:::

Checking these three marks needs no inner sight. Any believer can ask what a person's presence turns them towards, what his speech adds to what they know, and what his conduct makes them want.

A guide therefore helps, and must be tested. Part Two has described what a traveller needs before setting out: the self, through which the road runs and which is deeper than it looks; the shariʿa, which bounds the road; and a guide who has walked it, trusted beside the Imam, and tested by the law, by long company, and by where he turns people's attention.

Once a guide is found, what does the road itself look like?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
This week, notice the people whose company you keep: at the mosque, at a majlis, at work, and at home. Of each, ask the three questions in ʿIsa's answer. Does the sight of them remind you of Allah? Does their speech increase you in knowledge? Do their actions make you desire the next world [@lantern, p. 13]? Where you find someone of whom all three are true, seek out their company.
:::
