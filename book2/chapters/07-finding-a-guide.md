On Fridays many of us read Surah al-Kahf [@mafatih, p. 85]. Halfway through it, the Prophet Musa (as) sets out to find a servant of God who has been taught knowledge from God's own presence (Q 18:65). This servant is known as Khidr [@forty_hadith, p. 508]. Musa is ready to travel for a long time to reach him (Q 18:60), and when he finds him he makes one request.

::: {.ayah ref="18:66"}
Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you for the purpose that you teach me some of the probity you have been taught?’
:::

In this verse a prophet to whom God spoke asks a stranger to teach him. The traveller on the road to God has the same need. The law marks the boundary of that road, and the next question is who walks beside the traveller on it. This chapter sets out why finding a guide helps the traveller, and how to know a true guide once found.

::: {.words}
*shaykh*: a spiritual master · *murid*: a disciple · *silsila*: a chain of masters traced back to the Prophet ﷺ · *wali* (plural *awliyaʾ*): a friend of God · *wusul*: arrival at the goal of the road
:::

## Why a traveller needs a companion

::: {.point}
A traveller needs someone who has walked the road, because the soul meets states on it that it cannot read alone.

1. On the road the soul passes into states the traveller does not understand.
2. Someone who has passed through those states can say what they mean and what to do next.
3. Sheikh Bahmanpour does not make a beginner wait for such a guide, because the first steps are the law.
4. At the start, a teacher is needed only to say which practices matter most.
5. Therefore: early or late, every traveller needs a companion who has walked the road; Murtada Mutahhari asks for one at every stage.
:::

A guide helps because the road has turns that a traveller cannot read alone, and those turns are in the soul. On the journey the soul can pass into strange states. There may come a time when a traveller hates worship, or cannot bear to live among people. Such states arrive without explanation. A traveller who does not know what they mean, or what to do next, can lose the way.

Someone who has already passed through these states can explain them. The traveller describes what they feel and is told what it means and what to do next. The advice can be as exact as which words of remembrance suit that stage, *Allahu akbar* or *la ilaha illa Allah*. This is why a guide must have travelled the road himself, and not only studied it.

How early in the journey this need begins is where scholars differ. Sheikh Bahmanpour does not make a beginner wait for a guide. Many who want to set out look first for a teacher, and when they cannot find one they conclude that they cannot begin. In his account, the guide who can read the soul's states is found only after the first steps, and the first steps are the law: "First you have to comply completely with whatever Sharia tells." The Sufi orders tell a beginner that anything done without their instruction will leave him lost, and he calls this "something of an exaggeration".

At the start, a teacher is needed in a narrower sense. He says which of the practices handed down in the hadith matter most for a beginner. Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, the teacher of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, had beginners recite the Musabbihat every night before sleep for a whole year. Only then did he give them a second instruction. The Musabbihat are the five surahs that open with God's glorification, and the Prophet ﷺ recited them every night before sleeping.

Murtada Mutahhari places the need at every stage, so on either account a traveller needs a companion who has walked the road. He asks that each stage be passed under the guidance of "a mature and perfect example of humanity who, having traveled this path, is aware of the manners and ways of each station". Without such a guide, the traveller "is in danger of going astray" [@light, p. 10]. The ʿurafaʾ call this guide Khidr, after the servant Musa followed. A verse of their poetry asks for his company: "Leave not this stage without the company of Khidr, / There is darkness ahead; be afraid of losing the way" [@light, p. 10].

## A guide beside the Imams, not in their place

::: {.point}
A Shia guide holds no authority beside the Imams, so he is trusted and consulted, never blindly obeyed.

1. The Sufi orders place authority in a chain of masters who pass on the Prophet's inner knowledge.
2. Shia hold that this knowledge passed by inheritance to the Imams, which leaves no room for such a chain.
3. Without that authority, a guide cannot ask for complete obedience; Shia look instead for a teacher they can trust.
4. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi calls such a teacher a general teacher, consulted as one consults anyone knowledgeable.
5. The general teacher is needed for a stretch of the road, whereas the special teacher is the Prophet and the Imams.
6. Therefore: the Imams' inner company never ends, so a guide walks beside the traveller under the Imams and never in their place.
:::

If a guide is needed, the next question is what authority he holds. The Sufi orders give one answer. Each order calls itself a path (*tariqa*) and is built round an elder with his own method of instruction. Each has a chain of masters (*silsila*) that it traces back to the Prophet ﷺ. Through that chain, the orders hold, the Prophet's inner knowledge passed from heart to heart, from each master to the next.

Shia agree that the Prophet's inner knowledge passed on, and that belief leaves no room for the chain. It passed by inheritance to the Imams (as). This is why Imam ʿAli (as) is not counted as one companion among others, however learned the others were. The orders, Sheikh Bahmanpour observes, keep the idea of an authority who receives the Prophet's inner knowledge, while not believing in the Imams. For Shia that authority belongs to the Imams, and so "We don't have such a thing as tariqa in Shia."

Without a chain of authority, a Shia guide cannot claim the obedience that the orders ask for. In an order, a disciple (*murid*) submits to the master (*shaykh*) completely, whatever he says and whatever he does. Shia travellers do not join an order. They find an instructor for themselves, one who comes from no order and holds no special licence to instruct. What they look for is a person they can trust, someone to whom "you could entrust your soul".

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi gives such an instructor a name and a place. He calls him the general teacher: someone who has not been specially appointed to guide anyone. Consulting him is like consulting anyone knowledgeable, as a verse of Surah al-Nahl tells believers to do [@kernel, p. 80].

::: {.ayah ref="16:43" words="9-"}
Ask the People of the Reminder if you do not know.
:::

The verse tells believers to ask those who know, and the general teacher is asked in this way for a stretch of the road. Once the traveller is honoured with visions of God's Attributes and Essence, Tabatabaʾi writes, "his company is not necessary". The special teacher is different. He is the one "specially designated to guide, and they are the Apostle of Allah and his true successors" [@kernel, p. 80]. These are the Prophet and the Imams (as).

The company of the Prophet and the Imams is the one the traveller never leaves. "The wayfarer cannot dispense with the company of the special teacher in any circumstance whatsoever, even if he has reached his sought homeland" [@kernel, p. 80]. The company meant is inner, and not "merely their outward company and comradeship". It lasts through every stage of the road and after its end [@kernel, pp. 80–81]. A guide therefore walks beside the traveller under the Imams, and never in their place. Chapter Fifteen returns to how this differs from the Sufi orders, with their chains and their pledges.

## Teachers within living memory

::: {.point}
A Shia traveller can name teachers of this road in our own time, all of them outside the Sufi orders.

1. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi named his own teacher, Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi.
2. Qadi's line runs back to Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani, whose pupils also taught Imam Khomeini.
3. These teachers shared one method: the road to God through knowledge of the self.
4. Therefore: they are named Shia teachers of our own time, scholars who stood outside the Sufi orders.
:::

If Shia travellers find an instructor for themselves, there must be instructors to find, and the tradition can name teachers who walked this road within living memory. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi named his own: "My preceptor was the late Haji Mirza Ali Agha Qazi" [@light, p. 106]. This is the same Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi who set beginners a year of the Musabbihat. Under him Tabatabaʾi travelled the path [@kernel, p. 8].

Qadi's line runs back two generations, to Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani. He is not the Hamadani who found the law, the path, and the truth in a single prayer. Akhund Hamadani had been a distinguished student of jurisprudence before he became a guide, and he trained several pupils who became guides in turn [@light, p. 106]. One of them was Qadi's teacher. Another gave Imam Khomeini his first formal classes in ethics and gnosis [@forty_hadith, p. 8]. How Akhund Hamadani was himself trained, by a judge whose own guide was a weaver no one could identify, is told in the box below.

These teachers shared one method, and it ran through the self, the one thing we know from within. Akhund Hamadani taught concentration on the self, and all his disciples followed "the method of gnosis (ma’rifah) of the soul, which is essential to the gnosis of the Lord" [@kernel, p. 87]. Qadi taught the same way. He asked a traveller to set a time each day or night to attend to the self, "for half an hour or a little more" [@light, p. 107].

They were scholars, several of them jurists, and they stood outside the Sufi orders. Tabatabaʾi is plain about it: "we belong to none of these orders and follow the directions of the late Akhund, who had nothing to do with these orders" [@light, p. 105]. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls Tabatabaʾi a symbol of Shia mysticism in the Shia world today.

::: {.box-story title="Story: The weaver at the judge's door"}
More than a hundred years before ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi wrote, Sayyid ʿAli Shushtari was a leading scholar and judge in the city of Shushtar. One day a weaver knocked at his door and told him that a ruling he had given on a property was wrong. The property belonged to an orphan, the weaver said, and its deed was buried in a certain place. Shushtari found the deed where the weaver had said, and found that the witnesses had lied. The weaver returned on the next two nights with the same warning, and on the third told him to sell his belongings, go to Najaf, and wait for him there. Shushtari went, followed the weaver's instructions, and "reached a position too high to be described". In Najaf he attended Shaykh Murtada Ansari's lectures on jurisprudence and took over Ansari's teaching after his death. In the six months he had left to live, he trained Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani [@light, pp. 105–106]. Tabatabaʾi traces his own line of teachers back to the weaver through Shushtari, and adds that "it is not known who that weaver was" [@light, p. 106].
:::

## How to test a guide

::: {.point}
A true guide is known by the law, by long company, and by turning people towards God, never by wonders.

1. A line of true teachers does not make every claimant true, and learning cannot show that a teacher has walked the road.
2. Wonders cannot show it either, because such powers come early on the road, far from its goal.
3. The law can: anyone who works wonders while abandoning one command of God is lying.
4. How far a guide has travelled is shown only by long company with him.
5. Long company is needed because the road is full of pretenders.
6. A pretender turns people towards himself, whereas a true guide turns them towards God.
7. Where long company is impossible, his teaching is weighed against the Qur'an, the Sunna, and the practice of the Imams.
8. ʿIsa's answer gives the simplest form of the test.
9. Therefore: any believer can test a guide by where his company turns them.
:::

A line of true teachers does not make every teacher who claims a place in it true, so a guide must be tested. The question is whether he has walked the road himself. His learning cannot answer it. Knowledge of the road is not the kind that study yields, so it cannot be read from what a teacher has written or studied.

Wonders cannot answer it either. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi lists what cannot show that a person has reached the goal of the road (*wusul*): "extraordinary feats, knowledge of hidden things, of private thoughts of persons, passing over fire and water", and knowledge of the past and the future [@kernel, p. 81]. Such powers become possible early on the road, when the soul first begins to see what is hidden [@light, p. 101]. In Tabatabaʾi's words, "there is a very great distance between this point and the frontiers of wusul and perfection" [@kernel, p. 81]. A wonder shows that a person sees what others do not. It does not show that he is a friend of God (*wali*) [@light, p. 101].

Where wonders prove nothing, the law gives a firm test. Najm al-Din Kubra's rule for the road applies here. Someone may be seen flying in the air or walking on the sea, but if they have abandoned "even one injunction of God", then "you should know that they are liars". Their wonders may be real, whereas their claim to know God is false.

Keeping the law rules a false guide out, but it does not show how far a true one has travelled. Only time shows that. Tabatabaʾi writes that the general teacher "is not recognized except through company and being with him in public and private". That company lasts until the traveller knows his reality and his certainty for sure [@kernel, p. 81]. A traveller comes to know a guide as anyone comes to know a person, by living alongside him. One of Akhund Hamadani's pupils, whose story is in the box below, lived alongside his teacher for fourteen years.

::: {.box-who title="Who's Who: Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi"}
Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi, "The most distinguished jurisprudent and perfect mystic" in his biographer's words, studied jurisprudence in Najaf under the great jurists of his day. There he came to know Akhund Mulla Husayn-Quli Hamadani, and he spent fourteen years with him, learning ethics and mysticism. He became a guide in his turn: the jurist Shaykh Muhammad Husayn Isfahani, himself an authority in learning and deeds, wrote asking for his instructions in ethics and mysticism. Maliki Tabrizi later settled in Qum, where he taught jurisprudence and ethics and wrote [@journey, p. 8]. He died in 1925, and three years later his ethics class was resumed by Mirza Muhammad ʿAli Shahabadi, the teacher of Imam Khomeini whom Chapter Four introduced. When Shahabadi left for Tehran in 1936, he gave the class to Imam Khomeini [@forty_hadith, p. 8].
:::

Long company matters because the road has many pretenders. Tabatabaʾi quotes a Persian couplet: "A thousand trap lies concealed in this wilderness, / Not one out of a million finds deliverance there from" [@kernel, p. 83]. A traveller, he warns, must not surrender himself to everyone who "exhibits his merchandise and puts out his wares on display", with claims to unveiling and inner sight [@kernel, p. 83].

Imam Khomeini names what such pretenders do. He writes of those who style themselves guides of God's creatures and leaders of mystic orders. They "have distracted the attention of the creatures of God from Him, attracting them towards themselves" [@forty_hadith, p. 102]. ʿIrfan is sought for God alone, and the guide is tested by the same aim. A false guide leaves people looking at him, whereas a true guide turns them towards God.

Not every traveller can keep long company with a teacher before trusting him, and Tabatabaʾi gives a test for that case too. Where it is hard to inquire into a teacher's state, the traveller should trust in God. He should then weigh whatever the teacher says or prescribes "in the light of the Book of God and the Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah and the practice of the Immaculate Imams" [@kernel, p. 83]. Only what agrees with them is put into practice.

The simplest form of the test comes in a saying of the Prophet ʿIsa (as). It is recorded in *Lantern of the Path*, the book attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as). ʿIsa's companions asked him with whom they should sit.

::: {.quote ar="[VERIFY: Arabic of the saying of ʿIsa in Misbah al-Shariʿa (Lantern of the Path, p. 13)]" source="Lantern of the Path"}
With one the sight of whom reminds you of Allah, … and whose speech increases you in knowledge, and whose actions make you desire the next world. [@lantern, p. 13]
:::

ʿIsa's three marks all ask where a person turns those around him: towards Allah, towards knowledge, and towards the next world. Checking them needs no inner sight. Any believer can ask what a person's presence reminds them of, what his speech adds to what they know, and what his conduct makes them want.

Finding a guide therefore helps the traveller, as long as the guide is tested. He has walked the road himself. He walks beside the traveller under the Imams, never in their place. He is known by the law, by long company, and by where he turns people, and never by wonders.

Once a guide is found, what does the road itself look like?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
This week, notice the people whose company you keep: at the mosque, at a majlis, at work, and at home. Of each, ask the three questions in ʿIsa's answer. Does the sight of them remind you of Allah? Does their speech increase you in knowledge? Do their actions make you desire the next world [@lantern, p. 13]? Where you find someone of whom all three are true, seek out their company.
:::
