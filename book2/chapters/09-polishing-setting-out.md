::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي وَقَدْ أَفْنَيْتُ عُمْرِي فِي شِرَّةِ ٱلسَّهْوِ عَنْكَ وَأَبْلَيْتُ شَبَابِي فِي سَكْرَةِ ٱلتَّبَاعُدِ مِنْكَ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, I have wasted my life committing the crime of forgetting You and played havoc with my youth, intoxicated with keeping myself away from You. [@light, p. 164]
:::

After the daily prayer, many of us ask God's forgiveness (*istighfar*) with the word *astaghfirullah*, often by habit. The words above come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, the prayer of the month of Shaʿban, and they ask forgiveness for something else. They name no particular wrong. They confess a whole life wasted in forgetting God, and a youth spent intoxicated with keeping away from Him.

A request of this kind is where the road to God begins. Of the four stages Chapter Eight set out, the first is polishing (*tajliya*), and repentance opens it. This chapter sets out the first stage in full. Anyone can begin it, by repenting. Repentance is then kept up at a steady pace. The law is kept strictly, starting with how we treat people.

::: {.words}
*tajliya*: polishing, the first stage of the road · *tawba*: repentance, turning back to God · *istighfar*: asking God's forgiveness · *mujahada*: the struggle against the self · *husn al-khuluq*: good character and conduct towards people
:::

## Anyone can begin

:::: {.point}
The road begins with repentance, and repentance is open to anyone after any sin.

1. Setting out has two moves: waking from heedlessness, then repenting (L08 ¶10).
2. Repentance (*tawba*) here reaches past sins: it turns from casualness towards God, from taking everything for granted and from wasting our lives (L08 ¶10); the Lantern names both kinds: "the repentance of the elite is for being occupied with anything other than Allah, and the repentance of the common people is for wrong actions".
3. Since repentance covers every wrong attitude towards God, a sinner qualifies (L08 ¶11–12). *(Story box: Fudayl the highwayman hears Q 57:16 from the house he is breaking into, answers "yes, it is time, my Lord", and becomes a trusted reporter in both traditions (L12 ¶19–22); Bishr al-Hafi in a line (L08 ¶12).)*
4. What holds a sinner back is despair, "this path is not for me", not the sin (L08 ¶16–18); Q 39:53 speaks to exactly that: "do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed Allah will forgive all sins."
5. Sincere repentance brings God's love (*Forty Hadith*) and polishes the heart "by the burnish of repentance" (*Lantern*), the polishing of Chapter Eight.
6. Therefore: anyone can begin, with the words the Munajat gives: "I am Your powerless sinning slave and Your repentant bondman".
::::

Setting out takes two moves. The first is to wake up, since we have been living as if asleep and heedless of a God who is near. The second move is to repent, and the mystics call this the first and most important step of the road. Imam Khomeini gives the same order in *Forty Hadith*: when the heart "awakes from its slumber of negligence, this state of awakening is followed by that of repentance" [@forty_hadith, p. 249]. <!-- src: L08 ¶10 -->

Repentance (*tawba*) in this sense reaches further than sins. It is, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "repenting from our casualness towards the Lord". We repent from taking everything for granted, from wasting our lives, and from not heeding the presence of God. No one is punished for these things. *Lantern of the Path*, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), sets this repentance beside the familiar kind: "the repentance of the elite is for being occupied with anything other than Allah, and the repentance of the common people is for wrong actions" [@lantern, p. 48]. <!-- src: L08 ¶10 -->

Repentance of this kind is open to a sinner as much as to a devout believer, because it covers every sin and every wrong attitude towards God. Among the early mystics were men who had lived as wrongdoers, Bishr al-Hafi and Fudayl ibn ʿIyad among them, until something struck a chord in their souls. They repented wholeheartedly, gave themselves to the religion of God, and set out on the road. Fudayl's turning came through a single verse. <!-- src: L08 ¶11-12 -->

:::: {.box-story title="Story: Fudayl the highwayman"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour tells the story of Fudayl ibn ʿIyad among his accounts of the early mystics. Fudayl was born in Samarqand, lived in Kufa, and died in Mecca in 187 AH, a contemporary of Imam al-Kazim (as) and Imam al-Rida (as). In his early life he was a highwayman. Once the young Fudayl climbed the wall of a house to reach a girl he loved. Someone inside was reciting the Qur'an, and he heard a verse of Surah al-Hadid.

::: {.ayah ref="57:16" words="1-13"}
Is it not time yet for those who have faith that their hearts should be humbled for Allah’s remembrance and toward the truth which has come down [to them],
:::

"Yes, it is time, my Lord," Fudayl answered. He turned back, went out into the desert, and began a life of hard austerity in repentance for what he had done. His repentance was sincere (*tawba nasuh*), and he became a great figure of the path, known for his fear of God and his weeping. He narrated many hadith from Imam al-Sadiq (as), and both traditions trust him: al-Kulayni reports his hadith in *al-Kafi*, and al-Bukhari reports them in his *Sahih*. <!-- src: L12 ¶19-22 -->
::::

What holds a sinner back is not the sin but despair. Many believers look at their past and conclude that this path is not for them. They allow that God may forgive them and admit them to jannah. The road to Him is another matter; in their eyes it belongs to people of a higher calibre. God would hardly accept on it someone who has wasted so much. A verse of Surah al-Zumar speaks to exactly this. <!-- src: L08 ¶15-18 -->

::: {.ayah ref="39:53" words="1-16"}
Say [that Allah declares,] ‘O My servants who have committed excesses against their own souls, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed Allah will forgive all sins.
:::

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads "who have committed excesses against their own souls" broadly. The words can mean real sins, but they also fit those who have wasted much of the capacity in their souls and now despair of moving on the path. The verse tells them not to despair, because God forgives all sins. It is an encouragement for the beginning of the road, so that no believer sets the road aside as meant for others. <!-- src: L08 ¶16-18 -->

Sincere repentance brings more than forgiveness. In *Forty Hadith*, Imam Khomeini cites Imam al-Sadiq (as): when a servant turns to God with sincere repentance, "God loves him and covers him (i.e. his sins) in the world and the Hereafter" [@forty_hadith, p. 249]. *Lantern of the Path* says what it does to the heart, which God created pure and clean. "When the pure heart turns grey by being fed on heedlessness and vexation, it is polished by the burnish of repentance and cleaned by the water of regret" [@lantern, p. 59]. This is the polishing that gives the first stage its name.

Anyone can therefore begin, and the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah gives the words to begin with. Near its end, the one praying comes before God as sinner and penitent at once.

::: {.quote ar="إِلٰهِي أَنَا عَبْدُكَ ٱلضَّعِيفُ ٱلْمُذْنِبُ وَمَمْلُوكُكَ ٱلْمُنِيبُ" source="Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah"}
My Lord, I am Your powerless sinning slave and Your repentant bondman. [@light, p. 165]
:::

The sentence admits weakness and sin, and in the same breath claims the name of God's repentant servant, in words any believer can say, whatever lies behind them.

## Steady, not fast

:::: {.point}
Repentance is kept up at a steady pace: not rushed, and not put off.

1. A repentant traveller may slip back; the answer is to repent again, because repentance is kept up, not done once (L08 ¶13–15). Side by side: Tabatabaʾi asks that a repented sin "must not be committed again"; set side by side, not merged (one speaks of the fall, the other of the standard).
2. The self fights back, "would come back and… take revenge" (L08 ¶15); this struggle with the self is *mujahada*.
3. Because the self fights back, rushing is dangerous: the Sheikh warns of "the dangers of rushing things forward" and asks for the pace the Imams advised (L08 ¶26–27).
4. Story (prose, unnamed): a young man in Tehran recited Ziyarat ʿAshura daily until light showed in his face; an elder told his father to stop him: "you need to have basis. You are rushing" (L08 ¶24–27).
5. Imam al-Sadiq (as) gives the same pace: faith has ten degrees, so "pull him up to you gently"; "Too much enthusiasm in the beginning leads to too little interest in the end".
6. Not rushing is not putting off: age makes the road harder, "but it is not impossible" (L08 ¶19–22); Imam Khomeini presses harder, "do not postpone the task of reform to old age", since "the springtime for tawbah is the time of youth"; all agree sooner is easier.
7. Therefore: repentance is kept up at a steady pace, not rushed and not put off.
::::

Once a traveller has begun with repentance, an old habit may still return, and when it does, the traveller repents again. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls repentance a continuous process. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi sets the standard beside it: a repented sin "must not be committed again" [@light, p. 89]. The traveller holds to that standard, and repents again on falling short of it. <!-- src: L08 ¶13-15 -->

Old habits return because the self fights back. The traveller's struggle with the self (*mujahada*) goes on through every stage of the road. At each stage there is a danger that the self "would come back and … take revenge", and when it strikes hard, it can leave a person reeling for days. <!-- src: L08 ¶15 -->

Because the self can strike back, rushing ahead is dangerous. A traveller who pushes forward faster than the self can carry may suddenly fall. A traveller may also arrive quickly at something and mistake it for the end of the road, which is why the right instruction matters. Sheikh Bahmanpour asks his listeners to be aware of "the dangers of rushing things forward" and to walk the road at the pace the Imams (as) advised. He illustrates the point with a story a friend from Iran had told him that morning. <!-- src: L08 ¶24, 26-27 -->

The friend, the son of a scholar in Tehran, had gone through a time in his youth when his heart was heavy and full of doubts. On someone's advice he began reciting Ziyarat ʿAshura every morning in full, with its hundred curses and hundred greetings. Over a long time it took effect, until he felt elated and people could see a light in his face. Then an elder of the path, a regular guest of the family in Tehran, saw his face and recognised that he was doing some practice. He told the father to make his son stop. <!-- src: L08 ¶24-26 -->

The young man felt so well that he asked why he should stop. The elder answered, "because you need to have basis. You are rushing." He meant that the young man was climbing faster than his foundation could bear, so he might suddenly fall. Progress has to be built on a foundation and come gradually. The lecture compares it with someone who suddenly fasts for three months. Such a person gains great spiritual energy and then feels abandoned by their own soul, unable to carry it further. <!-- src: L08 ¶26 -->

Imam al-Sadiq (as) gave the same counsel of pace. He told ʿAbd al-ʿAziz al-Qaratisi that faith has ten degrees "like the steps of a ladder which are climbed one by one. If you find anyone below you by one step, pull him up to you gently and do not burden him with what he cannot bear, or else you will break him" [@light, p. 89].

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi draws from this hadith the traveller's rule of moderation. A beginner full of zeal may take on every good practice at once and then tire of it, leaving the work incomplete. In his words, "Too much enthusiasm in the beginning leads to too little interest in the end" [@light, p. 89].

A steady pace still means setting out now, at whatever age. Some older believers conclude that the road is closed to them, which the lectures count as despair of God's mercy too. With age the road does become harder, as energy and capacity diminish, yet "it is not impossible", because age limits a person's ability and not the mercy of God. Imam Khomeini presses harder: "do not postpone the task of reform to old age" [@light, p. 127], since youth is "the springtime for tawbah" [@forty_hadith, p. 250]. Both agree that the sooner the road is begun, the easier it is. <!-- src: L08 ¶19-22 -->

Repentance is therefore kept up at a steady pace. It is renewed whenever the traveller falls and taken no faster than the self can bear, and it is not put off to old age, which only makes it harder.

## The law, starting with people

:::: {.point}
Polishing is keeping the law strictly, and its first demand is putting things right with people.

1. Polishing is keeping the law "in the strictest sense", the obligatory and the recommended (L07 ¶12–13), as Chapter Six set out (one sentence).
2. After repentance, "the first and foremost and most important condition is to behave yourself with regards to people" (L08 ¶39).
3. The reason: a "system of justice" underlies every action, and a wrong done to anyone, even an animal, blocks the traveller's rise until it is put right (L08 ¶39–40).
4. The inner road therefore cannot go around people: good behaviour is at the core of the Prophet's message, "I have been sent to perfect good behaviour" (Amuli).
5. The Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as), at the highest station, were people "everyone loved to sit with" (L08 ¶40–41); Imam ʿAli (as) describes the God-fearing: "They forgive those who oppress them, give to those who refuse them, and foster those who cut them off".
6. *(Story box: Hatim "the Deaf" spares a woman her embarrassment by pretending not to hear, and carries the name for life (L12 ¶25).)* Good conduct reaches down to the smallest courtesy to an ordinary person.
7. Therefore (section and chapter): the first stage, polishing, begins with repentance, open to anyone and kept up steadily, and is kept by the law, starting with people.

Close: "Polishing is open to every believer. Where does ʿirfan proper begin?"
::::

Repentance opens the first stage, and keeping the law makes up the rest of it. Polishing (*tajliya*) means keeping what the Prophet ﷺ ordered "in the strictest sense": doing the obligatory and the recommended acts, and avoiding the forbidden and the disliked. Chapter Six set out why this keeping lasts to the end of the road. In Shia mysticism, a traveller who has not polished soul and body by what the shariʿa says cannot move on to the next stage. <!-- src: L07 ¶12-13 -->

Within the law, one demand comes first. After repentance, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, the first and most important condition is how we behave towards people. A traveller may carry out every kind of repentance and every practice and still not go forward, because relations with people have soured. This first condition is good character and conduct (*husn al-khuluq*). <!-- src: L08 ¶39-40 -->

The reason is a "system of justice", which in the lectures underlies every action in this world and is "very, very strong". A wrong done to anyone, even to an animal, holds the traveller back from rising through the stations. The system will not let the traveller advance until such wrongs are put right, and this includes wrongs done without knowing it. The inner road therefore cannot go around people. <!-- src: L08 ¶39-40 -->

Good behaviour also stands at the centre of the religion. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, a fourteenth-century teacher of the path, places it "at the very core of the Prophet's message and purpose" and quotes the Prophet's words, "I have been sent to perfect good behaviour" [@inner_secrets, p. 108].

The Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as) show what this looks like at the highest station. They stood at the summit of the path, yet everyone loved to sit and talk with them, and they were pleasant and friendly with all. However high mystics rise, they have to stay friendly with ordinary people. Imam ʿAli (as), describing the pious in one of his orations, names these among their qualities. <!-- src: L08 ¶40-41 -->

::: {.quote ar="يعفو عمّن ظلمه ويعطي من حرمه ويصل من قطعه" source="Nahj al-Balagha"}
They forgive those who oppress them, give to those who refuse them, and foster those who cut them off. [@nahj, 1.191]
:::

Good conduct of this kind reaches down to the smallest courtesy shown to an ordinary person. One of the early mystics carried a nickname for the rest of his life because of one such courtesy.

::: {.box-story title="Story: Hatim the Deaf"}
Hatim al-Asamm was one of the great mystics of the early centuries, called the Luqman of this ummah. *Al-Asamm* means "the Deaf", though Hatim was not deaf. Sheikh Bahmanpour tells how he came by the name. A woman once came to him with a question. As she asked it, an embarrassing sound escaped her, and she was mortified. Hatim sensed her embarrassment. "What did you say?" he asked. "I didn't hear." She repeated the question more loudly, and again he said that he had not heard. She left certain that he was deaf, and relieved of her embarrassment. From then on he was known as Hatim the Deaf. In the lecture's words, "It is actually praise for his personality, the way he treated just an ordinary lady coming to him." <!-- src: L12 ¶24-25 -->
:::

The first stage, polishing, therefore begins with repentance. Repentance is open to anyone after any sin, and it is kept up at a steady pace. The rest of the stage is the law, kept strictly. Its first demand is that the traveller put things right with people, down to sparing an ordinary person's embarrassment.

Polishing is open to every believer. Where does ʿirfan proper begin?

::: {.box-try title="Try this"}
Sheikh Bahmanpour suggests beginning the road with a prayer of repentance that, the Mafatih reports, the Prophet ﷺ recommended for Sundays in the month of Dhu'l-Qaʿdah [@mafatih, p. 356]. According to the lectures, the traditionists allow it at any time, though a Sunday of that month is its best time. On that day, take a ritual bath (*ghusl*) and make wudu. Then pray four rakʿas, two at a time like every recommended prayer, reciting in each Surah al-Fatiha once, Surah al-Tawhid three times, and Surahs al-Falaq and al-Nas once each. Afterwards say *astaghfirullah* one hundred times (the lectures give seventy and name al-Nas alone; the Mafatih's count is followed here), then *la hawla wa la quwwata illa billahil-ʿaliyyil-ʿazim*. Then say the short supplication the Mafatih gives, which calls on God as the Mighty and the All-Forgiving and asks Him to forgive your sins and those of all believing men and women, since none forgives sins but Him. Among the rewards the Mafatih reports are that the repentance is accepted, and that those who pray it will find their rivals pleased with them on the Day of Resurrection [@mafatih, p. 356]. <!-- src: L08 ¶29-33 -->
:::
