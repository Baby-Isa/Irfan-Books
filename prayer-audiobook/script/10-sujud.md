# Track 10: Sujud, Prostration on the Earth

We have just risen from the bow. We stood up straight, and we said *sami' Allahu liman hamidah*. God hears the one who praises Him. For a moment we are upright again.

And now we go down. Not halfway, as in the bow, but all the way. To the floor. To the earth.

This is the *sujud*, the prostration. It is the lowest position the body can take in the prayer. And the sources say something about it that sounds, at first, like a paradox. The lowest moment of the prayer is the closest moment of the prayer. When your head is on the ground, you are nearer to God than at any other time.

So in this track we will go slowly. What we do and what we say. Why the forehead rests on the earth. Why there are two prostrations. And what happens in the heart when the head goes down.

---

First, the outward form, as it is done in the Ja'fari way.

After rising from the bow, it is recommended to say *Allahu akbar*, God is greater, and then to go down. You prostrate on seven parts of the body. The forehead. The two palms. The two knees. And the two big toes. All seven touch the ground together.

The forehead has a special rule. It must rest on the earth itself, or on something that grows from the earth and is not eaten or worn. Soil, stone, sand, wood, a mat woven from leaves. Not carpet, not cloth, not food. That is why so many of us carry a small tablet of clean, pressed clay, called a *turbah*. It is simply a way of taking the earth with you, so that wherever you pray, your forehead can still meet the ground. It is also strongly recommended to let the tip of the nose touch the earth as well, and Imam Khomeini mentions this as a sign of complete humility.

Then, with the forehead on the earth, you say *subhana rabbiyal-a'la wa bihamdih*. Glory be to my Lord, the Most High, and with His praise. Once is enough, and three times is recommended. Or you may simply say *subhan Allah*, glory be to God, three times, as in the bow.

Then you raise your head and sit, for a moment, back on your heels. It is recommended to say *Allahu akbar* again, and then *astaghfirullaha rabbi wa atubu ilayh*. I ask forgiveness of God, my Lord, and I turn back to Him.

Then *Allahu akbar*, and down again, for the second prostration, exactly like the first. The same seven points. The same words.

And when you come up from the second prostration and rise to stand for the next *rak'ah*, the next unit of the prayer, it is recommended to say, as you get up, *bi hawlillahi wa quwwatihi aqumu wa aq'ud*. By the might of God and His strength, I stand and I sit.

That is the whole of it. Two prostrations, in every unit of every prayer. Now let us look inside.

---

Let us begin with the earth.

Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, quotes the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family. The earth has been made for me a place of prostration, and a purifier.

Think about what that means. Every field, every desert, every patch of ground by the side of the road, has been made into a prayer mat. Imam Khomeini says the one who travels to God should tell his own heart that the house of nature is a mosque. We were brought into this world to worship in it. The whole world is the mosque, and the earth is its floor.

And then there is something even closer to home. In the Lantern of the Path, in the chapter on prostration, Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, peace be upon him, says this. Prostrate yourself with the prostration of someone who knows that he is created from the earth on which people tread.

The earth on which people tread. The ordinary ground that everyone walks over without a thought. That is what we are made of. And when the forehead goes down onto the clay, it is going home. It is touching its own origin.

Imam Khomeini makes a lovely point about this, which reaches back to the standing. He says that while we stand, our eyes rest on the place of prostration. And the place of prostration, he says, is dust, the original source of what we are. So all through the recitation, the little piece of clay in front of you is quietly reminding you where you came from, and where your head is about to go.

Many people's *turbah* is made from the earth of Karbala, where Imam Husayn, peace be upon him, gave his life. So at the lowest point of the prayer, the forehead rests on that memory too. But whatever it is made of, the meaning is the same. The forehead, the highest and proudest part of you, is placed on the humblest thing in the world.

---

Now, the seven parts of the body.

Imam Khomeini says something about them that I find very moving. He points out that the parts we place on the ground are the tips of the body. The forehead, the hands, the knees, the toes. These are the places where we feel things, and through which we move and act and exert our strength.

And placing all of these on the dust, he says, is a sign of complete surrender. It is offering up all of your powers at once. Nothing is held back.

There is a beautiful echo of this in what Imam al-Rida, peace be upon him, says about wudu, which Imam Khomeini also quotes. Why do we wash these particular parts of the body before prayer? Because, the Imam says, these are the parts that are exposed when the servant stands before the All-Powerful. With his face he prostrates. With his hands he asks and pleads. With his head he bows and prostrates. And with his legs he stands and sits.

So the water of wudu was already preparing the face for this moment. The face you washed at the beginning is the face you now lay on the earth.

And Sayyid Haydar Amuli, in Inner Secrets of the Path, adds a wider picture. He says that the movements of the prayer gather up the movements of all living things. Standing upright is the way of human beings. Bowing, with the back level, is the posture of the animals. And prostration, he says, is the posture of the plants. The Qur'an says that the herbs and the trees prostrate to Him. So when you go down, you are joining every plant and every tree along the road you are driving on. They have been in prostration all day.

---

Now the words. We are in the prostration, with the forehead on the earth, saying *subhana rabbiyal-a'la wa bihamdih*.

First, where these words come from. Imam Khomeini relates that when the verse was revealed, Glorify the name of your Lord, the Magnificent, the Prophet said, put this in your bow. And when the verse was revealed, Glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High, he said, put this in your prostration.

So both of these little phrases are taken straight from the Qur'an. In the bow we say *subhana rabbiyal-'azimi wa bihamdih*. Glory be to my Lord, the Magnificent. In the prostration we say *subhana rabbiyal-a'la wa bihamdih*. Glory be to my Lord, the Most High. Almost the same words. One word changes. Why?

Let us take the phrase apart, and come to that word at the end.

*Subhana*. Glory be. Imam Khomeini explains that to glorify God, in this sense, is to declare Him free of every description. Whatever you picture, He is beyond it. Whatever limit you could draw around Him, He is clear of it. So the first word of the prostration is already a kind of letting go. It says: whatever I think You are, You are more than that.

*Rabbi*. My Lord. Not simply the Lord, but my Lord. The One who has raised me and cared for me, from that dust the Lantern spoke of, step by step, until this moment.

And then *al-a'la*. The Most High.

Here is something worth noticing. When you were bowing, you said the Magnificent. When you are lowest of all, with your face on the ground, you say the Most High. It is exactly when you have gone as low as you can go that you speak of Him as higher than everything. The lower the servant, the higher the Lord appears. You cannot really say the Most High while you are standing tall. You can only really say it from the floor.

Imam Khomeini goes further. He quotes a narration from al-Kafi that the very first names God chose for Himself were *al-'Aliyy*, the High, and *al-'Azim*, the Magnificent. And he suggests that the High points to God's very Self, His Essence, while the Magnificent points to His attributes, to the qualities through which He shows Himself.

Let me put that simply. In the bow, you are bowing to His greatness as it shows itself in the world. His power, His knowledge, His mercy. All the qualities we can see at work around us. That is the Magnificent. But in the prostration, you go beyond the qualities. You are no longer looking at what He does, or what He is like. You are face down before Him, Himself. And all you can say is that He is higher than anything you could say. That is the Most High.

And then *wa bihamdih*. And with His praise. First we say what He is not. He is not limited, not like anything. Then we say what He is. He is the One to whom all praise belongs. Glory first, then praise. Letting go first, then thanks.

---

Now the inner meaning of the prostration as a whole.

Let us hear more from the Lantern of the Path, because Imam al-Sadiq's chapter on prostration is short and very strong. He begins with this. A person who performs a true prostration does not lose God at all, even if it is done only once in his entire life.

Only once. That is how much is held in this one movement. And he says, the person who does well in his prostration is never far from God.

Then he says something about the shape of the prostration itself, which I find very beautiful. Do you not see, he says, that in its outward appearance, the prostration is not complete except by disappearing from all things, and being veiled from all that the eyes see?

Think about the posture. When your face is on the ground, you cannot see anything. The room has gone. Your own body has folded up and hidden itself. And the Imam says, this is how God wants the inner being to be as well. Gone from everything except Him.

Then he says, if someone's heart is attached to something other than God in prayer, he is near to that thing, and far from what God wants from him in his prayer. And he quotes the Qur'an. God has not made for any man two hearts in his breast.

One heart. You only have one. It cannot be on the floor before God and in the office at the same time. Wherever it actually is, that is where you are near to.

---

Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, takes us further in.

He says that the secret of the prostration is to give up the self. The bow was the first step, and the prostration is the second. In the bow you humbled yourself. In the prostration you let go of yourself altogether.

And he gives a very concrete picture of this. The head, he says, holds the brain, which is like the throne of the soul. Everything you plan and decide and are proud of sits up there. And in the prostration, you take that throne and lay it on the lowest step of God's threshold. He says the one who prays should regard the whole world of dust as the doorstep of the King of kings.

The doorstep. When you put your forehead on the clay, you are not just on the floor. You are at a door.

And there is something more. Imam Khomeini says that the people of the heart see in this posture a kind of ascension that goes downwards. He calls it the ascension of Jonah. The Prophet Jonah, peace be upon him, was swallowed by the great fish and taken down into the darkness of the sea. And it was there, at the very bottom, that he turned to God, and was rescued. And the prostration is like that. You rise by going down.

Imam Khomeini even suggests that in putting the head on the dust, there is a hint of seeing the beauty of the Beautiful One hidden in the heart of the dust itself. As if, when you get right down close to the earth, you find that He is there as well.

---

But Imam Khomeini, as always, adds a word of honesty, and it is important.

He says that if, in this gesture of utter humility, there is still a claim in the heart, a feeling of how humble and spiritual I am being, then that is hypocrisy. And he calls this state the most dangerous one. Precisely because it is so high, the self can sneak in and take credit for it.

So what should we do? He says the traveller should cling to the hem of God's care, humbly and meekly, asking forgiveness for his shortcomings.

Asking forgiveness. Hold on to that. Because that is exactly what we do next.

---

We raise our heads from the first prostration and sit back. And we say *astaghfirullaha rabbi wa atubu ilayh*. I ask forgiveness of God, my Lord, and I turn back to Him.

There is a story in the Spiritual Journey of the Mystics that belongs here. Its author, the great scholar Haj Mirza Javad Agha Malaki Tabrizi, writes that when he was a student in Najaf, there was a learned sheikh there who was a shelter for the pious. He asked this sheikh what single practice had helped him most on the spiritual path. And one of the two things the sheikh recommended was a long prostration, every day and night, saying the words of the Qur'an: there is no god but You, glory be to You, I have been one of the wrongdoers.

Those are the words the Qur'an gives to the Prophet Jonah, in the belly of the fish. The same Jonah.

And the sheikh explained what to hold in the heart while saying them. I will give it in the book's own words, lightly shortened. O God, my soul is imprisoned in this worldly prison, chained by my own desires. And I will never say that You did this to me. Never. You are too glorious for that. It is I who have done this to myself, with my own hands.

That is the spirit of *astaghfirullah*. It is not anxious self-punishment. It is simply honesty. It is taking the blame off God, and off everyone else, and owning what is ours. And then, *wa atubu ilayh*. And I turn back to Him. Repentance, in the Arabic, is a turning. You have faced away. Now you turn round.

---

Why, then, are there two prostrations?

One standing, one recitation, one bow. But two prostrations. The Spiritual Journey of the Mystics gives the plainest answer. The prostration, it says, is the most supreme state a human being can reach. As the traditions say, a human being in prostration is nearer to God than in any other state. And it is the most important and most elegant state of servanthood. That, says the author, is why in each unit it is performed twice, while the other actions are performed only once. It is too precious to do only once.

And the same book recalls how the Imams loved to stay in prostration. Imam al-Sajjad, peace be upon him, would sometimes say there is no god but God, in truth, a thousand times in a single prostration. Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, adds that when Imam al-Sajjad went down into prostration, he would not raise his head until he was wet with sweat.

Imam Khomeini also gives a deeper answer to why we go down a second time, drawing on the prayer of the Prophet on the night of the ascension. Let me put it simply. In the first prostration, the servant is overwhelmed. It is like fainting before a great light. The words of glory that he repeats are what bring him back to himself. And when he comes back, a longing for that light catches fire in his heart, and he lifts his head. But when he sits up, he finds that there is still something of the self left in him. So with the *Allahu akbar*, he puts it aside, and goes down again. The second prostration goes deeper than the first, because now even the little that was left of him is let go.

So the sitting in between is not a pause. It is a moment of looking at yourself honestly. Asking forgiveness for what you find. And then going back down, with less of yourself to carry.

---

And then we rise.

We come up from the second prostration, and as we push ourselves up to stand, we say *bi hawlillahi wa quwwatihi aqumu wa aq'ud*. By the might of God and His strength, I stand and I sit.

Think of where it comes from. A moment ago, you had given up all your powers on the earth. Your forehead, your hands, your knees, your feet. Now you need them back, to stand up and carry on. And this phrase says: even the strength to stand is not mine. It is lent.

The Lantern of the Path puts it this way, in another chapter. You have no power over any of your movements, or any of your moments of stillness, except by the judgement and measure of God.

Your movements and your stillnesses. *Aqumu wa aq'ud*. I stand and I sit. Every rising and every going down happens by His strength. The prostration taught you that you are dust. Rising, you remember that the dust can only stand because He holds it up.

---

To carry into your next prayer.

When your forehead touches the clay, remember the Lantern of the Path. You are made of the earth on which people tread. Then say *al-a'la*, the Most High, and let it mean what it means from down there. The lower I am, the higher You are.

And when you sit up between the two prostrations and say *astaghfirullah*, do not rush it. Take one honest look at yourself. Then go down the second time with a little less of yourself to carry.
