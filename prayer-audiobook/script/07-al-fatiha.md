# Track 7: Al-Fatiha, Word by Word

We have said *Allahu akbar*. God is greater. The hands have come down. The prayer has begun, and everything outside it has been left behind.

Now we speak. And the first thing we say, in every prayer, is the same short surah. *Al-Fatiha*. The Opening. It is also called *al-Hamd*, the Praise, after its first word of praise. In the first two *rak'ahs* of every prayer it is obligatory. In the third and fourth, you may recite it, or the four glorifications instead. So you will say it at least ten times a day. Over a lifetime, it may be the words you speak more than any others.

That is exactly why it is so easy to say it without hearing it. So in this track we are going to slow right down. We will go verse by verse, and sometimes word by word. This is the longest track in the series, because *al-Fatiha* is the heart of the prayer.

---

First, a few words about how precious this surah is. Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, gathers a number of narrations about it at the end of his long commentary.

The Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, once said to his companion Jabir: Shall I teach you the most excellent surah that God has revealed in His Book? Jabir said yes, may my father and mother be your ransom. And the Prophet taught him *al-Fatiha*. Then he said: Shall I tell you something about it? It is a cure for every illness, except death.

And Ibn Abbas reported that the Prophet said: Everything has a foundation. The foundation of the Qur'an is the Opening. And the foundation of the Opening is *bismillah*. In the name of God.

So the foundation of the whole Qur'an is seven short verses. And the foundation of those seven verses is a single phrase. We are going to start very small, and find that it is very large.

---

Now, the thread that will run through this whole track.

Sayyid Haydar Amuli, in Inner Secrets of the Path, quotes one of the gnostics on the meaning of the prayer. The prayer, he says, is a conversation between God and His servant. And then he quotes a sacred hadith, a *hadith qudsi*, in which God Himself is the speaker. Imam Khomeini quotes the same hadith in the Disciplines of the Prayer. God says:

I have divided the prayer between Me and My servant. Half of it is for Me, and half of it is for My servant.

And then God goes through *al-Fatiha*, verse by verse, and says what He answers to each one. When the servant says this, God says that. When the servant says the next verse, God answers again.

So *al-Fatiha* is not a speech you make into silence. It is one half of a conversation. Every verse you say has a reply.

In this track, after each verse, we will stop and listen for what God answers.

Imam Khomeini draws a quiet but important lesson from this hadith. He says that when you recite *al-Fatiha*, you are not simply reading out somebody else's words. You are saying them as your own. He gives a lovely comparison. If you praise someone using the lines of a great poet, you have recited the poet, and you have also truly praised that person. In the same way, when you say *al-hamdu lillah*, you are reciting God's words, and you are also praising God yourself, with His own words.

And he points out that the hadith depends on this. God says, My servant has remembered Me. My servant has praised Me. If you were only reading aloud, there would be no remembering and no praising for God to answer.

---

One more thing before we begin. It is about the voice.

Imam Khomeini, in his Forty Hadith, has a section on the etiquette of reciting the Qur'an. He says the purpose of reciting is that the form of the Qur'an should be printed on the heart, that its commands should have an effect there, and that its call should take root there.

And then he says something surprising. He says that by etiquette, he does not mean what some reciters do, whose whole concern is the sounding of the letters. They become so busy with the sounds that they forget the meaning entirely. He calls this one of the ruses of Satan. And he says that after fifty years of such reciting, the words have often become so forced and so overdone that they come out distorted, in a strange shape.

The real etiquette, he says, is thinking and reflecting on the verses, and taking a lesson from them. He quotes Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, peace be upon him: This Qur'an is a minaret of guidance and a lamp in the darkness of the night. Let the one who looks, look carefully, and open his eyes to its light. For reflection is the life of the seeing heart.

Of course, the words of *al-Fatiha* should be said correctly. Learn them well, once, from someone who knows. Then let the tongue say them naturally, and give your attention to the meaning. We will come back to this warning at the very last word of the surah.

---

Before *al-Fatiha*, in the first *rak'ah*, it is recommended to seek refuge. Quietly, you say: *a'udhu billahi minash-shaytanir-rajim*. I seek refuge in God from Satan, the outcast.

This comes from the Qur'an itself. When you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge in God from the outcast Satan.

Early in his chapter on seeking refuge, Imam Khomeini turns to another verse. When Satan was expelled, he said to God: I shall lurk in ambush for them on Your straight path.

Notice where Satan says he will wait. Not on the wrong path. On the straight path. And Imam Khomeini says the prayer is the straight path of humanity, and the ladder of ascent to God. So of course Satan waits there. And so, before we set out, we ask for protection.

But Imam Khomeini is honest about how easily this becomes empty. He says there have been people who said these words for forty or fifty years, and were not saved from this bandit. In their conduct, and even in their beliefs, they followed him.

He gives a picture of it that is hard to forget. Imagine, he says, a man who sees an endless enemy army coming, and a strong fortress nearby. He says, I take refuge in this fortress from the enemy. But as he says it, he is walking away from the fortress, straight towards the army. That is the one whose seeking of refuge is only on the tongue. He suffers the enemy's harm, and the enemy's mockery as well.

So what makes the seeking of refuge real? Imam Khomeini names three things.

The first is sincerity. Satan himself said to God: I will lead them all astray, except Your sincere servants. The sincere are the ones he cannot reach.

The second is faith. Not argument, not knowledge in the head, but faith that has settled in the heart through remembrance. Satan, he points out, knew all about God and the hereafter. Knowledge alone did not save him.

The third is reliance. When the heart really sees that nothing has power except God, it has nowhere else to run. And then, Imam Khomeini says, its seeking of refuge becomes true.

And what exactly are we seeking refuge from? Here he says something that goes very deep. Whatever turns you away from God, he says, is your Satan. It might be position or rank. It might be learning. It might be comfort, or it might be hardship. When the heart loves something other than God, that thing becomes its world, and its snare.

The Lantern of the Path, in its chapter on reciting the Qur'an, gives the other side. Imam al-Sadiq says that when the heart of the reciter fears God, the accursed Satan flees from him.

Imam Khomeini adds one more detail. He says the seeking of refuge is not part of *al-Fatiha*. It is a doorway before it. Leaving behind comes before taking on. You turn away from the house of the self first, and then you enter.

---

Now the first verse. *Bismillahir-Rahmanir-Rahim*. In the name of God, the Beneficent, the Merciful.

In the school of the Ahl al-Bayt, the *bismillah* is part of *al-Fatiha* itself. It is its first verse, and it cannot be left out.

What does it mean, to begin in someone's name? Imam Khomeini quotes Imam al-Rida, peace be upon him, who was asked exactly this. The Imam said: When someone says *bismillah*, he means, I place upon myself a mark from among the marks of God, and that mark is worship. He was asked, what is this mark? He said, it is a sign.

So the *bismillah* is like a seal pressed into wax. You are stamping yourself as His. Imam Khomeini says that for people like us, the discipline is simple. As you say the *bismillah*, brand the heart with the mark of servanthood. Do not be satisfied with words that slip out without intention.

He also explains why the refuge comes first. As long as a person is under Satan's control, he says, he carries Satan's marks. Only when the house has been cleared can God's mark be placed on it.

There is a deeper reading too, which Imam Khomeini gives from the people of knowledge. In Arabic, a name is a sign. And everything in existence is a sign of God, a name of God. The trees, the sky, the road in front of you, your own breath. All of it came into being through His name. So the one who says *bismillah* should tell his heart that every movement and every stillness, his own and the whole world's, stands by the name of God.

Then come two names of mercy. *Ar-Rahman*. The Beneficent. *Ar-Rahim*. The Merciful. Both come from the word *rahmah*, mercy. Imam Khomeini quotes a narration which says that God is *Rahman* to all His creatures, and *Rahim* to the believers in particular. *Ar-Rahman* is the mercy that gives existence to everything, without exception. *Ar-Rahim* is the mercy that guides and perfects. And *ar-Rahman* comes first, he says, because it is wider. My mercy encompasses all things. His mercy comes before His anger.

And then Imam Khomeini says something very practical. If you want your *bismillah* to be real, he says, carry God's mercy into your heart. And the sign that it has really reached your heart is that you look at God's servants with kindness and gentleness, and want good for every one of them.

He gives an example from the Qur'an. Pharaoh was the worst of tyrants. He called himself the most high lord, and he had the baby boys of Moses's people put to death. God could have destroyed him with a single flash of His anger. Instead, He spent years preparing Moses, and then sent him, with his brother Aaron, to this one man. And He told them: speak to him gently. Perhaps he will remember, or fear.

That is the mercy of the *bismillah*. If God said that about Pharaoh, how should we speak to the people in our own lives?

*Bismillahir-Rahmanir-Rahim*.

And God answers. My servant has remembered Me.

In the Disciplines of the Prayer, Imam Khomeini quotes the verse: So remember Me, and I will remember you. And a sacred hadith, in which God says to Moses: I am the companion of whoever remembers Me. You have hardly begun the prayer, and already you have been remembered.

---

The second verse. *Al-hamdu lillahi Rabbil-'alamin*. All praise belongs to God, the Lord of the worlds.

Start with *al-hamd*. Praise. Imam Khomeini explains that we praise for two reasons. We praise someone for a kindness they have done us, and we praise something for its beauty. And then he asks: who is the real giver of every kindness, and the real owner of every beauty? Whoever the giver seems to be, the gift was God's. Whatever beauty you see, it is a ray of His beauty. So all praise, every praise ever spoken by anyone, about anything, in the end returns to Him. That is why it is *al-hamd*, all praise, and not just some.

He tells a small story about Imam Muhammad al-Baqir, peace be upon him. One day the Imam came out of a house and found that his riding animal had gone. He said: If I find it, I will praise God as He deserves to be praised. When the animal was found, the Imam mounted it, straightened his clothes, and said: *al-hamdu lillah*.

That was all. Two words. Because those two words, said truly, are the fullest praise there is.

Imam Khomeini quotes the Prophet on the weight of these words. *La ilaha illallah*, there is no god but God, fills one half of the scale, and *al-hamdu lillah* fills the other. The servant's saying *al-hamdu lillah* is heavier in God's scales than the seven heavens and the seven earths. And if God gave one of His servants the entire world, and then that servant said *al-hamdu lillah*, his saying it would be better than everything he had been given.

But then the Imam turns the mirror on us. The one whose tongue never stops praising the people of this world, he asks, how can he say, all praise belongs to God? It is a fair question. How much of our day is spent admiring, flattering, envying, or fearing other people? The verse is gently asking us where our praise really goes.

And the Prophet himself said, about praising God: I cannot count Your praises. You are as You have praised Yourself. Even the greatest of praisers ends by confessing that he cannot praise enough.

Now the word *Rabb*. Lord. In Arabic it carries the sense of the one who raises and nurtures, the one who brings something up, step by step, to its completion. And *al-'alamin*, the worlds. Imam Khomeini mentions that some connect this word to *'alamah*, a sign. Every world is a sign of its Lord.

He describes the nurturing hand of God in a way that is very moving. God has taken you, he says, out of weakness, lack, fear and the darkness of non-existence, and brought you into strength and security and the light of being human. He has carried you through stage after stage of creation to reach this one. And He goes on raising you, until you become something you cannot now imagine. He quotes a sacred saying: Son of Adam, I created all things for you, and I created you for Myself.

There is a sober side to this word as well. Imam Khomeini reminds us that in the grave, the angels will ask: Who is your Lord? And only the one whose heart has truly been raised by God will be able to answer. He says that even a high fever can make a person forget everything he has learned, except what has become part of his nature through constant remembrance. So the time to learn the answer to that question is now. And one of the places we learn it is right here, saying *Rabbil-'alamin*, ten times a day.

*Al-hamdu lillahi Rabbil-'alamin*.

And God answers. My servant has praised Me.

In Imam Khomeini's version of the hadith, God adds a phrase: this is the meaning of, God hears the one who praises Him. In Arabic, *sami' Allahu liman hamidah*. Hold on to those words. We will meet them again when we rise from the bow.

---

The third verse. *Ar-Rahmanir-Rahim*. The Beneficent, the Merciful.

We have just heard these names in the *bismillah*. Why again?

Imam Khomeini suggests that in the *bismillah*, our attention is on how everything comes out of God, into existence. In the body of the surah, our attention is turning the other way, to how everything returns to Him. The same mercy that sent us out is the mercy that brings us home.

He also describes what happens inside the heart at this verse. Once you have understood that all praise belongs to God, you begin to see, everywhere, His mercy spreading out existence, and His mercy bringing things to their perfection. And when you see such beauty and such generosity, he says, the heart feels awe. The greatness of God is planted in it.

And Imam Khomeini offers a striking image here. He says that four names in this surah, *Rabb*, *Rahman*, *Rahim* and *Malik*, may be the inner reality of the four great angels who carry the Throne. The Lord, who raises and provides, is the inner reality of Michael, who is in charge of provision. The Beneficent, who spreads out existence, is the inner reality of Israfil, who blows the Trumpet and spreads out the spirits. The Merciful, who guides and perfects, is the inner reality of Gabriel, the teacher. And the Master, who gathers everything back, is the inner reality of Izra'il, the angel of death. So, he says, in these first few verses, the whole circle of existence is already contained.

*Ar-Rahmanir-Rahim*.

And God answers. My servant has extolled Me. My servant has magnified Me.

---

The fourth verse. *Maliki yawmid-din*. Master of the Day of Judgment.

There is a small point about how this word is said. Some of the early reciters read it without the long vowel, so that it means king. Imam Khomeini holds firmly to the reading with the long vowel, *Maliki*, meaning master, or owner. And his reason is beautiful. He says that *al-Fatiha* is not like other surahs. In every age, hundreds of millions of Muslims have heard it from hundreds of millions of others, who heard it from hundreds of millions before them, all the way back to the Imams and to the Prophet himself. Exactly the same, with not a letter added or taken away. So when you say it, you are saying it in the same breath as all of them.

Now, what kind of ownership is this? Imam Khomeini says that God's ownership is not like our owning a house, or a king owning a kingdom. Those are arrangements, agreements. It is nearer even than your ownership of your own hand. He quotes the Qur'an: We are nearer to him than his jugular vein.

But if God owns everything, always, why does the verse say Master of the Day of Judgment in particular?

Imam Khomeini gives two answers. The first is that the Day of Judgment is the day of gathering. Everything that is scattered across the days of this world is gathered there. So the Master of that day is the Master of all days.

The second is that on that day, His ownership becomes visible. Here, in this world, it is veiled. We imagine we own things. We imagine other people run things. On that day, the veils fall, and God asks: To whom does the sovereignty belong this day? And there is no one to answer. So He answers Himself. To God, the One, the Overwhelming.

And then Imam Khomeini says something remarkable. He says that the perfect human being, travelling in the ascension of the prayer, meets that day here, in this world. In the prayer, as in the resurrection, the veils fall away, and he sees that everything belongs to God.

The word *din* can mean judgment, the reckoning. Imam Khomeini adds that it may also mean religion itself. The Day of Judgment is the day when the results of religion appear, the day when what our faith really was is brought out from behind the curtain.

In the Disciplines of the Prayer, he describes this verse as the moment the idols of the heart break, and the true Owner of the house appears in it, with no intruder left.

*Maliki yawmid-din*.

And God answers. My servant has glorified Me.

There is another version of the hadith, which Imam Khomeini also mentions, in which God says: My servant has entrusted himself to Me.

That is worth sitting with. When you say Master of the Day of Judgment, you are not only describing God. You are handing yourself over to Him.

---

The fifth verse. *Iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in*. You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.

Listen to what has just happened in the language. Up to now, we have been speaking about God. Praise belongs to Him. He is the Lord, the Merciful, the Master. Now, suddenly, we speak to Him. You. *Iyyaka*.

Imam Khomeini says that in ordinary speech, a change like this is just a matter of style, to keep the listener awake. But in the prayer it is much more. Through the first verses, he says, the traveller has been coming out of the veils. At Master of the Day of Judgment, the crowd of other things fades. And now he finds himself present. Standing before God. So he stops speaking about Him, and speaks to Him, face to face.

Notice too that *Iyyaka*, You, comes first in the sentence. Not we worship You, but You, we worship. Imam Khomeini says the servant is putting the One he is speaking to before himself and before his own worship.

Then a question. Why do we say we? We worship, we ask for help. Usually you are praying alone.

Imam Khomeini gives three answers, and they are all lovely.

The first comes from the scholars. They say this is a kind of permitted trick. The worshipper slips his own small, imperfect worship in among the worship of all the others who pray, including the perfect friends of God, whose worship is certainly accepted. He presents it all together, as one bundle. And it is not the way of the Generous, they say, to pick through a bundle and accept some of it and reject the rest.

The second answer is his own. In the track on the *adhan* and *iqamah*, we heard that there is a whole kingdom inside you, with the heart as its king, and that the call to prayer tells that kingdom to get ready. Imam Khomeini says the *iqamah* then stands all those powers upright before God. Now the heart stands at the front as their imam, and there is a hadith which says: the believer alone is a congregation. So when you say we, your eyes and ears and hands and thoughts are all praying behind your heart.

The third answer is the widest. Imam Khomeini says that everything in existence worships God, in its own way. There is not a thing, the Qur'an says, that does not glorify Him with praise, but you do not understand their glorification. So when you say we worship, you are joining the whole of creation.

Why does worship come before asking for help? Imam Khomeini says that keeping worship for God alone is an early stage. Many people do that. But asking help from God alone, and truly seeing that every help that reaches you is really His help, comes later, and is harder. The first is wanting only Him. The second is seeing only Him.

And now the honest part. Imam Khomeini asks: The one whose heart leans on people, and whose trust is in them, with what tongue can he say, You alone we ask for help? He admits that for people like us, still veiled, this verse is a claim we have not yet made true.

But he does not leave us there. He says: if we become aware of our veil, and recognise our shortcoming, our worship becomes more luminous, in proportion to that awareness. And then he says this. If you are not yet a person for this field, then at least remember your faults, pay attention to your weakness, and perform your duties with shame, without claiming to be a true servant. And recite these verses through the tongues of the perfect ones.

What a kind piece of advice that is. When you say *iyyaka na'budu*, and you know it is not yet fully true of you, you can say it in the voice of the Prophet, and of the Imams, for whom it is entirely true. You are standing in their line. Their words carry yours.

*Iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in*.

And God answers. This is between Me and My servant. And in Amuli's version, God adds: and My servant shall be given what he asks for.

This is the middle of the surah, and the meeting point. The verses before it were God's half. The verses after it are yours. And this one, Imam Khomeini says, is said in privacy. There is no veil between the servant and God here. That is why God calls it between Me and My servant.

---

The sixth verse. *Ihdinas-siratal-mustaqim*. Guide us to the straight path.

Now comes the first thing we actually ask for. Of everything we could ask, the surah teaches us to ask for this.

Remember Satan's oath, at the doorway of *al-Fatiha*. I shall lurk in ambush for them on Your straight path. Now, at the heart of the surah, we ask to be guided on that very path. We sought refuge from the one who waits on it. Now we ask the One who owns it to lead us along it.

Imam Khomeini says that this verse means different things to different people. For those who have truly arrived, it means: keep us firm. Make us steady where we are. But as for us, he says, the people of the veil, we ask for guidance in its ordinary sense. Show us the way.

And what is the straight path? Imam Khomeini quotes a narration that the Prophet once drew a straight line, and other lines on either side of it, and said: this middle straight line is from me. And Imam al-Baqir said: We are the middle nation. So the straight path is the way of the Prophet and his family. Not too far to one side, and not too far to the other.

Imam Khomeini also gives a picture that I find very tender. He says that God, wanting to be known, placed in every creature two things. A fire, which is love, and a longing for the beautiful. And a light, which is guidance, to find the way. The fire drives us, and the light shows us where to go. And the path along which that light leads is the straight path, because, as the Qur'an says, surely my Lord is on a straight path.

He adds that every creature has its own path. The ways to God, as a saying goes, are as many as the breaths of the creatures. And the human path, he says, is the longest and the most delicate of them all, because a human being has so many veils to pass through.

*Ihdinas-siratal-mustaqim*.

And God answers. My servant has this right, and it is granted to him.

Listen to that. Granted. In Amuli's version: These are for My servant, and My servant shall be given what he asks for. You are not asking into the void. The answer has already been promised.

---

The seventh verse. *Siratal-ladhina an'amta 'alayhim*. The path of those You have blessed. *Ghayril-maghdubi 'alayhim*. Not of those upon whom is anger. *Wa lad-dallin*. Nor of those who go astray.

Who are those You have blessed? What is this blessing?

Imam Khomeini quotes the great scholar Shaykh Baha'i, who divides God's blessings into eight kinds. The blessings of this world and of the next, those we are born with and those we earn, those of the body and those of the spirit. A careful and beautiful list. And then Imam Khomeini says, respectfully, that the greatest blessing of all has slipped from the Shaykh's pen. The greatest blessing is knowing God. Knowing His names, knowing His works in the world, and knowing Him. That is the blessing of the ones on the straight path. Not only gardens, and not only rewards. Him.

And the two who are not on the path? Imam Khomeini reads them, in one place, as the two kinds of going too far. One goes too far one way, and the other falls short the other way.

He gives a clear example. There are people, he says, who stop at the outside of the religion. For them the Qur'an is only rules and outward forms, and they never reflect on it at all. And there are others who think they have found the inside, and so they turn away from the outward practices altogether. Both, he says, have left the middle. And he puts it very simply. The way to the inside passes through observing the outside. Without the outward form of the law, the way to the inside cannot be found.

That is why this series spends time on both. The washing and the words, and the meaning inside them.

---

And now, as promised, back to the voice. Because Imam Khomeini, in his Forty Hadith, chooses this very word, *ad-dallin*, as his example of something going wrong.

In the first track, we heard his warning against *waswas*, obsessive doubt. Here he is more precise. He says the commonest form of *waswas* is in the recitation of the prayer. People push so hard at the letters that they break the rules of correct recitation and change the shape of the word. He says some pronounce *dallin* so that its heavy letter sounds like a completely different one. And the breathy letter in the middle of *Rahman* and *Rahim* is turned around and around in the throat, until a strange sound comes out, and the word is no longer itself.

And then, he says, all the spiritual secrets of the prayer, the ascension of the believer, are neglected, while all the attention goes on these niceties. And in the end, the words are not even correct.

There is something almost humorous in it. A person can go astray in the very word that asks to be kept from going astray.

So say the words clearly. Say them as well as you were taught. And then trust them. The care that is left over belongs to the meaning.

*Siratal-ladhina an'amta 'alayhim, ghayril-maghdubi 'alayhim wa lad-dallin*.

And God answers, in Amuli's version. These are for My servant, and My servant shall be given what he asks for.

---

Let us step back and look at the whole surah.

Imam Khomeini, at the end of his commentary, says that *al-Fatiha* contains the whole journey. The seeking of refuge is leaving the house of the self. The *bismillah* is taking on God's mark. From the praise to Master of the Day of Judgment is the journey towards God, until everything else falls away. At *Iyyaka*, the traveller finds himself in God's presence, and from there he turns back, awake, to the world. And the journey ends, he says, with Guide us to the straight path.

All of that, in seven verses, in less than a minute.

So there is no need to hurry. In the Lantern of the Path, Imam al-Sadiq says that the one who recites with a humble heart and a still body will discover the sweetness of the way God speaks to His servants. And if he drinks one cup of that drink, the Imam says, he will never prefer any other state to it.

Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, describes the heart as being like a small child who cannot yet speak. At first, the tongue has to teach it. The tongue says the words, and the heart slowly repeats them. It is tiring work, like teaching a baby its first words. But one day, the heart begins to speak by itself. And then everything turns around. The heart speaks, and the tongue simply follows.

And he relates this story. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq was once praying, and he fell to the ground in a faint. When he came round, they asked him what had happened. He said: I kept repeating this verse to my heart, until I heard it from the One who spoke it. And my body could not bear to see His power.

We are not there. But that is where the road goes. From saying the words, to hearing them. And at last, to hearing the One who speaks them.

---

To carry into your next prayer.

Take the hadith of the divided prayer with you. In your next prayer, after just one verse of *al-Fatiha*, leave the smallest space before the next one, and remember the answer. After *al-hamdu lillahi Rabbil-'alamin*, God says, My servant has praised Me. You do not need to hear a voice. Only know that the words did not go into silence. They were heard.

And when you reach *Iyyaka na'budu*, and you feel that it is not yet true of you, do not stop, and do not strain. Say it in the company of the Prophet and his family, through their tongues, as Imam Khomeini advised. Let your small worship travel in among theirs.

In the first two *rak'ahs*, *al-Fatiha* is followed by a second surah. In the next track, we look at the two that are recited most of all: *al-Ikhlas*, the surah of God's oneness, and *al-Qadr*, the surah of the Night of Power.
