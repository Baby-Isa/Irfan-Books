# Track 12: Tashahhud and the Salam

We have come a long way together. We stood, and made our intention. We said *Allahu akbar*, and let the world fall behind us. We recited, we bowed, and we went down to the earth in prostration. Now, after the last prostration, we sit up. And this time, we stay sitting.

This is the last stage of the prayer itself. First the testimony, the *tashahhud*. Then the greeting of peace, the *salam*. In this track we will go through both of them slowly, word by word. And we will ask two questions. What does it mean to bear witness, at the very end of the prayer? And why should a prayer end by wishing peace on anyone at all?

---

First, the outward form.

The *tashahhud* is said sitting. It comes after the two prostrations of the second rak'ah. In the dawn prayer, which has only two rak'ahs, that is the end, and the *salam* follows straight away. In the prayers of three and four rak'ahs, there is a *tashahhud* after the second rak'ah, and then you stand up again and carry on. And there is a final *tashahhud* after the last rak'ah, which leads into the *salam*.

The *tashahhud* has three parts. Two testimonies, and then a prayer of blessing on the Prophet and his family.

The *salam* has three greetings. The first is recommended. With the second and the third, the prayer is complete, and you are free to rise.

That is all the outward form we need. Now the words.

---

The first testimony begins with *ash-hadu*. I bear witness.

That is the language of a witness. A witness is not someone who has an opinion. A witness is someone who was there, and saw. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, in Inner Secrets of the Path, quotes one of the gnostics who says that the testimony at the end of the prayer stands for the soul's arrival at the state of witnessing. You may remember the three words of Amuli from the first track. Worship Him. Come into His presence. Witness Him. Here, near the end of the prayer, is the word witness, in your own mouth.

*An la ilaha*. That there is no god.

*Illallah*. Except God.

*Wahdahu*. Alone.

*La sharika lah*. He has no partner.

No partner. It is easy to say that and think only of idols of wood and stone. But listen to how Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, in the Lantern of the Path, describes the people who say it. He says that the forelocks of all creation are in God's hand. Creatures possess neither breath nor sight, except by His power and will. They cannot bring about the least thing in His kingdom, except by His permission.

Neither breath nor sight. So the breath you are taking as you say these words is not yours. The eyes you are using to watch the road are not yours. They are lent. When you say *la sharika lah*, He has no partner, you are saying that you are not His partner either. Not even in your next breath.

---

The second testimony.

*Wa ash-hadu*. And I bear witness.

*Anna Muhammadan*. That Muhammad.

*'Abduhu*. Is His servant.

*Wa rasuluh*. And His messenger.

Notice the order. Servant comes first. Messenger comes second.

Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, stops on exactly this. He says that the state of servanthood comes before the state of prophethood. Servanthood is the ground on which every station stands, and prophethood is a branch that grows out of it.

He points to a verse of the Qur'an about the night journey of the Prophet. Glorified is He who carried His servant by night. Not His messenger. His servant. Imam Khomeini says that it was the step of servanthood that carried him up to nearness. And he says that in the *tashahhud*, coming back from the prostration, there is once again a turning to servanthood, before the mind turns to the messengership.

So when you say *'abduhu*, His servant, you are naming the highest station a human being has ever reached. And it is a station of being a servant. That is very good news for us. Because servant is a word that has room for us too.

---

Then the blessing.

*Allahumma*. O God.

*Salli 'ala Muhammadin*. Send Your blessings on Muhammad.

*Wa Ali Muhammad*. And on the family of Muhammad.

In the Lantern of the Path there is a narration, through Salman al-Farsi, in which the Prophet speaks about himself and his family. He says that God named them with names taken from His own names. God is *al-Mahmud*, the Praised, and I am Muhammad. And he says of himself and his family, before the heavens and the earth were made: we were lights who glorified Him, listened to Him and obeyed Him.

So the family of Muhammad is not an afterthought in this prayer. You ask for blessing on the Prophet, and in the same breath, on those who were his light.

Sayyid Haydar Amuli adds something lovely. He says that when you ask for blessings on the Prophet, you are in harmony with God Himself, and with the angels, and with all the believers. Because the Qur'an says that God and His angels bless the Prophet, and then it says, O you who believe, bless him too. So at this moment, sitting on your prayer mat, perhaps alone in a quiet room, you are joining a chorus that began with God.

---

Now, the inner meaning of the *tashahhud* as a whole.

Imam Khomeini makes a point here that I find very moving. I will put it simply.

There is a testimony before the prayer begins, in the call to prayer, the *adhan*. And there is a testimony at the end, in the *tashahhud*. The words are almost the same. But Imam Khomeini says they are not the same testimony at all.

The first is said before the journey. It is the testimony of someone who believes, and who is about to set out. The second is said after the return. You have stood, bowed, and lost yourself in prostration. Now you come back, and you bear witness again. And this second testimony, he says, carries a claim. It is the claim of someone who has actually seen.

And then he says something honest. He says that this lofty state is not possible for persons like us. So what should we do with these words? He says that the right courtesy, for people like us, is to remember our shortcomings, our humility, our inability, and to turn to the threshold with a kind of shame, and plead.

And he gives the words of that plea. Here is the heart of it, a little shortened.

O Allah! Of the states of the holy men, the steps of the chosen, the perfection of the sincere, we have no share but a few words. Loving this world bars us from the presence of Your intimacy, unless You, with Your hidden kindness, help us, the helpless, so that we may rise from our sleep of negligence and find our way to Your holy presence.

So the *tashahhud*, for most of us, is not a boast. It is a testimony said with lowered eyes. I bear witness, and I know I have not yet seen as I should. Help me to see.

---

The Lantern of the Path has a short chapter on the *tashahhud*, and Imam Khomeini quotes it in full. Imam al-Sadiq begins like this.

The *tashahhud* is praise of God. So be His servant in your innermost being, as you are His servant in word and claim. Join the truthfulness of your tongue to the pure truthfulness of your innermost being.

That phrase is the heart of it. Join the truth of the tongue to the truth of the inside. Your tongue has just said, I am a witness. Let the inside be a witness too.

The Imam says that God created you a servant, and asked you to worship Him with your heart, your tongue, and your limbs. All three. The tongue says the words. The limbs sit still in the posture of a servant. And the heart is invited to agree.

Then he turns to the blessing on the Prophet. He says, join your prayer on Muhammad to your prayer to God. Join your obedience to him with your obedience to God. And join your witnessing of him with your witnessing of God. And he reminds you that God commanded the Prophet to ask forgiveness for you, and to intercede for you.

Imam Khomeini draws it together. He says that there is no admission to the holy court, and to the place of intimacy, without the help of the Messenger of God. So the blessing on Muhammad and his family is not a formality at the door on the way out. It is the hand that takes yours, at the moment you are about to leave.

---

Now we come to the *salam*.

Imam Khomeini paints a picture here that changes how this moment feels.

He says that in the prostration, the traveller was taken out of himself. He was absent from the world. Now, in the *tashahhud*, he comes back to himself. He becomes awake and attentive again. And so he greets those around him with the greeting of someone who has come back from a journey.

That is what the *salam* is. It is what you say when you come home after being away. You walk through the door, and you say, peace be upon you.

And so the question is, who are you greeting? There are three greetings. Let us take them one at a time.

---

The first greeting.

*As-salamu 'alayka*. Peace be upon you.

*Ayyuhan-nabiyyu*. O Prophet.

*Wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh*. And the mercy of God, and His blessings.

Why is the Prophet greeted first? Imam Khomeini says that when the traveller returns, from the oneness he was lost in, back to the many things of the world, the first thing he meets is the Prophet. Of all creation, the Prophet comes first. So he is the first to be greeted.

And Imam Khomeini says one more thing about this greeting. He says that the *salam* on the Prophet is always a truthful *salam*. Because the Prophet, on his whole journey to God, going up and coming down, was truly at peace. Nothing but God ever had a hold on him. When you wish him peace, you are wishing him what he already has. You can say it with your whole heart.

---

The second greeting.

*As-salamu 'alayna*. Peace be upon us.

*Wa 'ala 'ibadillahis-salihin*. And upon the righteous servants of God.

Here you wish peace on yourself. On us. You are included. And then the circle widens to all the righteous servants of God, whoever and wherever they are, in every age.

Amuli, quoting the gnostic again, says that the peace in these words is an overflowing from God, pouring down from the world of holiness. And he says it reaches souls when they set down their imperfections and put on something of the divine character. So the peace you ask for here is not just good wishes. It is a gift that comes down from above, and it falls where it finds room.

There is a small, touching thing in the Disciplines of the Prayer. The book opens with a letter from Imam Khomeini to his son Ahmad. It is full of fatherly advice and honest humility. And the very last line of that letter, before his signature, is this. Peace be upon the righteous servants of God. He signed off with the words of the *salam*.

---

The third greeting.

*As-salamu 'alaykum*. Peace be upon you all.

*Wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh*. And the mercy of God, and His blessings.

*'Alaykum* is plural. Upon all of you. If you are praying in a congregation, it reaches the people beside you. But even when you pray alone, it is plural.

Imam Khomeini says that after greeting the Prophet, the returning traveller turns to all the other beings, one by one and all together. So this is the moment you turn back toward everything. And the Lantern of the Path, as we will hear in a moment, speaks of your guardians, the angels who keep you. It is not hard to hear them in this plural as well.

So in three greetings, you have greeted the Prophet, then yourself and all the righteous, then everyone. The circle has opened outwards, all the way. You came into the prayer by turning your back on the world. You leave it by turning back to the world, and wishing it peace.

---

Now, what does *salam* actually mean?

The Lantern of the Path has a short chapter on it. Imam al-Sadiq says this.

The meaning of the *salam* at the end of the prayer is security. Whoever carries out the command of God and the way of His Prophet, with humility, is secure from the trials of this world, and free from the punishment of the next.

Then he says something remarkable. *Al-Salam*, Peace, is one of the names of God. He entrusted it to His creation, so that they would use it in their dealings, their trusts and their agreements, and for the soundness of their life together.

So when you say *salam*, you are using one of God's own names. And it has been left with you, as a trust, to be used among people.

Then the Imam says what it takes to say it honestly. If you want to put this *salam* in its proper place, then be mindful of God, and keep your faith, your heart and your mind sound. Do not stain them with wrongdoing. Let your guardians, the angels who keep you, be safe from you, so that you do not weary them with your behaviour. Let your friend be safe from you, and your enemy too. If those closest to you are not safe from you, strangers can hardly expect to be. And whoever does not put *salam* where it belongs, he says, has no peace in his *salam*. His greeting is false, even if he offers it to everyone he meets.

That is strong. But notice what it is really saying. The *salam* is a promise. When you say peace be upon you all, you are telling the world: you are safe from me.

---

Imam Khomeini takes this further. He says that the name *al-Salam* has been placed, as a trust, deep inside your own nature. And the task is to let it spread, through everything you are, inside and out. Into your habits, your beliefs, your character, and your actions.

And he says the way to this security is *taqwa*, which means being mindful of God, guarding yourself. He describes it in layers. Guarding the outward life from wrongdoing. Guarding the inner character from excess. Guarding the mind. And, at the deepest level, guarding the heart from seeing anything but God.

And then he says what happens to someone in whom all of this has grown. His dealings with friend and enemy alike are carried out in peace. And he says that enmity is actually uprooted from his heart, however hostile his enemies may be to him.

Imagine that. A heart with no enemy in it. That is where the *salam* is pointing.

Sayyid Haydar Amuli notices one more layer, hidden in the word itself. The *salam* is also called the *taslim*, which comes from the same root as surrender. Amuli says that by saying the *taslim*, the one praying hands over the whole affair to God. You did your best. You stood, bowed, and bowed lower still. Now you let go of it. You hand the prayer over. What becomes of it is His.

---

Imam Khomeini gives one gentle warning. He says that if, in the prayer, you never really left the house of your own self, then the *salam* has no meaning. There was no journey, so there is no return, and no homecoming greeting. It is only the tongue chattering.

Please do not hear that as a reason for anxiety. Hear it as an invitation. Even if only one moment of this prayer was really present, then you did go somewhere. And you can come back from it with a real greeting, however small.

---

To carry into your next prayer.

When you say *'abduhu*, His servant, let it land. The highest of all human beings is named first as a servant. Let that be enough for you too. A servant, at the door, glad to be there.

And when you come to the *salam*, imagine yourself coming home after a journey. Greet the Prophet first. Then yourself and all the righteous. Then everyone. And as you say *as-salamu 'alaykum*, peace be upon you all, make it a quiet promise. You are safe from me today.

In the next and final track, we stay on the prayer mat a little longer, and see what the prayer leaves behind, once it is over.
