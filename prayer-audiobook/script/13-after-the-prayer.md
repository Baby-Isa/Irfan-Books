# Track 13: After the Prayer

The *salam* has been said. You have greeted the Prophet, and yourself, and all the righteous, and everyone. The prayer is complete.

But do not get up just yet.

There is a short time after the prayer that the scholars treasure. It has a name, the *ta'qib*, which means what follows after. Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, calls it one of the confirmed recommended acts, and says it should not be neglected. He says it is especially recommended after the dawn prayer and the afternoon prayer. In this last track, we will stay on the prayer mat a little longer, and then follow the prayer out of the door, into the day.

---

The first thing, straight after the *salam*, is three *takbirs*. *Allahu akbar*, three times.

Imam Khomeini says that the great men of religion take care to raise their hands with these *takbirs*, the way they did at the opening of the prayer. Up near the ears, palms open, facing the qiblah. He is honest that this is hard to prove, though some narrations mention raising the hands three times. And he suggests that the three *takbirs*, followed by a short supplication that begins *la ilaha illallah, wahdahu wahdah*, there is no god but God, alone, alone, is enough.

But why say God is greater, right at the end?

Imam Khomeini offers a reason that I find very beautiful. He says that raising the hands here may be a way of pushing away the prayer you have just prayed. Not because it was worthless, but so that conceit does not creep in. So that you do not get up thinking, well, that was a good one.

Each *Allahu akbar* says: God is greater than this prayer of mine. Greater than my praise. Greater than anything I could describe, or offer. You came into the prayer by throwing the world behind you with your hands. Now you throw the prayer behind you as well, so that only God is left in front of you.

---

Then comes what Imam Khomeini calls the best of all the supplications after the prayer. The *tasbih* of Fatimat al-Zahra, the daughter of the Prophet.

The practice is simple. *Allahu akbar*, God is greater, thirty-four times. *Al-hamdu lillah*, praise belongs to God, thirty-three times. And *subhan Allah*, glory be to God, thirty-three times. Many people count them on a string of prayer beads, or on their fingers. It takes a minute or two.

Imam Khomeini says that the Prophet taught it to his daughter Fatimah. And he records two narrations about it. The first says that if there had been anything better, the Messenger of God would have taught that to Fatimah instead. The second is from Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, who said that these glorifications, after every prayer, every day, were more beloved to him than a thousand rak'ahs of prayer every day.

A thousand rak'ahs. For a minute of praise, said with care.

What should the heart be doing while the tongue counts? Imam Khomeini says that in these words after the prayer, you should think about your shortcomings. The moments in this prayer when you were absent. He says that, in the school of love, these lapses of attention count as sins. Not in the school of law. In the school of love. The way a person who loves notices every moment they were not really listening.

But he does not leave you there. He says that these words after the prayer open a new door to the mercy of God. They are a chance to make up, as far as you can, for what was missing. And when you say *al-hamdu lillah*, he says, know that even this praise is not your own achievement. It is a support, and a power, and a success, that has come to you from Him.

There is a picture elsewhere in the same book that helps here. Imam Khomeini says that the heart, at the start, is like a baby that cannot yet speak. The words of remembrance have to be placed on its tongue, again and again, and it is tiring for the one who teaches. But then one day the baby begins to talk. And after that, you simply follow what it says. That is what repetition is for. Thirty-four, thirty-three, thirty-three. You are teaching your heart to speak.

---

After the prayer is also a time to ask.

In the Spiritual Journey of the Mystics there is a narration from Imam Ali, from the Prophet. Whoever supplicates after the obligatory prayers, his supplication is answered. And the same book passes on some advice about how to ask. Begin with blessings on the Prophet and his family. Then ask for what you need. Then end with blessings on them again. Because, it says, those blessings at both ends are accepted, and God is too compassionate to accept the two ends of a supplication and leave the middle unanswered.

The Lantern of the Path has a chapter on supplication, and it adds a courtesy. Imam al-Sadiq says, consider the One on whom you call. How you call on Him, and why. And be careful, he says, because a person may ask for something that would destroy him, imagining that it will save him.

Then he says that true supplication is a melting of the heart in the presence of its Lord.

And he gives good news. When you ask sincerely, one of three things will happen. Either God will give you quickly what you asked for. Or He will store up something better for you. Or He will turn away from you a hardship that would have broken you.

And then the most surprising line of all. The Prophet reported that God said: if anyone is so taken up with remembering Me that he forgets to ask Me, I will give him better than what I give to those who ask.

---

That brings us to remembrance, which is what the whole prayer has been, and what it is meant to become.

The Lantern of the Path has a chapter on remembrance too. It begins in a way you might not expect. Imam al-Sadiq says, the one who truly remembers God is the one who obeys Him. And whoever forgets Him disobeys. So remembrance is not only words. It is a way of walking through the day. Make your heart the guide of your tongue, he says. Let the tongue not move unless the heart points the way.

And then he says something that turns everything around. He says, God remembers you, but He does not need you. His remembrance of you is more glorious, more complete and more ancient than your remembrance of Him.

More ancient. Before you ever thought of Him, He had already thought of you. Every prayer you have ever prayed was an answer to His remembrance of you. And the Imam warns that if you start to admire your own remembrance, it turns into pride, and pushes you further away. But if you see His remembrance of you, it makes you humble, and that humility brings you near.

---

And now the prayer has to leave the mat.

Imam Khomeini says that the morning is the moment when we enter the busy world, with all its many things, and the danger of becoming so occupied with people and tasks that we forget God. So after the dawn prayer especially, he says, turn to God, and ask for help for the day ahead. He notes that each day of the week has its own guardian among the Prophet and the Imams of his family, and he suggests turning to that day's guardian, and asking God, through their intercession, for protection in the day ahead.

In his Forty Hadith, Imam Khomeini is blunt about a prayer that stays on the mat. He says that a prayer which does not keep a person from wrongdoing, and which only makes him proud, is not worthy of being called a prayer at all. Of the person who prays a great deal and looks down on others, he says that if his worship had been true and sincere, it would have reformed him.

So the real test of the prayer is not on the mat. It is in the next conversation. The next driver who cuts in front of you.

In his letter to his son Ahmad, at the start of the Disciplines of the Prayer, Imam Khomeini writes, try hard to keep moving along the straight path, even with a limping leg. Serve people, because they are the creatures of God. And he says that the prophets and the friends of God did their daily work like everyone else. But they worked with God, and for God.

Even with a limping leg. That is all that is asked.

---

Imam Khomeini ends the Disciplines of the Prayer very simply. He says that he had meant to write about the hidden faults that spoil a prayer, like conceit. But he had touched on them in another book, and now he was too busy, and his mind too distracted. So he ends, he says, by admitting his inability and his shortcomings, and asking forgiveness for his mistakes.

And then he prays. He says, O God, You clothed us in the garment of existence out of pure generosity, before we had done any service, and without needing our worship. Our worship adds nothing to Your kingdom. Our disobedience takes nothing away from it. So treat us with Your kindness, and overlook our failings.

And he closes with words from a supplication handed down from Imam Ali and the Imams of his family. O God, if I am not worthy of Your mercy, You are worthy of granting me Your vast favour. O God, grant me to be completely devoted to You, and light the eyes of our hearts with the light of witnessing You.

And in a dedication written more than forty years later, he said that in all that time he had not yet perceived the secrets of the prayer. Because, he said, perceiving is not the same as conceiving, and performing is not the same as forming an idea. If he could say that, after writing the book, then none of us needs to be ashamed of being a beginner.

---

So let us look back, for a moment, at the road we have travelled.

We began with water. Washing the face, the arms, wiping the head and the feet, and letting the body remind the heart that it, too, needs to be clean. We thought about the time and the place, the clothing and the qiblah. We heard the call, the *adhan* and the *iqamah*, and stood up with an intention. We said *Allahu akbar*, and the world fell behind our raised hands.

Then we spent a long time with *al-Fatiha*, the opening, word by word, and with the surahs that follow it. We bowed, in *ruku'*. We went down to the earth in *sujud*, and laid our foreheads on it. We raised our empty hands in the *qunut*, and glorified God in the four glorifications. We sat, and bore witness. And we came home with the *salam*, wishing peace on the Prophet, on ourselves and the righteous, and on everyone.

From water to peace. That is the journey of every prayer, five times a day.

At the very end of the book, after everything else, there are a few lines of Imam Khomeini which could stand for this whole series. Know that the prayer, besides its form, has a meaning. Apart from its outside, it has an inside. And just as the outside has its disciplines, so does the inside. And if you keep them, they breathe into the prayer a heavenly spirit.

Inside and out. That is all we have been trying to do.

---

To carry into your next prayer.

When the *salam* is said, do not rise at once. Stay for the three *takbirs*, and let each one mean, God is greater than this prayer of mine. Then, as you count the *tasbih* of Fatimat al-Zahra, remember the words of the Lantern of the Path. His remembrance of you is older than your remembrance of Him. You have been remembered all along.

And one last thing, for the whole journey. Do not try to hold everything you have heard in this series at once. Pick one thing. Hold it for a while. Then pick another. The prayer will be there tomorrow, and the day after, waiting for you. He does not need it. He is inviting you, for your own sake.

---

Thank you for travelling with me. May your roads be safe, and may every prayer bring you a little closer to home.

*As-salamu 'alaykum wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh*. Peace be upon you, and the mercy of God, and His blessings.
