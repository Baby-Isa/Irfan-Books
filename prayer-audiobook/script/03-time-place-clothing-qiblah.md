# Track 3: Time, Place, Clothing and the Qiblah

We have made wudu. The water is still drying on our face and arms. But before we stand, there are four more things the prayer asks of us. The right time. A proper place. Clean and fitting clothes. And the right direction, the qiblah.

On the outside, these are conditions. Boxes to tick. Is it time yet? Is this place all right? Am I dressed properly? Which way is Makkah? But each of them, as we will see, has an inside as well.

Sayyid Haydar Amuli makes a striking remark here. He says that all the divine laws are built on three things. Time, place, and brotherhood. Certain acts of worship belong to certain times. Certain acts belong to certain places. And all of them bind the believers together. So let us begin with time.

---

Outwardly, each of the five prayers has its own time. The dawn prayer, between the first light and sunrise. The noon and afternoon prayers, once the sun has passed its height. The sunset and night prayers, once the sun has gone down. And within each time, the best part is the beginning.

Amuli explains why the time matters with a little parable. Imagine, he says, a man who dies and leaves his sons a treasure, buried somewhere. He tells them where. Count so many steps from a certain wall, and dig. Now, if the sons count one step too many, or one too few, they will not find it. The treasure is there. But only at that spot.

The times of the prayer, says Amuli, are like that. They are not arbitrary. Something is waiting at that moment that is not waiting at any other.

And Imam Khomeini, in the Forty Hadith, relates a narration from Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, peace be upon him, that brings this to life. When someone prays an obligatory prayer at the beginning of its time, and takes care over it, an angel carries it up to heaven, white and pure. And the prayer says to the one who prayed it: May God take care of you, as you took care of me.

But when someone delays it without excuse until its time has passed, and does not take care over it, it goes up black and dark. And it says: You neglected me. May God neglect you, as you neglected me.

The prayer, in that narration, is alive. It knows how it was treated. And it answers back.

---

Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, describes how the people who truly know God wait for the time of prayer. They watch for it, he says, with all their hearts. They wait eagerly, preparing themselves to meet Him. For them, the time of prayer is the time of private conversation with God.

Then he turns to the rest of us, and to himself, with his usual honesty. We, he says, treat worship as a heavy duty, and conversation with God as a burden.

So he tells some stories, to wake the heart up.

Some of the wives of the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, said this about him. He used to talk with us, and we with him. But when the time for prayer came, it was as if he did not know us, and we did not know him. His whole attention had turned to God.

And it is said that when the time for prayer came, Imam Ali, peace be upon him, would tremble. Someone asked him why. He said: The time has come for the trust that God offered to the heavens and the earth and the mountains. And they refused to carry it, and were afraid of it.

But Imam Khomeini is careful to say that the friends of God do not only meet the time of prayer with fear. Sometimes, he says, the beauty and mercy of God stir them, and they meet it with longing and with joy. We will hear more of that longing in the next track.

So the discipline of time, says Imam Khomeini, is fear and hope together. Look at your own smallness, and at the greatness of the One who is calling you, and you will feel awe. Then look at His mercy. That someone like you is invited at all, five times a day, with no benefit to Him. And you will feel hope.

---

Now, in the first track, we heard Imam Khomeini say that the presence of the heart needs unoccupied time. A time set aside for prayer, the way we set aside time for work. In the Forty Hadith he tells a story that shows what that looks like when someone truly takes it to heart.

The great scholar Sayyid Ibn Tawus, he says, used to celebrate every year the anniversary of the day he came of age. The day he became responsible for the prayer. Because on that day, God had given him permission to worship Him.

Permission. Not obligation. That is how he saw it. The prayer was not a weight that fell on him at a certain age. It was a door that opened. And he kept its anniversary like a feast.

---

Now the place.

Outwardly, the place where you pray should be lawful. Not taken from someone without their permission. And the spot where your forehead rests in prostration must be clean. We will come back to that spot, and the small piece of earth many of us pray on, when we reach the prostration.

Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, describes three places in which every one of us is praying, whether we know it or not.

The first is the earth itself. The whole world. He says that the one who travels to God should tell his heart that this world is a mosque. We were brought into it in order to worship. And so we are, in a sense, in retreat in this mosque for the whole of our lives. The way someone in a mosque does not leave except for a real need, and then comes back.

The second place is the body. Your body, he says, is also a mosque. The place where all your powers, your eyes, your hands, your tongue, come to bow. So it should be kept clean, and not handed over to Satan.

And the third place is the heart. Here, he says, the responsibility grows. Because we do not just keep this mosque clean. We build it. And we have to be careful what we build. A mosque can be built for the wrong reasons, and then it is not a place where God is worshipped at all.

Sayyid Haydar Amuli says the same thing in a single line, quoting the Prophet. The heart of the believer is the house of God. And he quotes a sacred saying in which God says: Neither the earth nor the heavens can contain Me. But the heart of the believing servant contains Me.

So when you look for a clean, quiet corner to pray in, you are tending the first and second mosque. And the prayer is asking you, gently, to tend the third.

---

The Lantern of the Path has a chapter on entering the mosque, and it fits here, wherever you happen to be praying. Imam al-Sadiq says this.

When you reach the door of the mosque, know that you have come to the door of a mighty King. Only the purified tread on His carpet. And only the truthful are allowed to sit with Him.

Then he says something that keeps us humble. If God treats you with His mercy, He accepts a little worship from you and gives a great reward. But if He treated you with strict justice, asking for full truthfulness and sincerity, He could reject your worship even if it were a great deal.

So, says the Imam, acknowledge your poverty before Him. Be like the poorest of His servants. Empty your heart of everything that keeps you from your Lord. And look to see in which register your name is written.

---

Now the clothing.

Outwardly, the clothes we pray in must be clean, and lawfully ours, and they must cover what needs to be covered. A woman covers more than a man. And a man should not pray in pure silk, or wearing gold.

The Lantern of the Path has a short chapter on dress, and Imam Khomeini quotes it in full. Imam al-Sadiq begins with a verse of the Qur'an. The clothing of *taqwa*, of mindfulness of God, that is the best.

Outward clothing, he says, is a blessing from God, to cover the nakedness of the children of Adam. It is an honour He gave to human beings and to no other creature. And the best of your clothes are the ones that do not distract you from God. The ones that bring you closer to remembering Him, and thanking Him. And not the ones that lead you to pride and showing off.

Then he says this, and it is worth hearing slowly. When you put on your clothes, remember that God covers your wrongdoing with His mercy.

Every time you get dressed. Every time you cover yourself. Remember that God has been covering you, all along. Covering the things about you that you would not want anyone to see.

And the Imam draws the lesson straight away. Do not expose anyone's faults, he says, when God has concealed worse things in you. Busy yourself with your own faults.

---

Imam Khomeini takes this idea of covering a step deeper. He says that the human being is like a set of garments, one inside the other. Our clothes cover our body. The body covers the soul. And the soul covers the heart, and the heart covers the spirit, and so on, deeper and deeper.

And just as the body has its nakedness, which must be covered in the prayer, the soul has its nakedness too. Its bad habits. Its ugly traits. Its pride. Uncovering those in the presence of God, he says, is far more shameful than uncovering the body. He quotes the saying: God does not look at your outward forms. He looks into your hearts.

So what do we do? We cannot cover these things ourselves. Imam Khomeini says that we should place ourselves under two of God's names. The Concealer, and the Forgiver. Ask Him to cover what we cannot.

He has one more warning about clothes, and it is subtle. Pride does not only come from expensive clothes, he says. It can come just as easily from shabby ones. The person who dresses roughly, so that people will see how humble and unworldly he is, may be even worse off than the one who dresses in silk. And he quotes Imam al-Sadiq. God is angered by two kinds of fame. Fame through clothes. And fame through prayer.

The point is not to dress in any particular way. It is simply not to dress for anyone but God.

---

And finally, the qiblah.

Outwardly, you turn your face towards the Ka'bah in Makkah. Wherever you are in the world, there is one point you face. And so does everyone else who prays.

Imam Khomeini says that this simple act has two sides. You turn away from every scattered direction. And you turn towards one single point. And he says that in doing this, you are showing outwardly two things that God placed in every human heart, without exception. The first is that we dislike what is imperfect. And the second is that we love what is perfect.

Everybody, he says, is chasing perfection. The person who wants wealth thinks perfection is in wealth. The person who wants power thinks it is in power. They are all looking for the same thing. They are only looking in the wrong place. And the prophets came, he says, to show people where it really is.

He quotes a line of poetry by an Arab poet called Labid. Everything other than God is vain. And every pleasure, without doubt, will pass. When the Prophet heard it, he said: this is the truest poetry the Arabs have ever recited.

And Imam Khomeini recalls two sayings from the Qur'an, both spoken by the prophet Abraham. The first, when he watched the stars and the moon and the sun go down, one after another. I do not love the things that set. And the second, when he turned away from them all. I have turned my face towards the One who created the heavens and the earth.

That is the qiblah. Turning away from everything that sets, towards the One who does not.

---

Imam al-Sadiq, in the Lantern of the Path, has words about what to do with the heart as you face the qiblah. We will come to them properly when we stand for the prayer, two tracks from now.

There is a passage in Sayyid Haydar Amuli that makes the qiblah almost tender. He is quoting one of the mystics, explaining why we must not turn away from the qiblah during the prayer. It is when we turn away, he says, that Satan slips in and steals the heart. And then he adds: if the lover were truly paying attention, he would never turn his face from the qiblah anyway.

So the body faces Makkah. And the heart faces the One it loves.

---

To carry into your next prayer.

When you turn to face the qiblah, remember the words of Abraham. I do not love the things that set. Let everything that will pass away fall behind you, just for these few minutes.

And when you get dressed, any time today, remember the words of the Lantern of the Path. God covers your wrongdoing with His mercy.

In the next track, we hear the call that gathers all of this together. The *adhan* and the *iqamah*.
