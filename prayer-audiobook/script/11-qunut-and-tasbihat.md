# Track 11: Qunut and the Four Glorifications

In this track we visit two moments of the prayer that we stepped past on the way to the prostration.

The first is in the second unit of the prayer. You have recited *al-Fatiha* and the second surah. You have not yet gone down into the bow. And in that small space, you lift your hands and open them towards the sky. This is the *qunut*.

The second is in the third and fourth units of the longer prayers. Instead of *al-Fatiha*, you may say four short phrases of glory. These are the four glorifications.

One is a moment of asking. The other is a moment of praising. Let us take them in turn.

---

First, the *qunut*.

The word means something like standing devoutly before God. Outwardly, it is simple. In the second unit, after the recitation and before the bow, you raise your hands in front of your face, palms open and turned upwards, and you make a supplication. Any supplication. It is recommended, not obligatory, though Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, says it is not something one should neglect, and that some scholars even held it to be obligatory.

He also says that the supplication may be in any language, Arabic or not, though Arabic is better. So if, in the *qunut*, your heart wants to speak to God in English, it may.

Now, the hands. Why open, and why upwards?

In his Forty Hadith, Imam Khomeini looks at a saying of the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family. Make it your practice to raise your hands in prayer, and to turn them. He thinks it most likely refers to the opening *takbir*, with the palms turned towards the qiblah. But he gives a second possibility. Perhaps, he says, it means raising the hands in the *qunut*, and turning the palms upwards towards the sky. And he adds that this is simply how the devout pray. They know no other way of making the *qunut*. Just lifting the hands any old way, he says, is not enough.

The palms turned up to the sky. That is the posture of someone who is waiting to receive.

The Spiritual Journey of the Mystics, in a chapter on raising the hands in supplication, relates that when the Prophet stood to pray and ask, he would stretch out his hands exactly as the poor and destitute stretch out their hands for food. And it relates that Imam al-Sadiq, peace be upon him, taught that when you beseech God, you should hold your palms towards the heavens.

So that is the *qunut*. Two empty hands, held open, like someone in need. Because that is exactly what we are.

---

What should you say?

Imam Khomeini mentions that the jurists recommend a supplication called *du'a al-faraj*, the supplication of relief, which begins *la ilaha illallahul-halimul-karim*. There is no god but God, the Forbearing, the Generous. He points out that, short as it is, it gathers into itself almost all the remembrances of the prayer. It declares His oneness, glorifies Him and praises Him. It calls on His great names. And it sends blessings on the Prophet and his family.

He also suggests some lines from the whispered prayer of the month of Sha'ban, the prayer of Imam Ali, peace be upon him. Especially the line: O God, grant me complete devotion to You. Complete devotion. Cutting off from everything, and turning wholly to Him.

But Imam Khomeini's real advice is not about which words. It is about what we ask for.

He says that the *qunut* is a moment when God opens the door of supplication to His servant in a special way. So the servant should take care what he brings to that door. He should ask for knowledge of God, for intimacy with Him, for the door of nearness to be opened. And then he says, quite bluntly, that in such a state, to talk about the stomach and worldly matters is a shortcoming and a loss.

That may sound severe. We all have worries we want to bring to God. But think of it this way. You have been granted an audience with the King. You have a minute. Would you spend it asking for a sandwich?

The Lantern of the Path, in its chapter on supplication, says something that softens this and deepens it at the same time. It quotes God saying, in a sacred tradition: if anyone is distracted from asking Me by his remembrance of Me, I will give him better than what I give to those who ask.

So you can bring your worries. But you may find that when your heart is really turned towards Him, the list grows shorter. And that is no loss.

---

Now the inner meaning of the *qunut*, and here Imam Khomeini becomes very tender.

He says the *qunut* is giving up everything other than God, and turning completely towards Him, and holding out the empty hand of need to the One who needs nothing.

Then he addresses the reader directly, almost like a parent. Dear one, he says, now that you are far from your homeland, cut off from good company, held captive in this dark house full of trouble, do not spin around yourself like a silkworm.

The silkworm spins its thread round and round itself, until it is shut inside a cocoon of its own making. That is what we do when all our prayers are about ourselves. Our needs, our plans, our anxieties, wound tighter and tighter.

And he goes on. God has kneaded your nature with the light of knowledge and the fire of love. Do not put that fire out with the dust and ashes of this low world.

And then comes the hope. If you turn your attention to your original home, he says, and ask God to let you be devoted to Him, and lay out before Him, from your heart, how weak and helpless you are and how much you feel the separation, then perhaps help will reach you from the unseen. For it is His habit to be generous, and His custom to do good.

Imam Khomeini also compares the *qunut* with the prostration. He says they are like two sides of one thing. In the *qunut*, you turn towards your own lowliness as a servant, while remembering His might. In the prostration, you turn towards His might, while remembering your lowliness. One face looks up. The other looks down. Both are standing in the same truth.

---

There is a story about the *qunut* in the Spiritual Journey of the Mystics. It is about the author of the original book himself, the great scholar Haj Mirza Javad Agha Malaki Tabrizi.

One of his close friends described how he prayed at night. He would weep in his bed. He would go out into the yard, look up at the sky, and recite the verse: in the creation of the heavens and the earth are signs. He would lean his head against the wall and cry. He wept while making his wudu, sitting by the pool. And when he stood on the prayer mat, his friend says, he became deeply moved and wept a great deal during the prayer. And especially in the *qunut*.

Especially in the *qunut*. That is the moment when the hands are open and the heart has nothing left to hide behind.

You may not weep. Most of us don't. That is all right. But it is worth knowing that for those who loved God most, this little moment with open hands was where the heart broke open too.

---

Now let us move forward to the third and fourth units of the prayer.

In the Ja'fari prayer, in these last units, you may recite *al-Fatiha* alone, quietly. Or, in its place, you may say the four glorifications, also quietly. *Subhan Allah, wal-hamdu lillah, wa la ilaha illallah, wallahu akbar*. Glory be to God, and praise be to God, and there is no god but God, and God is greater. Saying them once is enough. Saying them three times is recommended.

Imam Khomeini, in the Disciplines of the Prayer, calls these the four pillars. Let us take them one at a time.

---

*Subhan Allah*. Glory be to God.

This, he says, is declaring God free of every description. It is the same glorifying we met in the bow and the prostration. Whatever we say about Him, He is beyond it. And he says that this comes first, before any praise, as a kind of fortress. Before we dare to praise Him, we enter the fortress of glorification. We remind ourselves that nothing we are about to say can contain Him.

He quotes a line of the poet Hafiz here, which I love. No one can net the phoenix. So take down your net. There will be nothing in it but wind.

And he adds that the highest knowledge of those who truly know God is to know their own inability to know Him.

*Wal-hamdu lillah*. And praise be to God.

Imam Khomeini says that this is why the four glorifications can take the place of *al-Fatiha*, which also begins with praise. All praise belongs to God, because every good thing anywhere comes from Him. And he says something here that I find freeing. When we really see this, he says, we are taken out from under the burden of being indebted to creatures. Every kindness anyone has shown you was, at its root, His kindness. You can thank people, and you should. But your heart does not have to bow to them.

*Wa la ilaha illallah*. And there is no god but God.

He says this follows naturally from the praise. If all praise belongs to Him, then all worship must belong to Him too. There is no real power acting in the world except His. And there is no one else to be worshipped. So, he says, all the idols are to be broken to pieces. The idols we carry around inside. The approval we chase. The things we cannot do without.

*Wallahu akbar*. And God is greater.

And then, at the end, having glorified and praised and declared His oneness, we say: and God is greater. Greater than what? Greater, Imam Khomeini says, than any description. Even greater than the three things we have just said. So the last word is a confession. We have done our best to praise You, and it falls short. You are greater than our praise.

---

Why say them three times?

Imam Khomeini says, in another place in the Disciplines of the Prayer, that repetition with a calm heart is how the heart learns to speak. At first, he says, we teach the heart the words, slowly and quietly, the way one teaches a child. Then, gradually, the tongue of the heart begins to speak by itself, and the outer tongue follows it.

He quotes Imam al-Sadiq from the Lantern of the Path. Let your heart be the qiblah of your tongue. Do not move your tongue unless your heart gives the order.

And of the four glorifications in particular, he says that by repeating them, the heart is clothed in the garment of remembrance, and takes off its own old garment, which is the garment of distance from God.

There is one more thing worth knowing as you say these words. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, in Inner Secrets of the Path, reminds us of the Qur'an's words. There is not a single thing but glorifies Him with His praise, but you do not understand their glorification. And Imam Khomeini, in his Forty Hadith, says that this glorifying by all things is real, even in plants and in what we think of as lifeless things. Someone whose inner eye was open, he says, would hear the whole chorus.

So when you say *subhan Allah* in the third unit of your prayer, you are not alone. You are adding one small voice to a song that every tree and every stone is already singing.

---

To carry into your next prayer.

In the *qunut*, before you say anything, just hold your hands open for one moment. Empty. Like someone in need. Remember Imam Khomeini's silkworm, and let the supplication turn outwards towards Him, instead of round and round yourself.

And in the four glorifications, give each phrase its own breath. Glory. Praise. No god but Him. And then, God is greater, greater even than what I have just said.

In the next track, we sit for the testimony, the *tashahhud*, and then we close the prayer with the greeting of peace, the *salam*.
