**Framing across the seven chapters: not yet varied.** Four of eight open "In one/two of his lectures, Sheikh Bahmanpour …" (Chs 2, 3, 4, 7), and seven of eight open with the Sheikh's name as the first words. The rewrites below open four of them on the story's subject (the man, the companion, the Qur'an's scene, ʿUnwan) and put the attribution in the second or third sentence, so that the reader meets the Sheikh in four different ways: "began his first lecture with", "traced the route", "in Sheikh Bahmanpour's telling", "the lectures explain". No story should say "in two of his lectures"; the count means nothing to the reader.

## Chapter One: "The man who asked what was enough" (lines 27–29)

**Does the story improve the exemplar?** Yes, modestly, and only in the trimmed form below. The exemplar's first section moves from an abstract claim ("a believer who prays, fasts … can hope for jannah") straight to four verses. The story gives that claim one concrete scene, and it is the Sheikh's own opening to the whole course, so it belongs in the chapter that opens the book. As added, it does not improve the exemplar: it is 125 words between two short paragraphs that form a pair ("The first is … The second claim goes further"), and its last sentence says what the paragraph before has just said ("This claim is not contested").

1. **Earns its place?** Yes: the reader finishes it knowing the first claim as a fact about the Prophet's own answer, not a scholar's concession.
2. **Told well?** The middle is fine. Two faults. (a) "From this answer the lecture drew its opening question: if the obligatory acts alone are enough for jannah, why should anyone take on the hard work of ʿirfan?" then "The Prophet's answer is the reason the first claim is not contested: the obligatory acts are enough for salvation." Two closing sentences, the second restating line 25. (b) "began the case for ʿirfan with a story about a man who came to the Prophet" is a long run-up before anything happens.
3. **Placement and links.** Right place, wrong length. The second-claim paragraph still reads as a pick-up ("The second claim goes further") if the box is short enough that the reader still holds "The first is …" in mind.
4. **Rewrite** (about 90 words):

Before:
> Sheikh Bahmanpour began the case for ʿirfan with a story about a man who came to the Prophet ﷺ. The man said that he had no eagerness for the night prayer (*salat al-layl*), the recommended fasts, and the like. He asked whether he would go to jannah if he prayed the five daily prayers, fasted in Ramadan, paid his zakat, and went on hajj once. The Prophet said yes: if he did these things, he would go to jannah. From this answer the lecture drew its opening question: if the obligatory acts alone are enough for jannah, why should anyone take on the hard work of ʿirfan? The Prophet's answer is the reason the first claim is not contested: the obligatory acts are enough for salvation.

After:
> A man once came to the Prophet ﷺ and said that he had no eagerness for the night prayer (*salat al-layl*), the recommended fasts, and the like. If he prayed the five daily prayers, fasted in Ramadan, paid his zakat, and went on hajj once, would he go to jannah? The Prophet said yes. Sheikh Bahmanpour opened his first lecture with this story and drew his question from the Prophet's answer: if the obligatory acts are enough for jannah, why take on the hard work of ʿirfan? The first claim is the Prophet's yes. The question is the one this chapter answers.

5. **Consequential edit to the exemplar paragraph (line 25), recommended.** Cut "Sheikh Bahmanpour accepts it as fair for ordinary believers." The box now shows his acceptance, the cut avoids naming him in two consecutive units (LESSONS 7), and it keeps Ch 1 under the naming cap: by hand I count 13 "Sheikh Bahmanpour / the Sheikh" in the chapter after the addition, against a cap of about 13 for its length. If the user would rather the exemplar's own sentences stay untouched, leave line 25 and accept the double naming.

## Chapter Two: "A thousand doors of knowledge" (lines 119–121)

1. **Earns its place?** Yes, for one reason: it is the only place the book shows *how* knowledge normally travels (image → words → image), which is what makes "from heart to heart" mean something. Without that contrast, "heart to heart" is a phrase.
2. **Told well?** The frame is weak at both ends. "In one of his lectures, Sheikh Bahmanpour asked whether knowledge can pass from one heart to another" repeats line 117 ("passes 'from heart to heart', in the Sheikh's words"). The last sentence, "In the lecture, this is the kind of knowledge the ʿurafaʾ have: given to the heart, not built up from words", repeats the section point and step 7. The report itself is told cleanly and matches L03 ¶10.
3. **Placement and links.** It sits after the section's last step and before a new heading, so it works as a closing illustration. Acceptable; a box does not need its own ladder step.
4. **Rewrite** (about 110 words):

Before:
> In one of his lectures, Sheikh Bahmanpour asked whether knowledge can pass from one heart to another. We know how it passes between minds: a speaker turns an image into words, and the words raise the image again in the listener's mind. Of knowledge passing between hearts we have no experience. In answer, he recalled a report from Imam ʿAli (as). When the Prophet ﷺ lay on his deathbed, a thousand doors of knowledge were opened in Imam ʿAli's heart, and from each door a thousand more opened. None of it came through language; it passed from heart to heart. In the lecture, this is the kind of knowledge the ʿurafaʾ have: given to the heart, not built up from words.

After:
> We know how knowledge passes from one mind to another, and Sheikh Bahmanpour traced the route. The speaker has an image in mind and turns it into words. The words pass through the listener's ears and nerves and raise the same image in the listener's mind. Everything goes through language. Of knowledge passing between hearts we have no such experience, and the lecture answered with a report from Imam ʿAli (as). When the Prophet ﷺ lay on his deathbed, a thousand doors of knowledge were opened in Imam ʿAli's heart, and from each door a thousand more. None of it came through language: it passed from one heart to another, which is how the ʿurafaʾ receive theirs.

## Chapter Three: "The young man who saw paradise" (lines 77–79)

1. **Earns its place?** Yes, and it is placed exactly right: Duʿa ʿArafah has just said God "lit the illuminations in the hearts of Your intimate servants", and here is one such heart, with the Prophet saying so in almost the same words. The reader finishes it knowing that the light the prayer asks for has been given to an ordinary companion.
2. **Told well?** Three faults. (a) "In two of his lectures" is the formula and a needless count. (b) The box stitches the Sheikh's version (L11 ¶43: the Prophet hushes him) to al-Kafi's (the Prophet praises him aloud to the companions). Both can stand, but the join needs to be visible in one clause, not a second mini-telling ("In its version, the Prophet turned to his companions and said…"). (c) The Sheikh is named twice in one box ("Sheikh Bahmanpour told of…", then "The lesson drawn in the lectures" is fine, but see the rewrite: keep one name). (d) The last sentence over-claims: "Harithah's sight was the kind the Munajat asks for" — the Munajat asks to look at *God*; Harithah saw paradise and hell. The true link is to Duʿa ʿArafah, the quotation immediately above.
3. **Fidelity note.** L07 ¶38 names him "Zayd ibn Harith" and has the Prophet approving; L11 ¶43 is unnamed and has the hushing. The box rightly goes around the garbled name (D39) and takes the name from al-Kafi via `light.md` p. 19, where the quoted line is verbatim. Good.
4. **Rewrite** (about 130 words):

Before:
> In two of his lectures, Sheikh Bahmanpour told of a young companion whom the Prophet ﷺ asked how he was. The young man answered that he could see paradise and hell, and could tell which of the companions were in each. The Prophet hushed him and told him not to speak of such things, but said that he knew he was truthful. The lesson drawn in the lectures is that the young man was not set apart from the other companions. He had purified his soul, and anyone who goes through that purification can see what others cannot. The hadith collection al-Kafi names him as Harithah ibn Malik al-Ansari. In its version, the Prophet turned to his companions and said, "This is a man whose heart has been illuminated with the light of faith by God" [@light, p. 19]. Harithah's sight was the kind the Munajat asks for: it came through the heart rather than the eyes, and God had lit it.

After:
> The Prophet ﷺ once asked a young companion how he was. The young man answered that he could see paradise and hell, and could tell which of the companions were in each. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's telling, the Prophet hushed him and told him not to speak of such things, but said that he knew he was truthful. The lesson drawn in the lectures is that the young man was not set apart from the other companions. He had purified his soul, and anyone who does the same can see what others cannot. The hadith collection al-Kafi names him as Harithah ibn Malik al-Ansari, and records the Prophet saying of him, "This is a man whose heart has been illuminated with the light of faith by God" [@light, p. 19]. His sight came through the heart, and God had lit it, as Duʿa ʿArafah says of the friends of God.

## Chapter Four: "Musa asks to see God" (lines 131–133)

1. **Earns its place?** Yes, for step 6 ("Even then the soul cannot encompass God: some understanding is possible, complete knowledge is not"): a prophet to whom God spoke was still refused the sight he asked for. But be honest about what it is. In L04 ¶56 the Sheikh gives it half a sentence ("that is what Prophet Musa was asking god for. Look at me okay … and Allah said you cannot"). The box is the book retelling Q 7:143. That is allowed (the Qur'an is held, and the brief lists the candidate), but the frame must not suggest the Sheikh told the scene.
2. **Told well?** The Qur'anic retelling is clean and the quotations are exact. Two faults. (a) At 185 words it is the longest of the eight, and the repentance line ("Immaculate are You! I turn to You in penitence") is a detail the point does not need. (b) The last sentence tangles (TONE 38) and introduces a second limit: "so while the soul is in the body some perception of God is open to it and a further degree is not." Step 6 is about the limit *even at the meeting*; this sentence is about the limit *in the body*. The chain has one limit (some understanding, never complete), and the box should land on that one.
3. **Placement and links.** Right place: after Khomeini's limit (line 129) and before step 7 (line 135), which the story leads into (what Musa perceived in this life is the measure of what he perceives beyond it).
4. **Rewrite** (about 125 words):

Before:
> In one of his lectures, Sheikh Bahmanpour turned to the Prophet Musa (as) to show a limit on perceiving God in this life. The Qur'an tells the scene in Surah al-Aʿraf. Musa comes to the meeting God appointed for him, and his Lord speaks to him. Musa then asks for more: "My Lord, show [Yourself] to me, that I may look at You!" God answers, "You shall not see Me" ([لَن تَرَىٰنِي]{lang=ar}). He tells Musa to look at the mountain instead: if it stays in its place, Musa will see Him. God discloses Himself to the mountain and levels it, and the prophet falls down in a swoon. When he comes round, he says, "Immaculate are You! I turn to You in penitence" (Q 7:143). Musa's request, as the lecture explains, was for a degree of perceiving God that cannot be reached in this body. Even Musa, to whom God spoke, was refused that sight, so while the soul is in the body some perception of God is open to it and a further degree is not.

After:
> Sheikh Bahmanpour points to the story of Musa (as) for this limit, and the Qur'an tells it in Surah al-Aʿraf. Musa comes to the meeting God appointed for him, and his Lord speaks to him. He then asks for more: "My Lord, show [Yourself] to me, that I may look at You!" God answers, "You shall not see Me" ([لَن تَرَىٰنِي]{lang=ar}), and tells him to look at the mountain instead: if it stays in its place, Musa will see Him. God discloses Himself to the mountain and levels it, and Musa falls down in a swoon (Q 7:143). As the lecture explains, Musa was asking for a degree of perceiving God that cannot be reached in this body. A prophet to whom God spoke was given some perception of Him and refused the rest, and the lectures set that same limit for every soul.

## Chapter Five (a): the churchman's question (line 54, prose paragraph; ladder step 8)

1. **Earns its place?** Yes. Step 7 asserts that the unseen worlds "surround this one, but in depth and not in distance"; this paragraph is the only evidence for "surround" from the tradition. The reader finishes it with Imam ʿAli's (as) own answer in hand.
2. **Told well?** Short and plain. Two faults. (a) "Imam ʿAli (as) also spoke of this world as surrounded" is an awkward pick-up ("spoke of … as surrounded"). (b) The last sentence dangles: the reader is not told why hell surrounding the faithless bears on the hereafter surrounding this world. One clause fixes it.
3. **Fidelity.** The transcript says "Christian Cardinal"; the book says "churchman". Right call (a cardinal in Imam ʿAli's day is an anachronism the reader would catch; D39 goes around it). Make sure the reply to the brief records the change.
4. **Framing.** "in a story Sheikh Bahmanpour tells" is the one story that does not open on the Sheikh. Keep that.
5. **Rewrite:**

Before:
> Imam ʿAli (as) also spoke of this world as surrounded, in a story Sheikh Bahmanpour tells. A Christian churchman once came to him with questions. One was whether the hereafter lies inside this world, or this world inside the hereafter. Imam ʿAli (as) answered that this world lies inside the hereafter. This world is the smaller one, and the hereafter surrounds it. The lectures set the answer beside the verse that says hell surrounds the faithless (Q 9:49).

After:
> Imam ʿAli (as) said the same of this world and the next, in a story Sheikh Bahmanpour tells. A Christian churchman once came to him with questions. One was whether the hereafter lies inside this world, or this world inside the hereafter. Imam ʿAli (as) answered that this world lies inside the hereafter: this world is the small one, and the hereafter surrounds it. The lectures set the answer beside a verse that says hell, which belongs to the hereafter, surrounds the faithless (Q 9:49).

## Chapter Five (b): "Ibn ʿArabi and Surah Yasin" (lines 148–150)

1. **Earns its place?** Yes. The section's point is that what we do now shapes the image body, and Shomali's "rewards are the realities of those actions made manifest" (line 144) is abstract until a reader sees a recited surah stand up as a man. The story makes the abstract claim concrete.
2. **Told well?** The best of the eight: a clear arc (illness, attackers, defender, the question, the answer, waking to the father in tears), plain sentences, every detail from L13 ¶35–36, the Sheikh's aside ("not a great miracle") rightly dropped. Two small faults. (a) "The lecture draws a general point from it" is throat-clearing before the point. (b) The closing line stops at "that image is as real as the figure the boy saw", which is the Sheikh's point about barzakh in general; one more clause ties it to the section's point about the image body.
3. **Placement.** After the section's conclusion (line 146), before the next heading; works as a closing illustration. The section has no other box (LESSONS 17 satisfied).
4. **Framing.** "Sheikh Bahmanpour tells a story that the great mystic Ibn ʿArabi told of his own childhood" is varied and natural. Keep.
5. **Rewrite** (last three sentences only):

Before:
> Ibn ʿArabi asked who he was. The figure answered that he was Surah Yasin, his protector. When the boy came round, he found his father at his bedside in tears. His father had just finished reciting Surah Yasin for his recovery, and the boy had not known. The lecture draws a general point from it. Even a lie or a word of comfort, which we take for mere words, has an image in the world of images, and that image is as real as the figure the boy saw.

After:
> The boy asked who he was. The figure answered that he was Surah Yasin, his protector. When the boy came round, he found his father at his bedside in tears. His father had just finished reciting Surah Yasin for his recovery, and the boy had not known. The lecture's point is general. A lie or a word of comfort seems to be only words, yet each has an image in the world of images, as real as the figure the boy saw. That is how our deeds shape the image body: each of them already has a form in the world the boy saw into.

## Chapter Six: the light of his wudu (line 91, prose paragraph; ladder step 7)

1. **Earns its place?** Yes. Step 6 says a false light is mistaken for God; step 7 adds that even a real light that comes through the law is mistaken for Him, which is a sharper point and the one the section needs before Tabatabaʾi's "preliminary to an enduring condition" (line 93).
2. **Told well?** Yes. 95 words, one scene, no suspense, the garbled name in L06 ¶67 ("by Aziz") rightly left out, and the ending states the point plainly: "The light of God was unknown to the disciples, so they took this one for His."
3. **Placement and links.** The first sentence ("The same mistake can be made over a light that is real and comes through the law") picks up line 89 exactly, and line 93 ("Even the experiences that come on the true path are not the goal") picks it up in turn.
4. **Framing.** "The Sheikh tells of a great mystic whose chain of teachers went back to Imam al-Rida (as)" is varied.

**Keep as is.**

## Chapter Seven: "ʿUnwan al-Basri at the door of Imam al-Sadiq" (lines 43–45)

1. **Earns its place?** Yes, for step 4 ("a beginner needs a teacher only to say which practices to begin with"): the Imam's first counsels to a seeker are rules for eating, which is exactly the "narrower" need line 41 describes, and it sits between the Qadi paragraph (which practices) and Tabatabaʾi (when a teacher is needed). The story also carries patience before being taught, which is a bonus and not a distraction.
2. **Told well?** The arc is clear and the eating rules match L08 ¶78–79. Three faults. (a) "In two of his lectures, Sheikh Bahmanpour told the hadith" is the formula. (b) "because ʿUnwan was not yet at a level where he could be taught" presents the Sheikh's gloss (L08 ¶77: "Because of course he was not that level") as the Imam's stated reason; keep the Sheikh's words apart from the Imam's (LESSONS 20). (c) At 190 words it is near the ceiling; "ʿUnwan persisted" and "He prayed that God would soften the heart…" say the same thing twice.
3. **Placement.** Right. The closing sentence ("The teacher ʿUnwan had to wait and pray for began by telling him which practices to start with") links back to line 41 and is a plain statement, not an aphorism. Keep it.
4. **Rewrite** (about 155 words):

Before:
> In two of his lectures, Sheikh Bahmanpour told the hadith of ʿUnwan al-Basri. ʿUnwan was an old man, near the end of his life, when he asked Imam al-Sadiq (as) to teach him. The sixth Imam at first turned him away, saying that he was busy with words of remembrance he recited at every hour of the day. He told ʿUnwan to go and learn from Malik ibn Anas and the other scholars of Medina, because ʿUnwan was not yet at a level where he could be taught. ʿUnwan persisted. He prayed that God would soften the heart of Imam al-Sadiq (as) towards him, so that he could learn something. When he went back, the Imam received him warmly and began to advise him. Among the first counsels were rules for eating: do not eat unless you are hungry, and when you do eat, eat only what is lawful and begin in the name of God. The teacher ʿUnwan had to wait and pray for began by telling him which practices to start with.

After:
> ʿUnwan al-Basri was an old man, near the end of his life, when he asked Imam al-Sadiq (as) to teach him. Sheikh Bahmanpour tells the hadith. The sixth Imam at first turned him away. He was busy, he said, with words of remembrance he recited at every hour of the day, and ʿUnwan should go and learn from Malik ibn Anas and the other scholars of Medina. The lectures explain that ʿUnwan was not yet at a level where the Imam could teach him. ʿUnwan did not give up: he prayed that God would soften the heart of Imam al-Sadiq (as) towards him, so that he could learn something. When he went back, the Imam received him warmly and began to advise him. Among the first counsels were rules for eating: do not eat unless you are hungry, and when you do eat, eat only what is lawful and begin in the name of God. The teacher ʿUnwan had to wait and pray for began by telling him which practices to start with.
