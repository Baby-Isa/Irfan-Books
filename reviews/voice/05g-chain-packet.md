# Chain packet 05g

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

[quotation]

¶1. These words come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, the prayer of the month of Shaʿban. The one who prays admits that neglect has put them to sleep, unready to meet God. Knowing His generosity has woken them. The prayer uses sleep as a picture of neglect and sets it beside the meeting with God.

¶2. The tradition also speaks of real sleep in the same breath as death. Lantern of the Path, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), calls sleep "the brother of death". Death is what we stand before at a graveside. There we say that the dead are now in barzakh (the world between this life and the resurrection) and many of us picture a waiting room somewhere far away.

¶3. Sheikh Bahmanpour teaches otherwise: at death "we don't go anywhere". The dead are not far away and the reason lies in what the soul is. The soul is the one thing we know from the inside, so the way to God runs through it and how deep it goes matters. This chapter sets out the soul and its worlds. It takes four steps: the worlds that creation holds, how the soul passes into them, how our deeds give the soul a form there and how the self that does those deeds can change. Together they show that what the soul finally becomes is, by God's grace, in our hands.

## Section 2: One world at three depths

**Point:** Creation has three worlds, one inside another, and none of them is far away.

¶4. Let's start with a claim of the mystics: the human soul is the whole of creation in small, a compact copy of everything that exists. If that is so, the soul can serve as a map of creation. Each kind of thing the soul holds has its counterpart in creation on a larger scale and each such kind makes up a world of its own. By looking at the kinds of thing the soul holds, we can learn the kinds of world that creation holds.

¶5. The soul holds three kinds of thing, because it knows in three ways: through the senses, through the imagination and through the intellect. Each way of knowing meets its own kind of thing. Taking them one at a time shows the three worlds.

¶6. The senses meet things made of matter: things with weight that take up space, such as a table, a hand or the rain. These make up the first world, the material world (ʿalam al-mulk). Because our senses witness it, it is also called the witnessed world. The other two worlds are called unseen. They are unseen not because they are far away but because the ordinary eye does not perceive them without training.

¶7. The imagination holds the second kind of thing. When we picture a friend's face, the picture has a shape and a colour, yet it weighs nothing and is made of nothing material. A dream does the same more vividly. Creation holds things of this kind on a larger scale, in the world of images (ʿalam al-mithal). Like the picture in our mind, that world has shape, size and colour and no matter at all. The difference is that the world of images is not inside anyone's mind. It is part of God's creation and it exists whether or not anyone imagines it.

¶8. The intellect holds the third kind of thing: meanings, with no shape and no matter. Every person we have met has a particular height, voice and face. Yet the mind can grasp "a human being" with none of these features and no such featureless person exists anywhere outside the mind. Creation holds pure meanings of this kind in the world of intellects (ʿalam al-ʿuqul). The angels who live there are pure intellect, attached to nothing material.

¶9. Creation therefore has three worlds, one for each of the soul's ways of knowing. The world of images stands between the other two as a middle world. That is why it is also called barzakh, "a barrier". Pure intellect and matter cannot meet directly, so a middle world is needed between them. Whatever is in the world of intellects is reflected first in the world of images, which has the shape and colour of matter without its weight. Only then does it reach the world of matter.

¶10. These three worlds are not places we travel to. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the world of images "is another dimension of the same world in which we are living" and so, with some difference, is the world of intellects. The unseen worlds surround this one, but in depth and not in distance: each is a deeper dimension of the world we are in now.

¶11. Imam ʿAli (as) described the same nesting when a Christian churchman came to him with questions. One of them, as the lectures tell it, was whether the hereafter lies inside this world or this world inside the hereafter. Imam ʿAli (as) answered that this world lies inside the hereafter: this world is limited and the hereafter encompasses it. The lectures set the answer beside a verse that says hell, which belongs to the hereafter, surrounds the faithless. If this world lies inside the hereafter, the hereafter is not somewhere else; it is around us now.

¶12. There are three worlds, then, one inside another and together they make one reality. The senses meet matter, the imagination meets the world of images and the intellect grasps pure meaning. Each world lies deeper than the one before and none of them is far away.

[box] A barzakh is a barrier, something that stands between two things and keeps each in its place. The Qur'an uses the word for the barrier God set between two seas that He merged, one sweet and one briny. It also uses it of death. One of the deniers, when death comes, asks to be sent back so that he may act righteously, and he is refused. Before such people, the verse says, is a barrier until the day they are resurrected. The ʿurafaʾ use the same word for the world of images, which stands between matter and pure intellect. Mohammad Ali Shomali notes that philosophers compare the body of that world with the bodies we see in dreams, and "call them 'Barzakh' or 'mithali'". The lectures join the two uses: the middle world is called barzakh because it stands between matter and intellect, and it is where the dead are until the resurrection.

## Section 3: A soul that crosses them

**Point:** When the body's hold on the soul loosens, the soul passes into the middle world; at death it stays there.

¶13. The soul has a share in each of these worlds, because it was made to stand between them. The Qur'an says that God breathed of His spirit into the first human being. God is not made of parts, so the spirit is called His out of honour, as the Kaʿba is called His house; it is the greatest of His creations. Pure spirit and matter cannot meet directly. The soul is the means by which that spirit reaches the body, as the world of images links intellect and matter. Standing between the two, the soul touches the material world through the body and the deeper worlds through the spirit.

¶14. For now, the body holds almost all of the soul's attention, so the soul lives in the material world and the deeper worlds pass unnoticed. That hold loosens at three times: in sleep, at death and in watchfulness. Let's take sleep, death and watchfulness one at a time, to see where the soul goes when the hold loosens.

¶15. The first is sleep. When we sleep, the soul attends less to the body. With the senses at rest, it can touch the world of images. It does not leave the body completely; if it did, we would be dead. This explains a dream that is more than memory. A person may dream of someone doing something that later happens and a memory cannot show the future. Time works differently in the middle world. The soul has seen there the counterpart of an event that has not yet happened here.

¶16. The second is death, which the Qur'an sets beside sleep. Surah al-Zumar names the two together.

[verse]

¶17. In both, the verse says, God takes the soul and the difference between the two is one of degree. Mohammad Ali Shomali explains that in sleep the spirit is still connected with the body, "but to a lesser degree than waking".

¶18. At death the soul lets go of the body completely. The spirit, Shomali writes, is then disconnected from the body and "belongs to another body which has the qualities of a material body like shape and size without the mass". This second body is the image body (badan mithali). "The body is not carrying the soul but the soul is carrying the body," Sheikh Bahmanpour says. When the appointed time comes, the soul sets the body down. It does not go on without a body, because a soul cannot be without one. It goes on in the image body, which was with us all along, unseen in another dimension.

¶19. The third is watchfulness (muraqaba): keeping the heart's attention on God. It happens while we are awake. A person who turns their attention to God until they forget the body does on purpose what sleep does and can then see in the middle world as one sees in a dream. Nothing leaves the body; what turns is the attention. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi said that the more he kept up his muraqaba, the more distinctly he saw into that world. Such moments come to the mystics as an opportunity from God and not as a power to use. Only the Imams (as), the lectures add, can pass between the worlds whenever they wish.

[box] Kernel of the Kernels, the record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures on the path, describes what may come to a traveller who keeps up muraqaba. Lights appear, at first like flashes of lightning, and "these lights belong to the class of entities of the realm of Barzakh". In the early stages the traveller may see his own soul in bodily form: "he might see himself standing before himself." The lectures then tell of their teacher, Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, who used to say: "One day emerging from the room into the veranda, I saw myself standing silent by my side. I looked very carefully at my face, and observed a mole on it. On returning to my room when I looked into the mirror, I saw that there was a mole on my face that I had not noticed until then".

¶20. Sleep, death and watchfulness are three ways of loosening one hold. In sleep the hold slackens for a night and in watchfulness a person loosens it on purpose. At death it lets go entirely. Each time, the soul reaches the world of images and at death it stays there.

¶21. This changes the picture at the graveside. The dead are in barzakh and barzakh is the world of images, the middle world the soul enters whenever the body's hold loosens. That world is not a distant room. It is a deeper dimension of the world we are living in, so the dead have gone deeper into our world, not away from it. Even the resurrection is a move deeper still, closer to the majesty and beauty of God and not a journey elsewhere.

## Section 4: Our deeds give the soul a form

**Point:** Because the soul is not fixed like an angel, what we do shapes it, and it meets that shape in the middle world.

¶22. The soul's movement between worlds sets it apart from the angels of the world of intellects. Each angel has a fixed station that it cannot rise above. An angel does not change or grow, because it is made as it will always be. Surah al-Saffat gives their own words.

[verse]

¶23. The human soul has no known place of this kind. It can move and change and no one knows how far it can go. The mystics say that this capacity is why the angels bowed down to Adam. The soul's reach expands, the lectures say, as it learns the names of God. A soul that is not fixed in advance can be shaped and how far it goes depends on the life it lives.

¶24. What shapes such a soul is what it does and above all what it does again and again. Shomali takes the example of lying. A child telling a first lie feels uneasy and later remorseful. With repetition "the soul becomes inured to the effects", until lying costs little effort. The act has become a habit and the habit has become part of the soul.

¶25. Some of that shaping shows on the face. A face can show faith or its absence to those with eyes to see it. In this world, though, the change is slow and small and it does not deform the body. The image body is far more sensitive. Sin deforms it and piety makes it shine, so that it takes the shape of what our habits have made of the soul.

¶26. The shape the image body has when the soul leaves this world is the shape it keeps. Imam Khomeini describes a comparable second form in his Forty Hadith. Beside the body that everyone can see, each person has "another form and a different face of him, which is heavenly and invisible, which is determined by the qualities of his soul and his inner nature". The same page names the moment that settles it: "in whichever state one's soul departs from the body, it assumes a form suited to it in the next world also". The face a soul wears in the next world is the face it has made by the time it dies.

¶27. The Qur'an points the same way. Surah al-Nabaʾ describes people coming forth on the Last Day.

[verse]

¶28. Shomali reports the hadith that the groups are formed "according to their true natures". Surah al-Hadid describes how the faithful will appear.

[verse]

¶29. The lectures read this light as the shining of the pious themselves. The body of the Last Day, they note, is not the image body, yet piety makes both shine. On the Last Day that light goes ahead of the faithful to light the way. In both verses, people appear as what their souls have become.

¶30. If the light of the faithful is their own piety shining, then some rewards are the deeds themselves, made visible. Shomali states this as a general principle about reward and punishment: "Sometimes rewards or punishments are nothing other than the actions. They are just the realities of those actions made manifest in another universe". A deed done here has a reality in the middle world and Ibn ʿArabi once met such a reality face to face.

[box] Sheikh Bahmanpour tells a story that the great mystic Ibn ʿArabi told of his own childhood. At the age of twelve he fell seriously ill and went into a coma. It was so serious that everyone thought he had died. In that state he saw horrible-looking people who were trying to hurt him. Then a figure breathing out a wonderful perfume defended him and drove them away. The boy asked who he was. The figure answered that he was Surah Yasin, his protector. When the boy came round, he found his father at his bedside in tears. His father had just finished reciting Surah Yasin for his recovery, and the boy had not known. In the Sheikh's reading, the figure was the image the recitation had formed in barzakh, for anything we do here has an image there. A lie or a word of comfort seems to be only words, yet each takes a shape in barzakh, as real as the figure the boy saw.

¶31. If some rewards are the deeds themselves, the form a soul meets after death is, in part, the work of its own deeds. Shomali draws a related conclusion from the fact that heaven and hell already exist: "our future is now present". The image body is being shaped now, by what we do and every prayer, lie or word of comfort adds to its form.

## Section 5: The self can change its state

**Point:** The self that does the deeds can change its state, so what the soul becomes is, by God's grace, in our hands.

¶32. The deeds that shape the soul are done by the self (nafs) and the self is not fixed either. The Qur'an gives the self three names and the lectures read them as three states. Let's take them from lowest to highest, to see how the self can move.

¶33. The lowest is the self that commands to evil (nafs al-ammara). In it the bodily side of a person overcomes the spiritual side and the soul goes after the body's pleasures. Surah Yusuf names this self together with the mercy that can lift it.

[verse]

¶34. The verse admits what this self does and names what overcomes it, the mercy of God. The pull of this self is not only towards pleasure. Worry binds the soul to the body as well: worry over whether our children will be poor or whether we will have a job next year. Worry keeps the soul occupied with the body and stops it rising towards God.

¶35. After giving in, the self may remember what it did and ask why. This is the second state, the self that blames itself (nafs al-lawwama). Its self-blame is a hopeful sign, because it shows that we hold values that matter more to us than the pleasure we gave in to. Surah al-Qiyama swears by it, which suggests its worth.

[verse]

¶36. A self that keeps up its self-blame comes in time to the third state, the self at peace (nafs al-mutmaʾinna). Surah al-Fajr calls this self back to its Lord. It relies on what belongs to the other world and not on this one. It is free of sin, of greed and of a mind turned wholly towards the body.

¶37. These are not three selves. "We don't have different types of nufus," Sheikh Bahmanpour says, using the plural of nafs. The one self takes a different name according to its attitude. Because we can change the attitude, we can change the state and with it the name. The name says where the self stands now and not where it must always stay.

[box] Murtada Mutahhari writes that the ʿurafaʾ "have different words for the human soul", and he sets out four. When the soul is "dominated and ruled by desires and passions they call it nafs". When it bears divine knowledge it is the heart (qalb). When "the light of Divine love dawns within it", it is the spirit (ruh), and when it reaches witnessing it is the secret (sirr). Beyond these lie the hidden (khafi) and the most hidden (akhfa). The lectures order them differently, describing the sirr as known only to God and the khafi as hidden even from the angels. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, a Shia master, counts the states of the self differently too, with four where the lectures have three. In his Inner Secrets of the Path the self ascends through "commanding to evil", "reproachful", "inspired", and "at rest". Imam Khomeini notes that the soul's levels are divided "sometimes into seven sections and sometimes into four", and sometimes into three or two. The lists count different things (names, states, and levels). Read together, they describe one soul seen in different ways. Chapter Eight takes nafs, qalb, and ruh as stages of the road.

¶38. The attitude is changed by acts already familiar to us. Each turns the soul's attention from the body towards God and that turning is what loosens the body's hold on the soul. Two of them show how this works: the remembrance of God and fasting with the night prayer.

¶39. The first is the remembrance of God (dhikr), which the lectures describe as refreshing and reviving the soul. Surah al-Raʿd names what it gives.

[verse]

¶40. In Arabic, the verse's word for finding rest comes from the same root as mutmaʾinna, the name of the self at peace. The rest that remembrance brings is the rest of the third state.

¶41. The second is fasting, with the night prayer beside it. Both work on the same tie to the body that sleep and watchfulness loosen. The soul manages the body, down to digesting a meal, so a fasting body asks less of it. In Ramadan God pours down His light everywhere. The soul of the one who fasts is less taken up with the body and it takes in that light without knowing it. The night prayer (salat al-layl) likewise helps the soul free itself from the body and see the light.

¶42. A change made this way lasts, because the soul takes on habits quickly. Trained to worry about the body, it worries about the body. Trained to care for higher things, it comes to care for them and to see the light. In the lectures' image, the soul is like a fine elastic thing. It can be held in the palm of a hand or stretched to the size of the universe and which it becomes depends on what we do with it. It has been entrusted to us for a short time. Whatever we do with it, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, "would remain with us forever".

¶43. The soul is therefore deeper than it looks. It lives in a creation of three worlds, one inside another and none of them is far away. When the body's hold loosens, the soul passes into the middle world and at death it stays there. Its deeds give it a form that it will meet there. The self that does those deeds can move from commanding to blaming to peace. What the soul finally becomes has been entrusted to us. The drive to begin is itself God's grace, given, the lectures say, once we have shown that we deserve it. The rest of the work is in our hands.
