## Reference: Author A (all of this is by Author A)

[quotation]

These words come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, a prayer many of us recite in the month of Shaʿban. The line asks God to light up the eyes of our hearts with the light of looking at Him, until those eyes pass through even the veils of light and reach the source of His grandeur. It is a request to see God with the heart and to be near Him. Requests like this one were written for us, by the Imams (as), as an endeavour for the ordinary believer.

Despite saying these words in our munajats and on auspicious occasions, we find that seeking to know God does not feature in our day-to-day routine. Praying, fasting and paying what is due, the wajibat, are the dominant undertaking and they are what most of us mean by practising the religion. The other side, the one the Munajat describes, in which the believer seeks to know God and to come near Him, is often less practised. That side of the religion is called ʿirfan.

ʿIrfan is the Arabic name for what English books call Islamic mysticism and the two words do not say the same thing. "Mysticism" suggests mystery, something hidden and hard to pin down. The Arabic word names a kind of knowing. It shares its root with maʿrifah, knowledge and an ʿarif is, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "someone who knows".

The knowing meant here is not information about God. It is knowing Him from close, with the heart, which is what the Munajat asks for. In Murtada Mutahhari's words, the ʿarif "wishes to reach the very kernel and reality of existence, God, to become connected to it and witness it". ʿIrfan is that knowing and the training of the soul that makes it possible. It is part of the Islam we already know: its sources are the Qur'an and the words of the Imams and the prayer at the head of this page is one of them.

This book argues that this side of the religion is worth every believer's pursuit. ʿIrfan travels through the self and never leaves the shariʿa. It is walked by a tested and ordered method. It is rooted in the Qur'an and the Imams. And it ends not in withdrawal from the world but in return: to people, with God and to God Himself.

The argument is addressed to anyone who prays, fasts and recites the duʿas and who has wondered whether there is more to these acts than getting them right; whether the religion has another dimension. No knowledge of ʿirfan, of philosophy or of Arabic is assumed. The book is meant as a welcoming introduction to ʿirfan, a guide to beginning the journey that strives for brevity and clarity. Each chapter opens with a section that details some of the words you'll meet, along with an explanation; all terms are gathered in the glossary at the end. Additional call-out sections are spread throughout with helpful context and short side notes.

The book is built in five parts. Each answers one question and depends on the one before.

Part One, The Call (Chapters One to Three), asks why a believer should bother. It answers that the Qur'an invites more than the minimum: not only knowing about God, but knowing Him. The goal of ʿirfan is already on our lips in our duʿas.

Part Two, Getting Ready (Chapters Four to Seven), describes the landscape a traveller passes through on the journey towards ʿirfan: the self. It also sets out the boundary that keeps the traveller safe, the shariʿa and the guide who walks alongside.

Part Three, The Road (Chapters Eight to Twelve), asks how the path is walked: through ordered stages, by repentance, discipline and love, to the loss of the false self.

Part Four, Fellow Travellers (Chapters Thirteen to Fifteen), meets those who walked the road before us. It shows the road's roots in the Qur'an and the Imams, where its travellers arrived and how Shia ʿirfan relates to Sufism.

Part Five, The Destination (Chapters Sixteen and Seventeen), asks what the road is for. The friends of God return to people, with God; every soul returns to God.

This book is inspired by a lecture series on Islamic mysticism given by Sheikh M. S. Bahmanpour. The content and structure of those lectures are the core of the book. Wherever the Sheikh is quoted directly, the words are his own, from that series.

Turning spoken lectures into a book has called for some editorial adaptation. To suit a wider readership, the book also adds context and explanation to support its main arguments. It draws on a wider literature as well: the words of the Imams in Nahj al-Balagha, al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya and the supplications of the Mafatih; then the great Shia teachers of the path, from Sayyid Haydar Amuli in the fourteenth century to modern scholars such as Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi, ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, Imam Khomeini and Ayatullah Mutahhari. Every source is given in a footnote, for readers who want to go further.

The finer questions of ʿirfan remain an ongoing conversation among our ʿulamaʾ: whether a seeker needs a guide from the first step, how much solitude is wise, how far ordinary believers can travel. Where they differ, this book presents each view with neutrality.

Three guiding principles frame the discussions in the chapters that follow. The first is that ʿirfan is never pursued for the sake of powers, such as reading minds or foreseeing future events. Sheikh Bahmanpour and the wider literature agree on this, as Chapter One shows.

The second is that there is no separate sect or order through which ʿirfan must be gained. Shia ʿirfan has no institution of authority beside the Imam. How this position differs from that of the Sufi orders is explored in Chapter Fifteen.

The third principle is the most important: no part of the journey towards ʿirfan requires stepping outside the shariʿa and nothing on that journey is gained by doing so. The beginning of ʿirfan, as the Sheikh puts it, is the shariʿa. The inner path does not go around the law or rise above it; it is the law lived from the inside. Nothing in this book asks a reader to set aside the rulings of the jurists or the guidance of their marjaʿ. A fear that mysticism might do exactly that is one reason some scholars have been wary of ʿirfan. Chapter Fourteen sets out their wider concerns, with the answers given to them.

By the end, a reader should feel able to say what ʿirfan is and is not, why it belongs to us and what the stages and dangers of the path entail. For those who wish to go further, each chapter and the epilogue offer small, practical steps for bringing this into daily life alongside the wajibat.

[verse]

There is a verse often recited at weddings, one most of us know. In Surah al-Rum, God says that He created spouses for us from among ourselves "that you may take comfort in them" and that He ordained "affection and mercy" between husband and wife. The verse ends with the words above: this is a sign for a people who reflect. Whether a person sees it depends on something in the person doing the looking. That something and why it is worth developing, is the subject of this chapter.

Let's start with two claims. The first is that a believer who prays, fasts, pays what is due and goes on hajj can hope for jannah, provided they do what is wajib and avoid what is forbidden. This claim is not contested. Sheikh Bahmanpour accepts it as fair for ordinary believers. Mohammad Ali Shomali makes the same point, describing the obligatory rules of fiqh as "the minimally necessary conditions of human perfection".

The second claim goes further and it is the one this book disputes. Some jurists and activists hold that the obligatory acts are the whole of the religion; anything beyond them, they say, is made up by the whims and wishes of certain mystics.

To see why there is more than the obligations, let's turn to the Qur'an and read on from the verse this chapter began with. Surah al-Rum names three more sets of signs: the creation of the heavens and the earth, with the variety of our languages and colours; our sleep and our work; the lightning and the rain that revives the dead earth. Each verse ends as the first did, by saying who the sign is for.

[verse]

Four times in a row, the Qur'an makes the same point. The signs are all around us: in marriage, in the people we meet, in sleep, in work, in the weather. Yet they are for those who reflect, know, listen and reason. This reflecting, knowing, listening and reasoning is what the Qur'an asks of us beyond the minimum.

For the avoidance of doubt, this invitation from the Qur'an is not an invitation to circumvent the law or to abandon the prescribed wajibat. Imam Khomeini insists that a genuine seeker's devotion "is within the bounds of the Shari'ah". Within those bounds, the Qur'an is just as insistent, in these verses and others, that it invites us to more than the obligatory acts.

An invitation addressed to those who reflect, know, listen and reason implies that many people do none of these things. This is not limited to the careless. Most pious people, even those careful about the shariʿa, do not find a sign in these verses. Looking at the variety of languages and colours, we are more likely to see a reason for division than a sign of God.

We have heard these verses many times and we accept that there are signs in them because the Qur'an says so; yet accepting that a sign exists is not the same as perceiving it. The Qur'an describes the gap in one verse.

[verse]

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi explains what passing by a sign means; a person who sees a red light thinks of the danger it warns of, not of the colour and shape of the lamp. In the same way, the things of the world are signs and "what the signs indicate is not this world, but the person of its Creator". Most of us see only the lamp. We witness the rain as rain and the marriage as a marriage and we pass them by without perceiving what they say about their Creator.

If the signs are present and plain and yet we still pass them by, the fault is on our side. The Qur'an says where it lies, speaking of people who have every faculty and use none of them.

[verse]

Cattle see and hear perfectly well, so the comparison cannot be about the senses. It is about what lies behind the senses. Eyes and ears take in the surface of things, whereas inside a human being there should be something more: a faculty, which animals lack, that turns what the senses take in into understanding. In most of us, it is not working.

Sheikh Bahmanpour compares this faculty to a machine. The ear catches the sound, but "there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is [rusted]." That machine is the soul. It has to be repaired before it can catch the signals of God. This is the whole subject in one sentence: mysticism is about making the soul hear, see and reflect. It is not easy and it requires a great deal of training.

Placing the fault in the receiver has two consequences. The first is that the problem is not a shortage of evidence. God is not withholding signs; we are failing to receive them.

The second is that the problem can be solved. A faculty that exists but has rusted can be repaired; one that is absent cannot be made fit for purpose. The Kernel of the Kernels, a record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's early lectures on the path, opens by saying that the pull towards the unseen is "part of the innate instincts of the human being". The longing for God and the faculty that perceives Him, are part of how we are made.

Repair is only the start. Once the inner faculty begins to work, the journey begins and it does not end; it is, in the Sheikh's words, a path that "has a beginning and no end".

### Passage A

The first is sleep. When we sleep, the soul attends less to the body. With the senses at rest, it can touch the world of images. It does not leave the body completely; if it did, we would be dead. This explains a dream that is more than memory. A person may dream of someone doing something that later happens and a memory cannot show the future. Time works differently in the middle world. The soul has seen there the counterpart of an event that has not yet happened here.

The Qur'an also sets sleep beside death. Surah al-Zumar names the two together.

[verse]

In both, the verse says, God takes the soul. The difference between the two is one of degree. Mohammad Ali Shomali explains that in sleep the spirit is still connected with the body, "but to a lesser degree than waking". At death it is disconnected from the body and "belongs to another body which has the qualities of a material body like shape and size without the mass". This second body is the image body (badan mithali). It has our shape, but it is not made of any material elements.

### Passage B

The great teachers of the Shia tradition make closely related points, each in his own words. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi tells the traveller that he "has no right to seek occult experiences and miraculous qualities", nor to perform austerities or recite invocations to gain powers such as crossing great distances in a moment or reading the thoughts of others. Whoever does so "does not move on the path of the Beloved's pleasure and his worship is not for the sake of God". It is his own self that he worships, "though he may not admit it by word of mouth".

Imam Khomeini reports the test his teacher Ayatullah Shahabadi used: the measure of true spiritual effort is how much selfishness is in it. If a traveller's "spiritual exercises are meant for acquiring powers for worldly ends, his efforts are rendered invalid".

### Passage C

Pursuing ʿirfan means training the soul and soul-training is not unique to Islam. People take up the inner life for different reasons. Some train the soul in an attempt to gain power: to be in two places at once, to see the future or the past or to master the hidden world of the soul. This kind of mysticism must be held apart from the real thing. Islamic mysticism is about catching the signs of Allah: becoming sensitive to them and learning to hear and see.

Sheikh Bahmanpour ended his first lecture more strongly. ʿIrfan "is not about any type of power or empowering the soul" to work karamat and muʿjizat, the wonders of the saints and the miracles of the prophets. Then: "These are shirk." Shirk, setting up a partner beside God, is the gravest charge in Islam.

### Passage D

Requests this high raise a difficulty and Sheikh Bahmanpour states it himself. These words, he says, belong to the peak of the understanding and longing of the muqarrabun, those brought near to God. He asks why we are told to recite them at all. Telling ordinary believers to say them, he says, is "like [telling] a primary school boy to read books of university".

The prayers themselves speak from need, as a pupil would. Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, offers God one thing as its means of approach, the supplicant's need of Him.

[quotation]

Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali makes the same plea from need and places the one who prays at a door. "O my Master, I, Your servant, am standing at Your door owing to neediness," it says (سَيِّدِي عَبْدُكَ بِبَابِكَ أَقَامَتْهُ ٱلْخَصَاصَةُ بَيْنَ يَدَيْكَ). The servant stands there because of need and the prayer gives no other reason for coming.

### Passage E

The intellect gives us a sample of the third world. Every person we have met has a particular height, voice and face. Yet the mind can grasp "a human being" with none of these features. No such person exists anywhere outside the mind. This kind of understanding deals in meanings, with no image and no matter. Its world is the world of intellects (ʿalam al-ʿuqul). The angels who live there are pure intellect, attached to nothing material.

The world of images stands between the other two. That is why it is also called barzakh, a barrier. Pure intellect and matter have nothing in common, so they cannot meet directly. Whatever is in the world of intellects is reflected first in the world of images, which has the shape and colour of matter without its weight. Only then does it reach the world of matter.

### Passage F

Murtada Mutahhari uses the same word as Sheikh Bahmanpour. The ʿurafaʾ, he writes, consider it "a kind of shirk (polytheism) for one's goal in life and particularly in worship to be something other than God Himself" and he recalls Imam ʿAli (as) saying that he worshipped God not from fear of His Fire or desire for His Paradise but because he found Him worthy of worship.

The scholars differ in emphasis but agree that powers are not the aim. Mutahhari also explains why seeking them would be shirk: the goal has become something other than God.

With powers set aside, what remains is the definition Sheikh Bahmanpour gave at the end of his first lecture: ʿirfan "is actually seeking and nothing else". It seeks two things: maʿrifah, the knowledge of God; and the love of God.

### Passage G

Acquired knowledge is the work of reason, ʿaql, the theoretical intellect by which we reason, understand and argue. Reason has a limit and Sheikh Bahmanpour says where it lies. When we know a thing, we know its attributes: its colour, its size, what it does. Reason does not reach the thing that carries those attributes. Every description says that it is brown or square or useful and none says what the "it" is.

In the Sheikh's account, Ibn Sina, the master of the philosophers, granted this. To know the reality of a thing, he held, is beyond human capacity; what we know of an object is its characteristics and attributes. If this is true of the things around us, it is true far more of God. God has no colour, no dimension and no function for the mind to take hold of.

### Passage H

The second kind is knowledge by presence, ʿilm al-huduri. It does not come from outside and needs no concept to carry it; it is with the soul already. The plainest case, in the Sheikh's account, is our knowledge of our own self. Hunger, the example this chapter opened with, is another and so is pain.

Knowledge by presence is complete in a way that acquired knowledge is not. When the body is hungry, the soul knows it at once and without effort and nothing of the hunger is missed. The hunger may even be false, the Sheikh notes, yet the feeling is real and drives us. Anxiety works in the same way. It has a cause outside us, but the feeling comes from inside the soul; the cause is not the feeling.

### Passage I

The line at the head of Part One takes this humility further. In it the Munajat admits that the one reciting had no strength to move away from disobedience "except when You awakened me to Your love". In this line, even the turning towards God is His doing.

Sheikh Bahmanpour's answer to his own question rests on the same humility. We do not understand many of the meanings of these words, he says and they are not of our level or within our capacity. Not understanding them does not make them meaningless. We have been honoured, because we are allowed to recite them. In his account, God invites the reciter to come and say them; if the reciter is sincere, God may grant the request. What the Imams asked for, the Sheikh adds, God granted them.

### Passage J

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads "those who possess knowledge" as another name for the ʿurafaʾ. Every Muslim knows that there is no god but Allah. Our knowledge of tawhid, God's oneness, is still not the knowledge of those who witness it. Tawhid is simple to say and hard to grasp; the intellect alone cannot reach its depths and only the awakened soul can. Surah Fatir describes the same people by what they feel.

[verse]

Most of us fear Allah's punishment. The people of this verse fear Him with a fear that is not about punishment at all.

Surah Yunus gives them another name: the awliyaʾ Allah, the friends of God. Imam ʿAli (as) describes what these friends see that others miss.

### Passage K

These words come near the end of Duʿa Kumayl, the prayer Imam ʿAli (as) taught to Kumayl ibn Ziyad, one of his closest disciples. The Mafatih al-Jinan recommends it on every Thursday evening, the night before Friday, when many of us hear it. The line asks God for a heart enthralled by His love.

A request for God's love begins to answer the question Chapter Two ended on. That chapter argued that knowing God is reached by purifying the heart and it asked where an ordinary believer already stands on this path. The words we already say ask for exactly what Chapter Two described. Duʿa Kumayl asks for the love that goes with that knowing; other prayers we recite ask for the knowing itself. This chapter sets out what those prayers ask for.

### Passage L

If the faculty can be repaired, there should be people in whom it already works. The Qur'an describes them more than once. People can be divided into two groups: those whose souls work and those whose souls do not. The first group are the ʿurafaʾ, the plural of ʿarif. The prophets and the Imams (as) are a class of their own; the ʿurafaʾ are ordinary believers who see more than the rest. The first description of them is in Surah al-Anfal.

[verse]

A heart trembles only at the mention of someone it has some experience of; a name means little to a person who does not know whom it names. The believers of this verse are thus the believers who know Allah. A verse in Surah Al ʿImran goes further, giving them another name.

### Passage M

Allah's light, the Sheikh says, is everywhere, in the heavens and in the earth and He is with us wherever we are. It is the heart that is closed. It is not the case, he says, that Allah picks out some people to receive light and passes over others. There is no hindrance on His side to pouring His knowledge and bounty upon us. His knowledge and bounty are all there; the work of the ʿurafaʾ is to open the heart and let the light in.

Imam Khomeini makes the same point in his comment on the hadith of light. There is, he writes, "no niggardliness in the higher realms", which give of their nature. Whatever stands between the heart and the light stands on our side. The light, the Sheikh says, comes to the hearts of the pure and darkness covers the impure. Chapter One reached the same conclusion by another road, when it placed the fault in the receiver.

### Passage N

Some of that shaping shows on the face. A face can show faith or its absence to those with eyes to see it and a pious person's face can carry light. In this world the change is slow and small and it never deforms the body. The image body is far more sensitive. Sin deforms it and piety makes it shine, so that it takes the shape of what our habits have made of the soul.

Imam Khomeini describes this second form in his Forty Hadith. Beside the body that everyone can see, each person has "another form and a different face of him, which is heavenly and invisible, which is determined by the qualities of his soul and his inner nature". The soul's state when it leaves the body fixes that form: "in whichever state one's soul departs from the body, it assumes a form suited to it in the next world also". Surah al-Nabaʾ describes people coming forth on the Last Day.

### Passage O

After giving in, the self may remember what it did and ask why. This is the second state, the self that blames itself (al-nafs al-lawwama). Its self-blame is a hopeful sign. It shows that we hold values that matter more to us than the pleasure we gave in to. Surah al-Qiyama swears by it.

[verse]

That God swears by this self shows its worth. A self that keeps up its self-blame comes in time to the third state, the self at peace (al-nafs al-mutmaʾinna). Surah al-Fajr calls this self back to its Lord. It relies on what belongs to the other world and not on this one. It is free of sin, of greed and of a mind turned wholly towards the body.

### Passage P

If a reward can be the deed itself made visible, the form a soul meets after death is largely its own deeds. In Shomali's words, "our future is now present". The image body is being shaped now, by what we do.

If deeds give the soul its form, the self that does the deeds is not fixed either. The Qur'an names the self (nafs) in three states. The lowest is the self that commands to evil (al-nafs al-ammara). In it the bodily side of a person overcomes the spiritual side and the soul goes after the body's pleasures. Surah Yusuf names this self together with the mercy that can lift it.

[verse]

The verse admits what this self does and names the one thing that checks it, the mercy of God. Its pull is not only towards pleasure. Worry binds the soul to the body as well: worry over whether our children will be poor or whether we will have a job next year. It keeps the soul occupied with the body and stops it rising towards God.

### Passage Q

These words come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, the prayer of the month of Shaʿban. The one who prays admits that neglect has put them to sleep, unready to meet God. Knowing His generosity has woken them. The prayer sets sleep beside the meeting with God.

The tradition does the same. Lantern of the Path, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), calls sleep "the brother of death". Death is what we stand before at a graveside. There we say that the dead are now in barzakh (the world between this life and the resurrection) and many of us picture a waiting room somewhere far away.

Sheikh Bahmanpour teaches otherwise: at death "we don't go anywhere". The dead are not far away and the reason lies in what the soul is. The way to God runs through the soul, the one thing we know from the inside. How deep the soul goes therefore matters. This chapter sets out the soul and its worlds: which worlds the soul lives in, how it moves between them and how its deeds give it a form there. These three steps show that what the soul finally becomes is in our hands.

### Passage R

The second gift is a different kind of life, promised in Surah al-Nahl to whoever acts righteously, man or woman, while having faith.

[verse]

This good life, hayat tayyiba, is a clean life. Its people live in the world without being soiled by it, rising above the worries that occupy everyone else. It is this good life that the mystics seek.

The third gift is peace, promised in the verse of Surah Yunus that names the friends of God.

[verse]

For the muqarrabun, the terms are entirely different from those of ordinary believers.

None of this would matter to an ordinary reader if it were reserved for prophets. Surah al-Nisaʾ answers that concern.

[verse]

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads this verse as naming four ranks of those God has blessed: the prophets; the truthful (siddiqin); the shuhadaʾ, which the translation used here renders "martyrs" and which he takes as "the witnesses"; and the righteous (salihin). The first three ranks are beyond us; the fourth is a doorway. The salihin are those able to receive this favour and from among them God chooses the truthful and the prophets. The way in is righteous action, as the verse on the good life also says.
