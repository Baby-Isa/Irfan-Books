# Chain packet 12

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

¶1. Twice in every unit of the prayer (rakʿa), we go down into prostration (sujud). Every other act of the prayer is performed once. With our foreheads on the ground, we glorify our Lord, the Most High and praise Him. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi calls this posture "the most supreme state and spiritual position for a human being, i.e., the position of annihilation in Allah" and recalls the tradition that a person in prostration "is nearer to Allah than in any other state".

¶2. The annihilation he names is what the mystics call passing away (fanaʾ) and in every prostration the body takes its posture for a moment. The traveller reaches the state itself only at the end of a long road. In the third stage, God adorned the emptied heart with His gifts and love carried the traveller until even love was left at the door. What remains before God is the traveller himself and the fourth and last stage is about him. This chapter sets out what passing away is: what is lost in losing the self, what is found in finding God and how the traveller comes back.

## Section 2: What is lost is the false self

**Point:** In passing away the traveller loses the sense of self before God's glory; he neither ceases to exist nor becomes God, so what is lost is a false self.

¶3. Passing away begins at the height of adorning. At the end of that stage God shows the traveller a small measure of His glory. No one can watch that glory and perceive himself at the same time. The glory does not wait for the traveller to clear a space for it. It comes and everything else goes from his sight, even his sense of himself. Something like it happens to a person seized by great fear or great joy. He can cut his hand and not feel it, because what is in front of him has taken all his attention. <!-- src: L07 ¶55-57 -->

¶4. Passing away is therefore a change in what the traveller sees and not in what exists. Sheikh Bahmanpour defines it as "the annihilation of self perception of the mystics in perceiving the unveiling [of] the reality of God". The world goes on as before. What changes is the traveller's knowing and so the lectures call passing away "an epistemological issue", a matter of perception and not of existence. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi says the same of the world around the traveller: "this does not mean that everything in the material world loses its existence. Actually, the devotee sees unity in plurality". <!-- src: L07 ¶59; L09 ¶4 -->

¶5. The traveller's soul remains too. What he loses is the sense of self, while "the soul is there, everything is there". Passing away is not nirvana, which the lectures describe as the Buddhist goal of ending individuality. In Islamic mysticism, "it is the individual who is going to be there forever getting more expanded, more capable". If the individual dissolved, no one would be left to taste what God shows, since only an individual can experience anything. The Qur'an keeps the individual even in paradise, where "Their Lord will give them to drink a pure drink". <!-- src: L07 ¶49-51, ¶56; L09 ¶8 -->

¶6. Nor does the traveller become God. Passing away is never a merging of the divine and the human. The lectures call such a merging impossible and the claim to it shirk or unbelief (kufr). Whoever claims to have dissolved in God or to have God dwelling in him, has not understood what happened to him. Before God's glory the traveller stops perceiving himself, but he never stops being a creature. <!-- src: L07 ¶60-61 -->

¶7. What goes, then, is the false picture of a self that stands on its own. This self is the last thing emptying leaves behind and passing away is where it falls. We lost our original self to worldly desires. Passing away breaks the walls of illusion built around us and returns us to the self God created. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, it is a move "out of the imaginary you to the real you". <!-- src: L09 ¶9 -->

[box] The two words for this stage come from one pair of verses in Surah al-Rahman.

[verse]

[box] "Ephemeral" translates fanin, from the root of fanaʾ, passing away; "lasting" translates yabqa, from the root of baqaʾ, remaining. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures read the Face of a thing as "that which manifests it", and the Face of God as His Names, through which every creature faces Him. All else passes away. The traveller who passes away from himself reaches "total annihilation (fana' kulli) and, thereafter, the station of life in God (baqa'), whereat eternal life becomes established for him".

¶8. What is lost in passing away is therefore the false picture of a self that stands apart from God. The soul remains and so does the world around it. God remains God and no creature merges with Him.

## Section 3: Three degrees: in action, in attributes, in essence

**Point:** Passing away comes in three degrees, each the lived truth of words we already say.

¶9. Passing away does not come all at once. It comes in degrees and their order follows the order of the veils. Sayyid Haydar Amuli quotes a gnostic: "The essence is veiled by the attributes and the attributes by the action". The line names three layers, so the unveiling has three degrees. It runs from the outermost layer inwards, in action first, then in attributes and last in essence. Let's take them in turn. Each makes words we already say into something the traveller sees.

¶10. The first degree is passing away in action. We think it is our eyes that see, whereas at this degree the traveller realises that "it is really not you who is seeing". When a hand moves, the power in it is God's, flowing into the hand, though the intention behind the act remains ours. This is the lived truth of words many of us repeat after the prayers: there is no movement and no power except through God (la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah). The traveller comes to the place a verse of Surah al-Kahf describes. <!-- src: L09 ¶52-54, ¶60 -->

[verse]

¶11. That there is no authority but God's is something any believer can work out. A person can think about it for weeks and conclude that God created him, provides for him and moves his hand. The traveller at this degree tastes it. The difference is that between a recipe and the dish. Reading the recipe, we can imagine how the dish will taste, whereas only the cooked dish gives the taste. <!-- src: L09 ¶57-59 -->

¶12. The second degree is passing away in attributes. Wherever the traveller looks, even at the material world, he sees the attributes of God at work. Life, knowledge and power are in everything around him and all of them are His. This is the lived truth of the words we say most often, there is no god but God (la ilaha illa Allah), because Allah is the name that gathers all His attributes. The Throne Verse opens on the attribute of life. <!-- src: L09 ¶60-61 -->

[verse]

¶13. In this verse life belongs to God and whatever lives does so through Him. The same is true of movement, knowledge, power and energy, wherever they appear. At this degree the traveller sees them as His wherever he looks. <!-- src: L09 ¶63 -->

¶14. The third degree is passing away in essence. Here the traveller no longer sees even the attributes. In the lectures' words, there is "just His glory, which is overshadowing everything". What is left to him is one word, Huwa, "He". The Qur'an uses this pronoun for God and it points to Him without naming anything beside Him. Surah al-Ikhlas opens on this word. <!-- src: L09 ¶64-66 -->

[verse]

¶15. Sheikh Bahmanpour reports a hadith that shows this word on the lips of Imam ʿAli (as). On the night before the battle of Badr, the Imam dreamt of Khidr, the servant of God whom Musa followed. He asked Khidr to teach him a prayer he could repeat. Khidr taught him a short prayer that calls on God as Huwa, He, besides whom there is none. On the day of Badr, the report goes on, Imam ʿAli (as) was seen attacking from every side, reciting Surah al-Ikhlas and the words of this prayer. <!-- src: L09 ¶65 -->

¶16. Where the lectures count three degrees, ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi counts four, adding a unity of the Names between the attributes and the essence. His order is the same, with God's actions first and His essence last.

¶17. At the last degree the traveller reaches what the line this book opened with asked for. In the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah we ask God to light up the eyes of our hearts until they pierce the veils of light and reach the source of His grandeur. The sentence, which the Imams (as) placed on our tongues, does not end there.

[quotation]

¶18. Once the eyes of the heart have passed the veils, the prayer asks that our souls be suspended by the glory of God's holiness. A soul suspended in this way has not dissolved into what it sees. It is still there, held by that glory and no longer turned on itself.

¶19. Passing away thus comes in three degrees, in action, in attributes and in essence. At each one, words the believer already says become what the traveller sees: no power except through God, no god but God and He.

## Section 4: Coming back, sober

**Point:** Passing away is a beginning, not an end: the healthy outcome is to come back to oneself, sober, seeing God and creation together.

¶20. Passing away is the summit of the road, but not its end. Of the traveller who reaches it, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, "That's the beginning, that's not the end." Most mystics never reach this station. Those who do still face dangers, because nearness to God is not safety. Satan once stood near to God, in a place where pride had no room and there he grew proud. He fell, defeated by nothing but his own self. <!-- src: L09 ¶20, ¶23 -->

¶21. Not everyone who passes away comes back well. The lectures describe three ways of going wrong. Let's take them as principles rather than as the stories of particular people.

¶22. In the first, the traveller stays lost. Drawn to the divine light, he is held for ever by the beauty of God and does not come back to himself. Such mystics are a small minority. Souls differ in how they meet so great an encounter, as two people caught in the same accident may come out of it in different states. <!-- src: L09 ¶30-31 -->

¶23. In the second, the traveller is held only for a while and in that time he loses control of his tongue. He utters ecstatic sayings (shath), words in which he confuses himself with God. While he is out of his mind, he is not held to religious duties, like anyone who has lost his reason. Some mystics go further and say that duties are lifted for ever from one who reaches this station, even once he is in his right mind. The lectures hold this to be unacceptable, above all in the Shia understanding of mysticism and in most of the Sunni tradition as well. <!-- src: L09 ¶38-42 -->

¶24. In the third and the most dangerous, the traveller can no longer tell himself from his Lord or his Lord from His creation. He falls into licence and says that "whatever sin is done is done by God". He has forgotten what the first degree taught: the power in an act is God's, whereas the intention is ours. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi lists the same dangers among those on the road, including the "claim of incarnation and identification with God, denial of being obligated to abide by religious injunctions and regarding everything lawful". <!-- src: L09 ¶43, ¶53 -->

¶25. All three dangers have one cause, which is rushing. Most mystics who fall into them have pushed themselves to this station in haste, through the wrong kind of practice. Their souls then grow out of proportion, like a body whose head grows huge while the rest stays small. A traveller who practises in a balanced way is raised to the station gradually. The law, a guide and a measured pace keep him to that way. Chapters Six, Seven, Nine and Ten set them out. The tradition remembers Bayazid Bistami for his ecstatic sayings and Junayd of Baghdad for the sober way; Chapter Fourteen returns to both. <!-- src: L09 ¶44, ¶47-48; L07 ¶64 -->

¶26. A traveller who has walked at the right pace comes back well. The mystics liken passing away to intoxication (sukr). The healthy return they call sobriety after obliteration (sahw baʿd al-mahw) or separation after unification (farq baʿd al-jamʿ). In passing away the traveller seemed to be one with God. With every illusion gone, he gradually comes to tell himself apart from God and God apart from His creation. Imam Khomeini describes the return as God's grace, which "comes to his aid, bringing him to himself and returning him to the domain of his own self, whereat he finds himself in the state of awakening (sahw)". <!-- src: L09 ¶45, ¶50 -->

¶27. This sobriety is not a step down. Murtada Mutahhari writes that the return to remaining (baqaʾ) is not "a retrogression from a higher state" and calls sobriety "loftier even than mahw and mahq", the effacement and obliteration of passing away. The Imams (as) lived in it. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures say that they reached "the station of subsistence after annihilation", a state "which combines the two realms of Unity and plurality" and that they never held back from "observance of the most minor of the laws".

¶28. In this sobriety neither God nor creation veils the other. For us, creation veils God from our sight and we see the world without seeing Him. In passing away, God's glory veils creation and the traveller sees only Him. In the return, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "neither of them is veiled by the other". Sayyid Haydar Amuli quotes a gnostic on the same balance. Merging alone leads to heresy, whereas separation alone denies that God is the Absolute Actor. The gnostic tells the traveller to keep both, "for the one who joins them is the one of true affirmation". <!-- src: L09 ¶46 -->

¶29. Passing away is therefore a beginning and its healthy end is the return. Love carries the traveller to the loss of the false self before God's glory and grace brings him back sober, seeing God in all things while remaining himself. Imam Khomeini cites a saying of Imam ʿAli (as) to show what creation becomes for such a person: no longer a veil that hides God, but a place where He is seen.

[quotation]

¶30. In Imam ʿAli's line the world has not disappeared and neither has the one who looks at it. Nothing in it hides God from his sight.

¶31. Others have walked this far. Where did the road come from and who walked it first?
