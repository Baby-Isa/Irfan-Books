# Chain packet 04x

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

¶1. In the month of Shaʿban, many of us recite the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. One of its lines is a confession.

[quotation]

¶2. The prayer offers these words as a "confession of my sins" and most of us say them in that spirit. They also say something about the soul. It was given into our care and it can be neglected.

¶3. The soul deserves that care because everything we know, we know with it. Our eyes and ears take in the world, but the soul is what understands what they bring. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the soul is "the means to understand and to learn".

¶4. The soul is also the one thing we know from the inside. Everything else reaches us through an idea of it and the idea can be wrong. Our own hunger we know with no idea in between and the soul knows itself in the same direct way. This is knowledge by presence (ʿilm al-huduri). The soul's knowledge of itself is the clearest case, since here the one who perceives and the thing perceived are the same.

¶5. Because the soul is both the instrument of all knowing and the one thing we know directly, the theory of ʿirfan begins with it. Nothing else can be studied until that instrument is understood. Knowing God, rather than knowing about Him, needs this same direct knowing and the soul is the one place where we have it. This chapter sets out why we must know ourselves to know our Lord and how.

## Section 2: The human story is the soul's story

**Point:** The Qur'an places the human story in the soul: whoever purifies it succeeds, and whoever betrays it fails.

¶6. The Qur'an gives the reason we must know ourselves. Surah al-Shams opens with a run of oaths: by the sun and the moon, the day and the night and the sky and the earth. The last oath is by the soul and "Him who fashioned it and inspired it with [discernment between] its virtues and vices". Every soul comes with the means to tell good from evil and the next two verses say what follows from that gift.

[verse]

¶7. Success and failure, in these verses, rest on one thing: what is done with the soul. Whether a person purifies the soul or betrays it decides the outcome of their life.

¶8. The whole story of a human being in this world is therefore the story of the soul. We are here to develop it and to make of it what we choose. The Munajat's confession names the failure the surah describes, since a soul that has not been looked after is a soul that has not been purified.

[box] Mirza Muhammad ʿAli Shahabadi was the teacher who most shaped Imam Khomeini's spiritual life. Imam Khomeini calls him "our shaykh" (shaykhuna), and their relationship "was that of a murid with his murshid" (a disciple with his spiritual guide). Shahabadi taught a few chosen students the Fusus al-Hikam of Ibn ʿArabi, one of the most demanding books of mystical theory. For the care of the soul, however, he sent them to the Qur'an. He told them to attend constantly to the closing verses of Surah al-Hashr, which begin by telling every soul to consider what it sends ahead for tomorrow. Reciting them, he said, is "very effective for curing the soul and for holding the mischief of the self and Satan at bay".

## Section 3: Forgotten together, known together

**Point:** Knowing God begins with knowing the soul, because the two are forgotten together and known together.

¶9. If the human story is the soul's story, losing sight of the soul is the deepest loss. Surah al-Hashr names its cause: forgetting God.

[verse]

¶10. The verse calls such people transgressors. Sheikh Bahmanpour counts most of us, practising Muslims included, among those who have forgotten their souls. He does not mean that we will be punished for it. He means that we do not realise the potential our souls hold and so we do not develop them as we could.

¶11. A forgotten soul also hides its own faults. The worst sins are the ones we do not recognise as sins, because what is not recognised cannot be cured. This is one reason believers are told to seek God's forgiveness (istighfar) at all times: it covers the sins we commit without seeing them.

¶12. The cure follows from the verse. If forgetting God makes us forget the soul, remembering God (dhikr) turns our attention back to it. This is how the mystics read the verse. The return comes in stages. A person first turns back to the self and sees that it must have a Creator. Full knowledge of the soul comes later, as the remembrance of God deepens.

¶13. The link also runs from the soul to faith. Surah al-Anʿam says what is lost along with the soul.

[verse]

¶14. A ruined soul loses faith because its owner can no longer see it. A soul that is seen points to its Maker. The body is put together from parts and a person can suppose that matter assembled it by itself. The soul is not put together from parts, so it cannot be put down to chance. As soon as it is seen, its Creator is seen. The lectures give one cause for every theory that puts the world down to chance: those who hold it cannot see their own souls.

¶15. Forgetting runs in both directions and so does knowing. A saying handed down from the Prophet ﷺ and from Imam ʿAli (as), puts it in a single line.

[quotation]

¶16. The saying holds because the soul reflects its Maker. God's qualities are infinite and differ greatly from the soul's. Yet there is a likeness and through it God can be known by looking within. This does not make the soul a part of God. The soul carries something of the spirit (ruh) that God breathed into the first human being. That spirit is something God created, not God Himself.

¶17. Knowing God therefore begins with knowing the soul. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi draws this from a verse that tells believers to take care of their own souls. From it he concludes that the seeker "must see Allah through the window of his own soul".

[box] The saying is short in Arabic: man ʿarafa nafsahu faqad ʿarafa rabbahu. Its verb, ʿarafa (to know), shares its root with maʿrifah and ʿirfan, and its nafs is the soul that Surah al-Shams swears by. Mohammad Ali Shomali writes that "it is difficult to find a book on ethics (Akhlaq), which has not quoted it". It comes down by two routes. Lantern of the Path, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), gives it as the words of the Prophet. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi quotes it from Imam ʿAli (as), as the lectures do. The saying has also been read two ways. Some take it as a denial: no one can know their own soul completely, so no one can know their Lord. Others take it as a promise: whoever comes to know his soul does come to know his Lord. The lectures accept the first reading but prefer the second. They point to other narrations, which show that the saying speaks of a knowledge we can reach.

## Section 4: Near already: lifting the veils

**Point:** God is already near, so reaching Him means lifting, in this life, the veils over our own soul.

¶18. Looking within does not mean going anywhere, because God is already near. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "We are actually in [H]is presence at the moment. However, [H]e is not in our presence." The soul stands before God now. What it lacks is the perception of the One before whom it stands.

¶19. Reaching God therefore covers no distance. The journey to Him is a sharpening of the heart's sight, not a movement from one place to another. What stands between the soul and Him is a veil over the heart (hijab, plural hujub). To be brought near to God is to have such a veil removed. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah asks for this removal when it prays that the eyes of our hearts pass even through the veils of light. Chapter Eight sets out these veils and the stages in which they are lifted.

¶20. For every soul, the veils will one day come off, at the meeting with God that the Qur'an promises. Surah al-Anʿam warns those who deny that meeting.

[verse]

¶21. The verse speaks of the meeting with God (liqaʾ Allah). Every soul will take part in this meeting, believer or unbeliever, because every soul can perceive God. At the meeting, as the lectures put it, "We remove the layers that cover our soul". Each of us will see our own soul as it is.

¶22. What is seen at that meeting is the soul and the soul is a reflection of God. Seeing it is therefore seeing a reflection of God and not God Himself. The verse still calls it a meeting with Him, because on that day God becomes present to the soul. The believer perceives His mercy and reward, whereas the unbeliever perceives His justice.

¶23. Even unveiled, the soul does not give a complete knowledge of God. The soul knows itself by presence, yet even so it cannot see to its own depths. If our own soul cannot be known completely, still less can its Lord, who is without limit.

¶24. Imam Khomeini draws the same line. Some scholars take every mention of liqaʾ Allah to mean the Last Day, with its reward and punishment. Imam Khomeini holds that the meeting is open to the seeker as well. Yet even those who hold the meeting open do not mean, he writes, "that it is permissible to fathom the mystery of the Divine Essence". The meeting brings nearness to God (qurb), never a grasp of Him.

¶25. What a soul perceives of God at that meeting depends on what it perceives of Him now. The lectures draw this from Surah al-Isra.

[verse]

¶26. If blindness in this world carries into the next, the veils cannot be left for that day. The whole effort of the path is to remove them now, so that the heart can see the glory of God and feel His presence. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi says what the seeker finds behind them. The one given God's grace can "remove the material curtain from his spirit and self" and see the self's reality. Such knowledge of the self, he writes, is the meaning of Imam ʿAli's (as) saying that whoever knows himself knows his Lord.

¶27. The way to God therefore runs through the self. God is already near and the soul reflects Him. What keeps us from Him is a veil over our own soul. Lifting it means coming to know the soul as it is, which is how we come to know our Lord.

¶28. If the way to God runs through the self, how deep does the self go?
