# Chain packet p05

## Section 1: Depths, not distances

**Point:** The material world, the middle world of images, and the world of intellects are one reality at three depths, and each of us already knows a sample of all three from our senses, our imagination, and our understanding. The dead have not gone far: they have passed into a deeper side of the world we live in now.

¶1. The mystics hold that the human soul is the whole of creation in small, a compact copy of everything that exists. If that is right, the three powers by which we know things, the senses, the imagination and the intellect, should each give us a sample of one world and each world can be approached through something we already know.

¶2. The first world is the one our senses meet: ʿalam al-mulk, the material world. It is made of matter, things with weight that take up space, such as a table, a hand or the rain. It is also called the witnessed world, because our senses witness it and the other two worlds are called unseen. They are unseen not because they are far away but because the ordinary eye does not perceive them without training.

¶3. The second world can be approached through the imagination. When we picture a friend's face, the picture has a shape and a colour, yet it weighs nothing and is made of nothing material. A dream does the same thing more vividly. We see people who move and speak, with faces, sizes and clothes of a certain colour and none of it is matter.

¶4. Our imagination is a sample of a larger world of this kind, ʿalam al-mithal, the world of images. That world keeps some features of matter, such as shape, size and colour, while having no matter at all. The difference is that the picture in our mind belongs to us, whereas the world of images depends on no one's mind. It exists in its own right and it is the world the dead enter.

¶5. The third world can be approached through the intellect. Each person we have met has particular features: a height, a voice, a face. Yet the mind can grasp "a human being" with no particular features at all, though no such person exists anywhere outside it. Understanding of this kind deals in meanings, with no image and no matter. Its world is ʿalam al-ʿuqul, the world of intellects and its inhabitants, the angels, are pure intellect, attached to nothing material.

¶6. The world of images stands between the other two and this is why it is also called barzakh, a barrier. Pure intellect and matter have nothing in common, so they cannot meet directly and a middle world is needed to bring them together. Whatever is in the world of intellects is reflected first in the world of images, which has something of matter's shape and colour without its weight, before anything reaches the world of matter.

¶7. None of these worlds is a place we travel to. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the world of images "is another dimension of the same world in which we are living" and so is the world of intellects. The unseen worlds surround this one as an envelope surrounds a letter, except that the surrounding is in depth and not in distance: each is a deeper dimension of the world we are in now. The tradition names still greater realms beyond them, the Footstool (kursi), the Throne (ʿarsh) and jabarut, which no one can picture.

¶8. This changes the picture at the graveside. The dead have not been taken to a distant room; they have passed into a deeper dimension of the world we are living in. Even the resurrection is not a journey elsewhere but a move deeper still, closer to the majesty and beauty of God.

[box] A barzakh is a barrier, something that stands between two things and keeps each in its place. The Qur'an uses the word for the barrier God set between two seas that He merged, one sweet and one briny. The ʿurafaʾ use it for the world of images, which stands between matter and pure intellect and links them. Mohammad Ali Shomali notes that philosophers compare the body of that world with the bodies we see in dreams, and "call them 'Barzakh' or 'mithali'". The Qur'an also uses the word of death. When death comes, the verse says, a person asks to be taken back so that he may act righteously in what he left behind, and he is told that a barzakh stands before him until the day he is raised. The barrier stands between this life and the resurrection.

## Section 2: A soul that crosses them

**Point:** The soul has a share in all three worlds, and it passes into the middle world whenever its attention to the body loosens: in sleep, at death, and in contemplation. Unlike the angels it has no fixed station, so how far it goes is left open to the life it lives.

¶9. The soul has a share in each of the three worlds. Chapter Four said that it carries something of the spirit God breathed into the first human being. A soul was needed, on this teaching, because pure spirit and a body of clay have nothing in common, so something that reaches both had to stand between them, as the world of images stands between the world of intellects and ours. Each of us has an existence here and a counterpart in the world of images and the counterpart is far larger than the self that walks about here.

¶10. For now the soul's attention is held almost wholly by the body, so it lives in the witnessed world. There are three times when that hold loosens: in sleep, at death and in contemplation.

¶11. The first is sleep. When we sleep, the soul attends less to the body. With the senses no longer crowding out its other perceptions, it can touch the world of images, though it does not leave the body completely; if it did, we would be dead. This explains a dream that is more than memory. A person may see someone in a dream doing something that later happens and a memory cannot show the future. Time works differently in the middle world and the soul has seen there the counterpart of an event that has not yet happened here.

¶12. The Qur'an sets sleep and death side by side in Surah al-Zumar.

[verse]

¶13. The verse speaks of the soul being taken in both and Mohammad Ali Shomali explains the difference of degree. In sleep the spirit is still connected with the body, "but to a lesser degree than waking". At death it is disconnected from the body and "belongs to another body which has the qualities of a material body like shape and size without the mass". This second body is the badan mithali, the image body: it has our shape, but it is not made of any material elements.

¶14. Death, then, is not the soul slipping out of a body that held it. "The body is not carrying the soul but the soul is carrying the body," Sheikh Bahmanpour says and when the appointed time comes, the soul sets the body down and goes. It does not go without a body, because a soul cannot be without one. The image body was with us all along, in another dimension and unseen and the soul goes on in it. The image body is not the soul itself; it is the body the soul has in the middle world.

¶15. The third loosening can happen while we are awake. Sleep showed that when the soul's attention leaves the body, it touches the middle world. A person who turns that attention to Allah until they become unaware of the body does the same thing awake and sees there as one sees in sleep. This is the work of muraqaba, watchfulness: keeping the heart's attention on God. Nothing leaves the body in it, the Sheikh makes clear; what is turned is the attention.

¶16. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi was known for such moments. They come to the mystics, Sheikh Bahmanpour explains, as an opportunity given by Allah and not as a power to be used; only the Imams (as) can pass between the worlds at will. The more Tabatabaʾi kept up his muraqaba, he said, the more distinctly he saw. None of this is the aim of the path. Chapter One placed that aim in God alone: powers of any kind, sought for themselves, make the goal something other than God.

[box] Kernel of the Kernels, the record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures on the path, describes what may come to a traveller who keeps up muraqaba. Lights appear, at first like flashes of lightning, and "these lights belong to the class of entities of the realm of Barzakh". In the early stages the traveller may see his own soul in bodily form: "he might see himself standing before himself." The lectures then tell of their teacher, Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, who used to say: "One day emerging from the room into the veranda, I saw myself standing silent by my side. I looked very carefully at my face, and observed a mole on it. On returning to my room when I looked into the mirror, I saw that there was a mole on my face that I had not noticed until then".

¶17. Sleep, death and contemplation show a soul that can move between worlds. The angels show what it is to have no such movement and Surah al-Saffat gives their own words.

[verse]

¶18. Each angel has a known place, a fixed station that it cannot rise above. An angel does not change or grow, because it is made as it will always be. The human soul is different. It can move and change and no one knows how far it can go; the mystics say that this capacity is why the angels bowed down to Adam. How far a human soul travels is not settled at its creation, as an angel's station is and it is left to the life the soul lives.

## Section 3: The form our deeds make

**Point:** The image body the soul carries into the middle world is shaped by its deeds, as a habit shapes a character: sin deforms it and piety makes it shine. What the soul meets there is in large part its own deeds made visible, and that form is being made now.

¶19. If every soul passes into the middle world in any case, as the dead at the graveside already have, it can seem that what we do here changes little. The answer lies in the image body and a familiar process shows how. Shomali takes the example of lying. A child telling a first lie feels uneasy and later remorseful; "as one repeats this behaviour though, the soul becomes inured to the effects", until lying costs little effort.

¶20. Repeated acts shape the self in this way and some of the shaping shows. A face can show faith or its absence to those with eyes to see it and a pious person's face can carry light. The change is slow and small and it never deforms the body. The image body is far more sensitive. Sins deform it and piety makes it shine, so that the image body takes the shape of what the habits have made of the soul.

¶21. Imam Khomeini describes this second form in his Forty Hadith. Beside the body that everyone can see, each person has "another form and a different face of him, which is heavenly and invisible, which is determined by the qualities of his soul and his inner nature". The state of the soul when it leaves the body fixes that form: "in whichever state one's soul departs from the body, it assumes a form suited to it in the next world also".

¶22. The Qur'an describes people coming forth on the Last Day in Surah al-Nabaʾ.

[verse]

¶23. The hadith, as Shomali reports them, say that the groups are formed "according to their true natures". On that day, "when the veil is finally lifted from before our eyes, we will see ourselves and others as they really are". Some "could have fallen lower than a bug, while others could have ascended higher than angels". The last section found that the soul, unlike an angel, has no fixed station. Here is the other side of that freedom: a soul can sink below every animal as well as rise above the angels.

¶24. The shining of the pious is described in the Qur'an too, in Surah al-Hadid.

[verse]

¶25. The light that the faithful carry goes ahead of them and lights their way and it is their own. Shomali writes of reward and punishment in the same terms. "Sometimes rewards or punishments are nothing other than the actions," he writes. "They are just the realities of those actions made manifest in another universe".

¶26. If a reward can be the deed itself made visible, the form a soul meets after death is in large part the form its own deeds have been giving it. In Shomali's words, "our future is now present". The image body is being shaped today by what we do today.

## Section 4: The self can change its state

**Point:** There is one nafs, called commanding, self-blaming, or at peace according to its state, and it can move from one state to the next. Remembrance, fasting, and the night prayer move it, and whatever we make of it stays with us forever, so what the self finally becomes is in our hands.

¶27. If deeds give the soul its form, the self that does the deeds is not fixed either. The Qur'an names the nafs, the self, in three states. The first is the self that commands to evil, al-nafs al-ammara, named in Surah Yusuf.

[verse]

¶28. The verse names the self at its lowest and, in the same breath, the mercy of God that can lift it. The self commands to evil when the bodily side of a person overcomes the spiritual side and the soul goes after the body's pleasures. The pull is not only towards pleasure. Worrying whether our children will be poor or whether we will have a good job next year, also binds the soul to the body and keeps it from rising towards God.

¶29. The second is the self that blames itself, al-nafs al-lawwama, by which Surah al-Qiyama swears.

[verse]

¶30. After giving in, this self remembers what it has done and asks why it did it. The self-blame is a hopeful sign, because it shows that we hold other values, which matter more to us than the pleasure we gave in to.

¶31. The third is the soul at peace, al-nafs al-mutmaʾinna, which Surah al-Fajr calls back to its Lord and to which Chapter Seventeen returns. A person who keeps up the self-blame of the second state comes in time to rely on what belongs to the other world and not on this one. The self then becomes tranquil, free of sin, of greed and of a mind turned wholly towards the body.

¶32. The three are not separate selves. "We don't have different types of nufus," Sheikh Bahmanpour says, using the plural of nafs. The one self takes a different name according to its attitude and because we can change the attitude, we can change the name.

¶33. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, a Shia master of an earlier age, counts four states where the lecture counts three. In his Inner Secrets of the Path, the self ascends from "commanding to evil" to the "reproachful", from there to the "inspired" and finally to the station where it is "at rest". Both counts describe one self climbing. Amuli sets two lines of verse beside the climb: "If the self is neglected, then it keeps company with meanness, / And if it is urged on then, it longs for nobler qualities".

[box] The ʿurafaʾ give the one soul several names, one for each depth it reaches. Murtada Mutahhari sets out four. When the soul is "dominated and ruled by desires and passions they call it nafs". When it bears divine knowledge it is qalb, the heart. When "the light of Divine love dawns within it", it is ruh, the spirit, and when it reaches witnessing it is sirr, the mystery. Beyond these lie khafi, the hidden, and akhfa, the most hidden. The lecture's list differs in order and detail: it describes the sirr as known only to God, the khafi as hidden even from the angels, and the qalb as the heart that keeps turning, receiving from God and attending to the body. Imam Khomeini notes that the soul's levels are divided "sometimes into seven sections and sometimes into four", and sometimes into three or two. Chapter Eight takes nafs, qalb, and ruh as stages of the road.

¶34. What urges the self on are acts already familiar to us and the first is the remembrance of God, dhikr. Surah al-Raʿd names what it gives.

[verse]

¶35. The rest the verse promises is the peace of the soul at peace and only remembrance gives it. On this teaching, meditation that consists of sitting still and thinking of nothing is shallow. Real meditation is reflection inside the soul and it is carried by dhikr, which refreshes and revives the soul. Chapter Four's verse said that those who forget God are made to forget their own souls; the reverse holds too and without remembering God a person cannot come to know their own soul.

¶36. Fasting and the night prayer work on the same tie to the body that sleep and muraqaba loosen. The soul manages the body, down to the digestion of a meal, so a body that is not eating asks less of it. In Ramadan Allah pours down His light everywhere and the soul of the one who fasts, less taken up with the body, takes in that light without knowing it. Salat al-layl, the night prayer, likewise helps the soul free itself from the body and see the light.

¶37. These acts work because the soul takes on habits quickly. Trained to worry about the body, it worries about the body; trained to care for higher things, it comes to care for them and to see the light. In the lecture's image, the soul is like a fine elastic thing that can be held in the palm of a hand or stretched to the size of the universe and it has been entrusted to us for a short time. Whatever we do with it, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, "would remain with us forever".

¶38. The self is therefore deeper than it appears. It lives in the material world and in the world of images and it reaches towards the world of intellects; it passes into the middle world a little each night in sleep and fully at death. Its deeds give it a form that it will meet there and the self that does them can move from commanding, to blaming, to peace. What it finally becomes has been entrusted to us and it is in our hands.
