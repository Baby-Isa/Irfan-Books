# Chain packet 04y

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

¶1. In the month of Shaʿban, many of us recite the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah. One of its lines is a confession.

[quotation]

¶2. The prayer offers these words as a "confession of my sins". They also say something about the soul. It was given into our care and it can be neglected.

¶3. The soul deserves that care because everything we know, we know with it. Our eyes and ears take in the world, but the soul is what understands what they bring. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, the soul is "the means to understand and to learn".

¶4. We also know the soul from the inside. The images in our mind are known this way: they are present to the soul, so we know them with nothing in between. This is knowledge by presence (ʿilm al-huduri). The soul's knowledge of itself is its clearest case, since here the one who perceives and the thing perceived are the same.

¶5. The theory of ʿirfan therefore begins with the soul, since every other study is carried out with it. Knowing God, rather than knowing about Him, would need this same direct knowing and so it too must happen in the soul. This chapter sets out why we must know ourselves to know our Lord and how.

## Section 2: The human story is the soul's story

**Point:** The Qur'an places the human story in the soul: whoever purifies it succeeds, and whoever betrays it fails.

¶6. The Qur'an gives the reason we must know ourselves. Surah al-Shams opens with a run of oaths: by the sun and the moon, the day and the night, the sky and Him who built it and the earth and Him who spread it. The last oath is by the soul and "Him who fashioned it and inspired it with [discernment between] its virtues and vices". Every soul comes with the means to tell good from evil and the next two verses say what follows from that gift.

[verse]

¶7. Success and failure, in these verses, turn on what is done with the soul. Whether a person purifies the soul or betrays it decides the outcome of their life.

¶8. The whole story of a human being in this world is therefore the story of the soul. We are here to develop it and to make of it what we choose. The Munajat's confession names the failure the surah describes, since a soul that has not been looked after is a soul that has not been purified.

[box] Mirza Muhammad ʿAli Shahabadi was the teacher who most shaped Imam Khomeini's spiritual life. Imam Khomeini refers to him as "our shaykh" (shaykhuna), and the editor of the Forty Hadith describes their relationship as "that of a murid with his murshid" (a disciple with his spiritual guide). Shahabadi taught a few select students the Fusus al-Hikam of Ibn ʿArabi. For the care of the soul, however, he sent them to the Qur'an. He told them to attend constantly to the closing verses of Surah al-Hashr, which begin by telling every soul to consider what it sends ahead for tomorrow. Reciting them, he said, is "very effective for curing the soul and for holding the mischief of the self and Satan at bay".

## Section 3: Forgotten together, known together

**Point:** Knowing God begins with knowing the soul, because the two are forgotten together and known together.

¶9. If the human story is the soul's story, losing sight of the soul is the deepest loss. Surah al-Hashr names its cause: forgetting God.

[verse]

¶10. The verse calls such people transgressors. Sheikh Bahmanpour counts most of us, Muslims included, among those who have forgotten their souls. He does not mean that we will necessarily be punished for it. He means that we do not realise the potential our souls hold and so we do not develop them as we could.

¶11. The cure follows from the verse. If forgetting God makes us forget the soul, remembering God (dhikr) turns our attention back to it. This is how the mystics read the verse. The return comes in stages. A person first turns back to the self and sees that it must have a Creator. Fuller knowledge of the soul comes later, as the remembrance of God deepens.

¶12. Where remembering God brings the soul back, losing the soul takes faith with it. Surah al-Anʿam says so.

[verse]

¶13. A ruined soul loses faith because its owner can no longer see it. A soul that is seen points to its Maker. The body is put together from parts and a person can suppose that it came about by an evolution of matter. The soul is not like that: as soon as it is seen, its Creator is seen.

¶14. Forgetting runs in both directions and so does knowing. A saying handed down from the Prophet ﷺ and from Imam ʿAli (as), puts it in a single line.

[quotation]

¶15. The saying holds because the soul reflects its Maker. God's qualities are infinite and differ greatly from the soul's. Yet there is a likeness and through it God can be known by looking within. This does not make the soul a part of God. The soul carries something of the spirit (ruh) that God breathed into the first human being. That spirit is something God created, not God Himself.

¶16. Knowing God therefore begins with knowing the soul. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi ties this to a verse that tells believers to take care of their own souls. He writes that a believer who hears this verse with the desire to remember Allah alone "must see Allah through the window of his own soul".

[box] The saying is short in Arabic: man ʿarafa nafsahu faqad ʿarafa rabbahu. Its verb, ʿarafa (to know), shares its root with maʿrifah and ʿirfan, and its nafs is the soul that Surah al-Shams swears by. Mohammad Ali Shomali writes that "it is difficult to find a book on ethics (Akhlaq), which has not quoted it". Some sources give it to the Prophet and others to Imam ʿAli (as). Lantern of the Path, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), gives it as the words of the Prophet. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi quotes it from Imam ʿAli (as), and so do the slides for the lectures. The saying has also been read two ways. Some take it as a denial: no one can know their own soul completely, so no one can know their Lord. Others take it as a promise: whoever comes to know his soul does come to know his Lord. The lectures accept the first reading but prefer the second. They point to other narrations, which show that the saying speaks of a knowledge we can reach.

## Section 4: Near already: lifting the veils

**Point:** God is already near, so reaching Him means lifting, in this life, the veils over our own soul.

¶17. Looking within does not mean going anywhere, because God is already near. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "We are actually in [H]is presence at the moment. However, [H]e is not in our presence." The soul stands before God now. What it lacks is the perception of the One before whom it stands.

¶18. Reaching God therefore covers no distance. The journey to Him is a sharpening of the heart's sight, not a movement from one place to another. What stands between the soul and Him is a veil over the heart (hijab, plural hujub). Coming near to God (qurb) means having such a veil removed. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah asks that the eyes of our hearts penetrate even the veils of light. Chapter Eight sets out these veils and the stages in which they are lifted.

¶19. For every soul, the veils will one day come off, at the meeting with God that the Qur'an promises. Surah al-Anʿam warns those who deny that meeting.

[verse]

¶20. The verse speaks of the meeting with God (liqaʾ Allah). Every soul will take part in this meeting, believer or unbeliever, because every soul can perceive God. At the meeting, as the lectures put it, "We remove the layers that cover our soul". Each of us will see our own soul as it is.

¶21. What is seen at that meeting is the soul and the soul is a reflection of God. Seeing it is therefore seeing a reflection of God and not God Himself. The verse still calls it a meeting with Him. On that day the soul perceives the presence it has always stood in. The believer perceives God's mercy and reward, whereas the unbeliever perceives His justice.

¶22. Even then, the soul cannot encompass God. The depth of our own soul cannot be known, so a complete knowledge of its Lord is out of reach. As the lectures put it, "You can get some understanding but knowing [H]im completely … is impossible." Imam Khomeini sets a similar limit for the seeker. Those who hold that the meeting with God is open to the seeker, he writes, do not mean "that it is permissible to fathom the mystery of the Divine Essence", nor that the Essence can be encompassed even by knowledge by presence.

¶23. What a soul perceives of God at that meeting depends on what it perceives of Him now. In the lectures' words, "whoever is completely blind in this world is completely blind in the other world." The Qur'an uses the same image.

[verse]

¶24. The seeker therefore begins lifting the veils in this life. There is a second reason not to wait: the journey every soul makes through death is longer and harder than the one a seeker takes by choice. In the Sheikh's account, the many methods the mystics have devised all aim at one thing, removing the veil that covers the heart.

¶25. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi describes what the seeker finds when the self is uncovered. The one given God's grace can "remove the material curtain from his spirit and self" and see the self's reality. For him, this knowledge of the self is the meaning of Imam ʿAli's (as) saying that whoever knows himself knows his Lord.

¶26. The way to God therefore runs through the self. God is already near and reflected in the soul and the veil that keeps us from Him lies over the soul itself.

¶27. If the way to God runs through the self, how deep does the self go?
