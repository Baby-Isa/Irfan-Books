# Chain packet c982

## Section 1: Cut off from all else

**Point:** The prayers' first request is that the heart be cut off from everything but God. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah and Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali ask for it, and Duʿa ʿArafah describes it in those God loves. The severance they mean is in the heart, not a withdrawal from family or society.

¶1. Let's start with the order in which the prayers ask. Sheikh Bahmanpour reads the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah as a sequence. Those who seek God, he says, want to know Him and to look at Him, but "they have to cut off from everything first". In the prayer, the request for light in the eyes of the heart, with which this book opened, follows straight on from a request to be cut off.

[quotation]

¶2. The Arabic word for this severance is inqitaʿ, being cut off and the Munajat asks for it complete. Sheikh Bahmanpour asks whether it is good to be cut off completely from everything else. In the heart, he answers, it is. He places this severance a step beyond the sensitivity to God's signs that Chapter One described.

¶3. The severance the prayers ask for is therefore in the heart, not in the household or the marketplace. It is the severance Chapter One found in the friends of God, who keep their families and their place in society while their hearts are cut off from everything else.

¶4. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali makes the Munajat's request in its own way, naming the attachment it wants removed. "O my Master, (please) expel love for the world out of my heart," it asks (سَيِّدِي أَخْرِجْ حُبَّ ٱلدُّنْيَا مِنْ قَلْبِي). What the prayer asks to lose is the heart's love for the world, not the world itself or the people and the work in it.

¶5. Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, describes the same severance from the other side, as something God has already done for those He loves.

[quotation]

¶6. The line names God as the one who cleared these hearts. He removed love for others from them and what remained was love for Him alone.

## Section 2: To see, to love, to draw near

**Point:** A heart cut off from all else is free for God, and the prayers go on to ask to see Him with the heart, to love Him and to come near Him. These are the knowing and the love that Chapter 1 named as the two aims of ʿirfan.

¶7. A heart cleared of everything but God is free for Him. The prayers go on to ask for three things: to see God with the heart, to love Him and to come near Him. Let's take them in turn.

¶8. The first is sight, the subject of the Munajat's petition for light in the eyes of the heart. Sheikh Bahmanpour explains why the request is made of the heart. The eyes in our heads work and still we miss the signs, because the faculty behind them is blind. The prayer asks God to give that inner eye light to see by.

¶9. The same petition asks that these eyes pass through the veils of light. The mystics speak of veils of light and darkness that the heart must pass through and Sheikh Bahmanpour notes that people unfamiliar with such words sometimes take them for mistakes. The phrase comes from the Munajat itself.

¶10. Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, speaks of the heart's light as something God has already given to some.

[quotation]

¶11. The word translated "Your intimate servants" is awliyaʾ, the friends of God whom Chapter One met in Surah Yunus. Chapter Two described knowledge of God as a light that God places in a heart that has been purified. This line says where He has placed it, in the hearts of His friends.

¶12. The second request is love. The Munajat asks for a place among God's friends and it defines that place by love.

[quotation]

¶13. Sheikh Bahmanpour reads the friendship asked for here, wilaya, as having two parts. One is maʿrifah: understanding God, looking at Him, knowing Him. The other is love for Him. In his reading neither is fixed; the love is incessant and the maʿrifah keeps increasing. These are the two aims Chapter One gave ʿirfan and the Sheikh finds both in one line of the Munajat.

¶14. The third request is nearness and the Munajat makes it earlier in the same prayer.

[quotation]

¶15. In this request the heart is brought near by its own passion for God. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali goes further and asks to love the meeting with God itself. "O Lord of Glory and Honor, (please do) make me love meeting You," it says (يَا ذَا ٱلْجَلالِ وَٱلإِكْرَامِ حَبِّبْ إِلَيَّ لِقَاءَكَ).

¶16. The Whispered Prayer of the Lovers asks for a place among those who have already been brought near. It is one of the fifteen whispered prayers printed with the Sahifa and Sheikh Bahmanpour read several of its lines to his listeners.

[quotation]

¶17. For some of us, the Sheikh says, calling on God can feel like calling into the sky to someone far away. For some, God is present when they call and they feel His response. The prayer asks to be placed with these: among those chosen for His nearness and granted the sight of His face.

¶18. Murtada Mutahhari names these prayers together in his introduction to ʿirfan. "The Du'a' Kumayl, the Du'a' Abi Hamzah, the supplications of al-Sahifat al-Kamilah and the group of supplications called Sha'baniyyah," he writes, "all contain the most sublime spiritual ideas". He cites them to show that ʿirfan has its sources inside Islam, with no need to look for its origin elsewhere.

## Section 3: A pupil reading above his level

**Point:** The prayers ask from need and humility, in words far above our level, as if a primary school pupil were handed a university book. Sheikh Bahmanpour's answer is that we are honoured to be allowed to say them, and that God may grant a sincere reciter's request.

¶19. Requests this high raise a difficulty and Sheikh Bahmanpour states it himself. These words, he says, belong to the peak of the understanding and longing of the muqarrabun, those brought near to God. He asks why we are told to recite them at all. Telling ordinary believers to say them, he says, is "like [telling] a primary school boy to read books of university".

¶20. The prayers themselves speak from need, as a pupil would. Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, offers God one thing as its means of approach, the supplicant's need of Him.

[quotation]

¶21. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali makes the same plea from need and places the one who prays at a door. "O my Master, I, Your servant, am standing at Your door owing to neediness," it says (سَيِّدِي عَبْدُكَ بِبَابِكَ أَقَامَتْهُ ٱلْخَصَاصَةُ بَيْنَ يَدَيْكَ). The servant stands there because of need and the prayer gives no other reason for coming.

¶22. The line at the head of Part One takes this humility further. In it the Munajat admits that the one reciting had no strength to move away from disobedience "except when You awakened me to Your love". In this line, even the turning towards God is His doing.

¶23. Sheikh Bahmanpour's answer to his own question rests on the same humility. We do not understand many of the meanings of these words, he says and they are not of our level or within our capacity. Not understanding them does not make them meaningless. We have been honoured, because we are allowed to recite them. In his account, God invites the reciter to come and say them; if the reciter is sincere, God may grant the request. What the Imams asked for, the Sheikh adds, God granted them.

[box] Duʿa is the general word for supplication: any request addressed to God, in any words. A munajat is one kind of duʿa. The Mafatih's English calls the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah a "confidential prayer", and William Chittick translates the fifteen munajat printed with the Sahifa as "whispered prayers". Both translations describe speech meant for God alone. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi explains why the Imams' supplications reach further than their other words. In their narrations they spoke with people; in their supplications they spoke with God, and the level of speech depends on the understanding of the one addressed. The Qur'an, he writes, is a "Descending-Qur'an", God's words to His servants. The supplications are an "Ascending-Qur'an", the words of His most distinguished servants rising towards Him.

## Section 4: Prayers for ordinary nights

**Point:** These are the prayers of the Mafatih al-Jinan, set on nights and days that ordinary believers already keep. The scholars note that few reach their depth, while the Sheikh recommends them to everyone; the chapter claims access to the words, not attainment of what they ask.

¶24. God's invitation to recite these words comes through books that ordinary believers already use. Four of the prayers quoted in this chapter are in the Mafatih al-Jinan, the standard manual of Shia devotion. The Mafatih sets each of them on a night or a day in the believer's year.

[box] The Mafatih al-Jinan, "Keys to the Heavens", gathers the supplications of the Imams, the words to be recited at particular times, and the forms for visiting the Imams' shrines. Its compiler, Shaykh ʿAbbas Qummi, was a scholar of vast learning who died in Najaf in 1359/1940. Al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya is far older. William Chittick calls it "the oldest prayer manual in Islamic sources", composed by Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (ʿa), the fourth Imam; Shia tradition ranks it behind only the Qur'an and Nahj al-Balagha. The Imam died in Medina in 95/713–4, or a year earlier. The fifteen whispered prayers, the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers among them, were added to several modern editions of the Sahifa. They seem to have reached most Shia through ʿAllama Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, the compiler of Bihar al-Anwar.

¶25. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah is listed among the acts of the month of Shaʿban. The Mafatih gives a report that calls it the confidential prayer of Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams, which they used to say in that month. It adds that it is "advisable to say it with presence of heart all the time".

¶26. The other prayers have their own times. Duʿa Kumayl belongs to Thursday evenings and to the night of mid-Shaʿban. Duʿa ʿArafah is the prayer of Imam al-Husayn (as) on the Day of ʿArafah. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali belongs to Ramadan. Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as) said it in the last hour of the month's nights. Maliki Tabrizi tells the traveller not to forget it, every night or every other night.

¶27. Imam Khomeini, in one of his talks, takes this familiarity for granted. "You are already familiar with the words used in Imams' supplications," he tells his listeners, before reading them lines from the Shaʿbaniyyah.

¶28. Familiarity with the words is not the same as reaching what they describe and the scholars say so. Maliki Tabrizi, having called the supplications an Ascending-Qur'an, writes that this "is not accepted and comprehended by any one, except a small number of believers who are thoroughly familiar and enlightened". Imam Khomeini makes a narrower point. On one hard question, how to put God's relation to His creation into words, he says that "those who are fit to do so" should pray in the style of the Shaʿbaniyyah for God to enlighten them.

¶29. Sheikh Bahmanpour still recommends the Munajat to every listener, to be recited in Shaʿban as often as they can, while granting that we do not understand many of its meanings. The two positions answer different questions. The scholars speak of how far a reciter reaches; the Sheikh speaks of who may begin. What this chapter claims is access to these words, rather than attainment of what they ask for.

## Section 5: Words already on our lips

**Point:** The goal of ʿirfan is already on the believer's lips. The prayers we keep on ordinary nights ask, in the voice of need, for severance from all else, and for sight, love and nearness.

¶30. Access to these words is where an ordinary believer already stands on the path. Part One began with the Qur'an's invitation to more than the minimum. Chapter Two showed that the more is knowing God, not only knowing about Him, reached by purifying the heart. This chapter has found that knowing, with the love that goes with it, in prayers believers already say.

¶31. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, Duʿa Kumayl, Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali and Duʿa ʿArafah ask God to cut the heart off from all else, to light it, to fill it with His love and to bring it near. They ask in the voice of someone in need, on nights the ordinary believer keeps. The goal of ʿirfan is therefore not foreign to the believer: it is already on our lips. The full text of the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, in Arabic and English, is given in the Appendix.

¶32. These prayers keep asking God to work on the heart and the self. What is this self?
