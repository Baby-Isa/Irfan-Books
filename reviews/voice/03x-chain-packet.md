# Chain packet 03x

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

[quotation]

¶1. These words come near the end of Duʿa Kumayl, the prayer Imam ʿAli (as) taught to Kumayl ibn Ziyad, one of his closest disciples. The Mafatih al-Jinan recommends it on every Thursday evening, the night before Friday, when many of us hear it. The line asks God for a heart enthralled by His love.

¶2. Knowing God, rather than only knowing about Him, comes through purifying the heart. That leaves the question of where an ordinary believer already stands on this path. Duʿa Kumayl asks for the love that goes with that knowing and other prayers we recite ask for the knowing itself. This chapter sets out what these words we already know ask for: the knowing and love that ʿirfan seeks.

## Section 2: Cut off from all else

**Point:** The prayers' first request is a heart cut off from everything but God: a severance in the heart, not a withdrawal from the world.

¶3. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah asks to be cut off from everything before it asks to see God. This book opened on its request for light in the eyes of the heart. In the prayer, that line follows straight on from a request for severance. Sheikh Bahmanpour reads the order as deliberate: those who seek God want to know Him and to look at Him, but "they have to cut off from everything first".

[quotation]

¶4. The Munajat asks for this severance (inqitaʿ) in full. Taken at its word, the request could sound like a call to leave family, work and society behind, a reading the prayers themselves rule out. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali, recited on the nights of Ramadan, names exactly what is to be cut away.

[quotation]

¶5. The prayer asks to lose love for the world, not the world itself or the people and work in it.

¶6. The friends of God live out this severance: they keep their families and their place in society and it is their hearts that are cut off from everything but God. Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, describes the same severance from God's side: He has already made it in the hearts of those He loves.

[quotation]

¶7. What God removes in this line is love for others and He removes it from the heart. The severance the prayers ask for is therefore in the heart, not in our homes or our work.

## Section 3: To see, to love, to draw near

**Point:** The prayers then ask for what ʿirfan seeks: to see God with the heart, to love Him, and to come near Him.

¶8. A heart cleared of everything but God is free for Him. The prayers go on to ask for three things: to see God with the heart, to love Him and to come near Him.

¶9. The first is sight. The Munajat asks God to light the eyes of the heart so that they look at Him and pass even through the veils of light. Yet the Qur'an says that sight cannot perceive God, so some who meet these words take them for a mistake. Sheikh Bahmanpour raises this difficulty himself.

¶10. His answer, given in a later lecture, is that the eyes cannot catch God but the heart can. That agrees with the verse. Our physical eyes work perfectly well. We still miss God's signs, because the inner faculty behind them is blind. The prayer asks God to give that inner faculty, the eye of the heart, light to see by. Duʿa ʿArafah speaks of that light as something God has already lit in the hearts of His friends.

[quotation]

¶11. "Your intimate servants" here are the friends of God (awliyaʾ). The line names God as the one who lit their hearts. The lighting of any heart is His act, which is why the Munajat asks Him for the light.

¶12. The second request is love. The Munajat asks for a place among God's friends and it defines that place by love.

[quotation]

¶13. The Sheikh reads the friendship asked for here (wilaya) as having two parts. One is maʿrifah: understanding God, looking at Him and knowing Him. The other is love for Him. The love never stops and the knowing keeps growing. These are the two things ʿirfan seeks and both are in one line of the Munajat.

¶14. The third request is nearness and the Munajat makes it earlier in the same prayer.

[quotation]

¶15. In this request the heart is brought near by its own passion for God. The Whispered Prayer of the Lovers is one of fifteen whispered prayers printed with al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya. It asks for a place among those who have already been brought near (muqarrabun).

[quotation]

¶16. The difference nearness makes is felt in prayer. For some of us, calling on God can feel like calling into the sky to someone far away. Others feel Him present when they call and feel His response. With this third request, the prayers have asked for all that ʿirfan seeks: to see God with the heart, to love Him and to be near Him.

## Section 4: A pupil reading above his level

**Point:** Words above our level are still ours to say, because we say them from need.

¶17. Requests this high raise a difficulty, which Sheikh Bahmanpour puts plainly. These words come from the peak of understanding and longing, from those brought near to God, yet ordinary believers are told to recite them. That, in his words, is "like [telling] a primary school boy to read books of university".

¶18. The prayers themselves speak from need. Duʿa ʿArafah comes to God with nothing to offer but its need of Him.

[quotation]

¶19. Need is the only plea in this line and the Munajat line at the head of Part One goes further. There the reciter admits to having had no strength to move away from disobedience "except when You awakened me to Your love", so even the turning towards God is His doing.

¶20. The answer to the difficulty rests on the same humility. The words do not claim that the reciter has reached their level. They plead from below it. Many of their meanings are above us and not understanding them does not make them meaningless. We are honoured by the invitation to recite them. If the reciter is sincere, God may grant the request, as He granted the Imams what they asked.

[box] Duʿa is the general word for supplication: any request addressed to God, in any words. A munajat is one kind of duʿa. The Mafatih's English calls the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah a "confidential prayer", and William Chittick translates the fifteen munajat printed with the Sahifa as "whispered prayers". Both translations describe speech meant for God alone. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi explains why the Imams' supplications reach further than their other words. In their narrations they spoke with people, whereas in their supplications they spoke with God, and the level of speech depends on the understanding of the one addressed. The Qur'an, he writes, is a "Descending-Qur'an", God's words to His servants. The supplications are an "Ascending-Qur'an", the words of His most distinguished servants rising towards Him.

## Section 5: Prayers for ordinary nights

**Point:** These prayers are set on nights we already keep, so anyone may begin them.

¶21. The invitation to recite these words reaches us through a book most believers already own. Four of the prayers quoted in this chapter are in the Mafatih al-Jinan, which sets each of them on a night or a day in the believer's year.

¶22. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah is listed among the acts of the month of Shaʿban. The Mafatih reports it as the prayer Imam ʿAli (as) and the other Imams used to say in that month. It adds that it is "advisable to say it with presence of heart all the time". The full text, in Arabic and English, is given in the Appendix.

¶23. The other prayers have their own times. Duʿa Kumayl belongs to Thursday evenings and to the night of mid-Shaʿban. Duʿa ʿArafah is the prayer of Imam al-Husayn (as) on the Day of ʿArafah. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali belongs to Ramadan, when Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as) said it in the last hour of each night. Maliki Tabrizi tells the traveller not to forget it and to say it every night or every other night.

¶24. These nights make the words familiar, so familiar that Imam Khomeini could assume it in his listeners. "You are already familiar with the words used in Imams' supplications," he told them, before reading them lines from the Shaʿbaniyyah.

[box] The prayers in this chapter reach ordinary believers through two books. The Mafatih al-Jinan ("Keys to the Heavens") gathers the supplications of the Imams, the words to be recited at particular times, and the forms for visiting the Imams' shrines. Its compiler, Shaykh ʿAbbas Qummi, was a scholar of vast learning who died in Najaf in 1359/1940. Al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya is far older. William Chittick calls it "the oldest prayer manual in Islamic sources", composed by Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as). Shia tradition ranks it behind only the Qur'an and Nahj al-Balagha. The fourth Imam died in Medina in 95/713–4, or a year earlier. The fifteen whispered prayers, the Whispered Prayer of the Lovers among them, were added to several modern editions of the Sahifa. They seem to have reached most Shia through ʿAllama Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, the compiler of Bihar al-Anwar.

¶25. Familiarity with the words is not the same as reaching what they describe. Maliki Tabrizi sees the supplications as a servant's own words rising to God. That, he writes, "is not accepted and comprehended by any one, except a small number of believers who are thoroughly familiar and enlightened".

¶26. Sheikh Bahmanpour still recommends the Munajat to every listener, to be recited in Shaʿban as often as they can. The two positions answer different questions: how far a reciter reaches and who may begin. This chapter claims only the second, access to these words rather than attainment of what they ask for.

## Section 6: Words already on our lips

**Point:** The goal of ʿirfan is already on the believer's lips.

¶27. Access to these words is where an ordinary believer already stands on the path. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, Duʿa Kumayl, Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali and Duʿa ʿArafah ask for the knowing that ʿirfan seeks and the love that goes with it. They ask God to cut the heart off from all else, to light it, to fill it with His love and to bring it near.

¶28. They ask in the voice of someone in need, on nights the ordinary believer keeps. The goal of ʿirfan is therefore not foreign to us: it is already on our lips.

¶29. These prayers keep asking God to work on the heart, the self within us. What is this self?
