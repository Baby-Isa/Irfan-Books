# Chain packet 12b

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

¶1. Twice in every unit of the prayer (rakʿa), we go down into prostration (sujud). Every other act of the prayer is performed once. With our foreheads on the ground, we glorify our Lord, the Most High and praise Him. Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi calls this posture "the most supreme state and spiritual position for a human being, i.e., the position of annihilation in Allah" and recalls the tradition that a person in prostration "is nearer to Allah than in any other state".

¶2. The annihilation he names is what the mystics call passing away (fanaʾ) and in every prostration the body takes its posture for a moment. The traveller reaches the state itself only at the end of a long road. In the third stage, God adorned the emptied heart with His gifts and love carried the traveller until even love was left at the door. What remains before God is the traveller himself and the fourth and last stage is about him. This chapter sets out what passing away is: what is lost in losing the self, what is found in finding God and how the traveller comes back.

## Section 2: What is lost is the false self

**Point:** In passing away the traveller loses the sense of self before God's glory; he neither ceases to exist nor becomes God, so what is lost is a false self.

¶3. Passing away begins at the height of adorning. At the end of that stage God shows the traveller a small measure of His glory. No one can watch that glory and perceive himself at the same time. The glory does not wait for the traveller to clear a space for it. It comes and everything else goes from his sight, even his sense of himself. Something like it happens to a person seized by great fear or great joy. He can cut his hand and not feel it, because what is in front of him has taken all his attention. <!-- src: L07 ¶55-57 -->

¶4. Passing away is therefore a change in what the traveller sees and not in what exists. Sheikh Bahmanpour defines it as "the annihilation of self perception of the mystics in perceiving the unveiling [of] the reality of God". The world goes on as before. What changes is the traveller's knowing and so the lectures call passing away "an epistemological issue", a matter of perception and not of existence. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi says the same of the world around the traveller: "this does not mean that everything in the material world loses its existence. Actually, the devotee sees unity in plurality". <!-- src: L07 ¶59; L09 ¶4 -->

¶5. The traveller's soul remains too. What he loses is the sense of self, while "the soul is there, everything is there". Passing away is not nirvana, which the lectures describe as the Buddhist goal of ending individuality. In Islamic mysticism, "it is the individual who is going to be there forever getting more expanded, more capable". If the individual dissolved, no one would be left to taste what God shows, since only an individual can experience anything. The Qur'an keeps the individual even in paradise, where "Their Lord will give them to drink a pure drink". <!-- src: L07 ¶49-51, ¶56; L09 ¶8 -->

¶6. Nor does the traveller become God. Passing away is never a merging of the divine and the human. The lectures call such a merging impossible and the claim to it shirk or unbelief (kufr). Whoever claims to have dissolved in God or to have God dwelling in him, has not understood what happened to him. Before God's glory the traveller stops perceiving himself, but he never stops being a creature. <!-- src: L07 ¶60-61 -->

¶7. What goes, then, is the false picture of a self that stands on its own. This self is the last thing emptying leaves behind and passing away is where it falls. We lost our original self to worldly desires. Passing away breaks the walls of illusion built around us and returns us to the self God created. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, it is a move "out of the imaginary you to the real you". <!-- src: L09 ¶9 -->

[box] The two words for this stage come from one pair of verses in Surah al-Rahman.

[verse]

[box] "Ephemeral" translates fanin, from the root of fanaʾ, passing away; "lasting" translates yabqa, from the root of baqaʾ, remaining. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures read the Face of a thing as "that which manifests it", and the Face of God as His Names, through which every creature faces Him. Everyone on earth is ephemeral, as the verse says, and what lasts is the Face. The traveller who passes away from himself reaches "total annihilation (fana' kulli) and, thereafter, the station of life in God (baqa'), whereat eternal life becomes established for him".

¶8. What is lost in passing away is therefore the false picture of a self that stands apart from God. The soul and the world around it remain and God remains God.

## Section 3: Three degrees: in action, in attributes, in essence

**Point:** Passing away comes in three degrees, each the lived truth of words we already say.

¶9. Passing away does not come all at once. It comes in degrees and their order follows the order of the veils. Sayyid Haydar Amuli quotes a gnostic: "The essence is veiled by the attributes and the attributes by the action". The line names three layers, so the unveiling has three degrees. It runs from the outermost layer inwards, in action first, then in attributes and last in essence. (ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi counts four, adding a unity of the Names before the last.) Let's take the three in turn. Each makes words we already say into something the traveller sees.

¶10. The first degree is passing away in action. We think it is our eyes that see, whereas at this degree the traveller realises that "it is really not you who is seeing". When a hand moves, the power in it is God's, flowing into the hand. The intention is ours, though the lectures call its origin one of the most complicated issues. This is the lived truth of a phrase many of us repeat after the prayers, that there is no movement and no power except through God (la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah). The traveller comes to the place a verse of Surah al-Kahf describes. <!-- src: L09 ¶52-54, ¶60 -->

[verse]

¶11. That there is no authority but God's is something any believer can work out. A person can think about it for weeks and conclude that God created him and provides for him. The traveller at this degree tastes it. The difference is that between a recipe and the dish. Reading the recipe, we can imagine how the dish will taste, whereas only the cooked dish gives the taste. <!-- src: L09 ¶57-59 -->

¶12. The second degree is passing away in attributes. Wherever the traveller looks, even at the material world, he sees the attributes of God at work. Life, knowledge and power are in everything around him and all of them are His. This is the lived truth of words we say often, there is no god but God (la ilaha illa Allah), because Allah is the name that gathers all His attributes. The Throne Verse opens on the attribute of life. <!-- src: L09 ¶60-61 -->

[verse]

¶13. In this verse life belongs to God and whatever lives does so through Him. The same is true of movement, knowledge, power and energy, wherever they appear. At this degree the traveller sees them as His wherever he looks. <!-- src: L09 ¶63 -->

¶14. The third degree is passing away in essence. Here the traveller no longer sees even the attributes. In the lectures' words, there is "just His glory, which is overshadowing everything". What is left to him is one word, Huwa, "He". The Qur'an uses this pronoun for God and it points to Him without naming anything beside Him. In Surah al-Ikhlas it is the word after "Say". <!-- src: L09 ¶64-66 -->

[verse]

¶15. Like the words of the first two degrees, Huwa is already on a believer's tongue. Sheikh Bahmanpour reports a hadith in which Imam ʿAli (as) is given a whole prayer built on it. Before the battle of Badr, he dreamt of Khidr, whom Musa followed in Surah al-Kahf. He asked Khidr to teach him a prayer he could repeat. Khidr taught him a short prayer that calls on God as Huwa, He, besides whom there is none. The histories report that on the day of Badr Imam ʿAli (as) was seen attacking from every side as he recited Surah al-Ikhlas and the words of this prayer. <!-- src: L09 ¶65 -->

¶16. The third degree closes the climb and the prayer this book opened with asks for something close to it. In the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah we ask God to light up the eyes of our hearts until they pierce the veils of light and reach the source of His grandeur. The sentence, which the Imams (as) placed on our tongues, does not stop at the veils.

[quotation]

¶17. Past the veils, the prayer asks that our souls be held by the glory of God's holiness. Each degree on the way there turns words the believer already says into what the traveller sees: in action, no power except through God; in attributes, no god but God; and in essence, He.

## Section 4: Coming back, sober

**Point:** Passing away is a beginning, not an end: the healthy outcome is to come back to oneself, sober, seeing God and creation together.

¶18. Passing away is the summit of the road, but not its end. Of the traveller who reaches it, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, "That's the beginning, that's not the end." Most mystics may never reach this station. Those who do still face dangers, because nearness to God is not safety. Satan once stood near to God, in a place where pride had no room and there he grew proud. He fell, defeated by nothing but his own self. <!-- src: L09 ¶20, ¶23 -->

¶19. Not everyone who passes away comes back well. The lectures describe three ways of going wrong. Let's take them as principles rather than as the stories of particular people.

¶20. In the first, the traveller stays lost. Drawn to the divine light, he is held for ever by the beauty of God and does not come back to himself. Such mystics are a small minority. Souls differ in how they meet so great an encounter, as two people caught in the same accident may come out of it in different states. <!-- src: L09 ¶30-31 -->

¶21. In the second, the traveller is held only for a while and in that time he loses control of his tongue. He utters ecstatic sayings (shath), words in which he confuses himself with God. While he is out of his mind, he is not held to religious duties, like anyone who has lost his reason. Some mystics go further and say that duties are lifted for ever from one who reaches this station, even once he is in his right mind. The lectures hold this to be unacceptable, above all in the Shia understanding of mysticism and in most of Sunni mysticism as well. <!-- src: L09 ¶38-42 -->

¶22. In the third and the most dangerous, the traveller can no longer tell himself from his Lord or his Lord from His creation. He falls into licence and says that "whatever sin is done is done by God". ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi warns of the same dangers, the "claim of incarnation and identification with God, denial of being obligated to abide by religious injunctions and regarding everything lawful", though he places them earlier on the road, at the stages of remembrance. <!-- src: L09 ¶43 -->

¶23. Most who fall into the worst of these dangers, licence, got there by rushing. They pushed themselves to this station in haste through the wrong kind of practice. Their souls then grew out of proportion, like a body whose head grows huge while the rest stays small. A traveller who practises in a balanced way is raised to the station gradually. The law, a guide and a measured pace keep him on that way. Chapters Six, Seven, Nine and Ten set them out. The tradition remembers Bayazid Bistami for his ecstatic sayings and Junayd of Baghdad for the sober way; Chapter Fourteen returns to both. <!-- src: L09 ¶44, ¶47-48; L07 ¶64 -->

¶24. A traveller who has walked at the right pace comes back well. The mystics liken passing away to intoxication (sukr). The healthy return they call sobriety after obliteration (sahw baʿd al-mahw) or separation after unification (farq baʿd al-jamʿ). The traveller who thought himself one with God in passing away gradually comes to tell himself apart from God and God apart from His creation. Imam Khomeini describes the return as God's grace, which "comes to his aid, bringing him to himself and returning him to the domain of his own self, whereat he finds himself in the state of awakening (sahw)". <!-- src: L09 ¶45, ¶50 -->

¶25. This sobriety is not a step down. Murtada Mutahhari writes that the return to remaining (baqaʾ) is not "a retrogression from a higher state" and calls sobriety "loftier even than mahw and mahq" (the effacement and obliteration of passing away). The Imams (as) lived in it. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's lectures say that they reached "the station of subsistence after annihilation", a state "which combines the two realms of Unity and plurality" and that they never held back from "observance of the most minor of the laws".

¶26. In this sobriety neither God nor creation veils the other. For us, creation veils God from our sight and we see the world without seeing Him. In passing away, God's glory veils creation and the traveller sees only Him. In the return, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "neither of them is veiled by the other". Sayyid Haydar Amuli quotes a gnostic on the same balance. Seeing only unity leads to heresy, whereas seeing only separation denies that God is the Absolute Actor. Both are ways of seeing and not a union of God and creature. The one who holds the two together, the gnostic says, has true affirmation. <!-- src: L09 ¶46 -->

¶27. Passing away is therefore a beginning and its healthy end is the return. Love carries the traveller to the loss of the false self before God's glory and grace brings him back sober, seeing God in all things while remaining himself. For us the many things of creation are a veil that hides God. Imam Khomeini writes that for those who have come back they are a mirror of His epiphany and he shows it with a saying of Imam ʿAli (as).

[quotation]

¶28. Others have walked this far. Where did the road come from and who walked it first?
