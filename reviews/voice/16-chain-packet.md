¶1. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah asks to be made one of those whom God calls and who answer Him and the line above describes them. God speaks to them in secret and they work for Him in the open. Imam ʿAli (as) once described the same people to Kumayl ibn Ziyad, whose duʿa many of us hear on Thursday evenings. Kumayl recalls that Imam ʿAli "took my hand and led me toward the cemetery. When he reached the desert, he sighed deeply". There, speaking of the few on earth who uphold God's proof, he said:

[quotation]

¶2. Imam ʿAli's description holds both halves of the Munajat's line. These people live in their bodies in this world, among everyone else, while their spirits are linked to the highest abode and their work on earth is to call people to God's religion. When Murtada Mutahhari introduces the four journeys of the mystics, he sets beside them this same saying about the friends of God in every age.

¶3. Chapter Twelve left the traveller coming back to himself, sober and called annihilation a beginning. This chapter follows him the rest of the way, which ends with him back among people and still with God. It ended this way for the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as) and for Imam al-Husayn (as) it ran as far as Karbala.

## Section 2: A quarter of the way

**Point:** Losing the self ends only the first of four journeys; in the second, God takes the traveller through His names and gives him back to himself as His servant.

¶4. Annihilation can look like the end of the road, yet the mystics count further. The journey to God, they say, is completed in "the four divine journeys" (al-asfar al-arbaʿa). The first runs from creation to God, whom they call the Real (al-Haqq) because He is the only reality. The second is in God and by God, the third goes from God back to creation by God and the fourth is in creation by God. Murtada Mutahhari gives the same four in the same order. Two of the journeys go to God and into Him and two come back.

[box] | Journey | What happens | In this book | |:---|:---|:---| | 1. From creation to God | The dark veils and the outer veils of light are removed, and the self rises to the spirit. It ends in annihilation. | Chapters Eight to Twelve | | 2. In God, by God | God shows the traveller His names. Annihilation gives way to remaining (baqaʾ), and he knows himself "a servant of Allah". | Chapter Twelve's sober return | | 3. From God to creation, by God | He sees every world as God's names at work, gains certainty, and can tell others. | This chapter | | 4. In creation, by God | He is shown how everything returns to God, and he lives among people to guide them. | This chapter; in full, the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as) |

[box] The order and the names follow the lectures and Murtada Mutahhari.

¶5. The first journey is the road we have followed through this book. On it the dark veils our deeds have made are removed and then the outer veils of light. As the veils go, the self that commands to evil gives way to the heart and the heart to the spirit. Of its four stages, Chapter Eight gave the first two to the traveller and the last two to God. The journey ends in annihilation and yet, in the lectures' words, "this is just one quarter of the way".

¶6. The rest of the way is travelled "by haqq", by God, which is how the lectures name the last three journeys. They explain the phrase at the start of the second, when the traveller "has nothing of himself and is under complete Wilayat of God. It is God who is actually taking them towards this tour." Up to annihilation the work was to get the self out of the way; once the self is gone, he is carried by God's guardianship (wilaya). Sheikh Bahmanpour finds this guardianship in a line many of us know from the last unit (rakʿa) of the night prayer:

[quotation]

¶7. In this line we ask God to take our affairs in hand as He has taken the affairs of others and the second journey is that guardianship lived. At the end of the first journey, in the lectures' image, there is "just God reflected into that mirror of the heart". In the second the traveller sets out to "examine and find out about that thing which is reflected in the heart". The lectures call it a harder journey than the first and a more delightful one.

¶8. Under God's guardianship the traveller is taken through His names and attributes one after another, as God "taught Adam the Names, all of them". In Mutahhari's words, the ʿarif "becomes acquainted with His Qualities and Names and himself becomes adorned with the same". Some names stay beyond the traveller because they are known only to God or to people of higher rank and only the Prophet ﷺ has "no one above him".

¶9. At the end of the second journey remaining (baqaʾ) follows annihilation and the traveller comes back from effacement to sobriety. He "feels his existence again", knows that God created him and finds himself "a servant of Allah". Chapter Twelve described this sober return and the map of the four journeys gives it its place, after God has shown the traveller His names.

¶10. Losing the self is therefore a quarter of the way. After the second journey the traveller is himself again and God's servant and he still faces God rather than the people he left behind.

## Section 3: The way back

**Point:** The road back has two more journeys: the third returns that servant to creation, seen now as God's names at work, and the fourth keeps him among people, with God, to guide them.

¶11. From that point the road turns back towards people. In the third journey the traveller comes "back from God to the world under the Wilayah of the Lord" and he is shown how anything can exist beside God at all. He is taken through the world of the names and the world of angels and spirits before he reaches the material world. Chapter Five described worlds that surround this one in depth rather than distance and the traveller now sees all three as "different manifestations of the names of Allah". What he sees gives him certainty, as a verse of Surah al-Anʿam says of Prophet Ibrahim:

[verse]

¶12. In this verse "dominions" translates malakut, which names the world of angels and spirits behind the heavens and the earth. Ibrahim was shown it so that he would be certain and the lectures give the third journey the same outcome, "reassurance about everything".

¶13. Certainty of this kind is meant to be passed on; the traveller now knows God's attributes and acts and "could inform others about it". Each time we say "In the Name of Allah, the All-beneficent, the All-merciful", we call God merciful because the Prophet ﷺ told us so and "otherwise we couldn't know". From this point Sheikh Bahmanpour stops calling him a mystic and calls him the knower. Mutahhari gives the third journey the same purpose: "In the third journey, he returns towards the creation, without becoming separated from God, in order to guide the people".

¶14. In the fourth journey, made in creation by God, he stays among the people. He is shown the reality of things and their effects in this world and the next and "the way everything returns to Allah". He can then tell people "the harms and benefits of everything and the causes of felicity or misery". For Mutahhari the fourth journey "is amongst the people while still united with God" and in it the ʿarif "seeks to guide their affairs so as to lead them towards God".

¶15. Whether a man who has seen only God can live among people again depends on where the road has left him. At the end of the first journey the difficulty is real. The drunk travellers of Chapter Fourteen stayed lost in annihilation and never went past this point. For anyone who finishes only that journey, it is "very difficult for them to mix with the people". ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi quotes a gnostic in that state who lived "among the people for 30 years". They thought he shared in everything they did, while he "did not see them and did not know anyone but Allah".

¶16. After the fourth journey the difficulty is gone. The Sheikh says that journey brings the traveller "down to the level of ordinary people", so that he can live with them and guide them. For the Prophet ﷺ, mixing with people is the easiest thing of all, because he sees everything as God's creation and connected to God. People cannot pull such a man away from God when he sees God in them. Chapter Ten described a heart so given to God that it could live in a crowded city as if in a cave and in the fourth journey that dedication is complete.

¶17. From outside, such a person looks like any of us and what passes between him and God stays hidden. The line from the Munajat at the head of this chapter holds both sides of his life: God speaks to His friends in secret and they work for Him in the open. The lectures tell a story of Imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq (as) as a lesson in hidden renunciation and it also shows the secret and the open in one life.

[box] Sufyan al-Thawri, an early mystic who died in 161/777, lived in the time of Imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq (as). In Sheikh Bahmanpour's account, Sufyan once saw the Imam at a gathering in a precious robe, the kind a king might wear. He went up to Imam al-Sadiq and asked whether such dress fitted the Imam's own teaching about religion and God. The Imam drew the robe aside and showed what he wore beneath it, a harsh garment that was "quite uncomfortable to wear". Then he said, "this is how you have to be. You shouldn't show off your zuhd." Renunciation (zuhd), on this telling, is best kept hidden. Read beside this chapter, the story also shows the Imam sitting among people as one of them, while what lay between him and God stayed out of sight.

¶18. The third journey therefore brings the traveller back to creation with certainty and the fourth keeps him among people to guide them while he stays with God.

## Section 4: Who completes it

**Point:** The whole return belongs to the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as), and the rest of us are called to follow them as far as God wills.

¶19. A road this high raises the question of who walks it to the end. The second journey has already set a limit, since only the Prophet ﷺ has no one above him. In the lectures, the third journey is open to "some very high caliber mystics", whereas the fourth belongs to the Prophet ﷺ and after him to the Imams (as).

¶20. In the Imams (as), the knowledge of the fourth journey stayed inside the law. When people asked them about anything, they knew the answer from an inner knowledge that reached even matters the shariʿa and the Book do not mention. Yet, as Sheikh Bahmanpour adds, "of course, they follow the Sharia" and they gave their answers by referring to the Book and the shariʿa. Chapter Six showed the law binding the traveller to the last stage and Chapter Twelve found the Imams holding the realms of unity and plurality together.

¶21. Whether holding God's world and ours at once belongs to them alone is a question ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi weighs in Kernel of the Kernels. The strong wayfarer, he says, is the one who, while witnessing these states, "also attends to the world of pluralities and who takes care of both the worlds". Of this rank he writes that it is "very sublime and elevated and access to it very difficult. Perhaps it is exclusive to the prophets and the awliya' and whoever God wishes". The awliyaʾ are the friends of God whom Imam ʿAli (as) described to Kumayl.

¶22. Tabatabaʾi finds both worlds held together in the Prophet ﷺ himself. Inwardly, in a saying of the Prophet that the Kernel quotes, "I have with God certain states which are beyond the capacity of any archangel". Outwardly, the Qur'an has him tell people:

¶23. The Prophet whose states with God were beyond any archangel lived among people as one of them. The rank is high, yet Tabatabaʾi keeps the road towards it open by separating the office from the station. In his words, "the office of prophethood and Imamate is something exclusive, but attainment to the station of absolute tawhid and fana' in the Essence of the One‑which is called wilayah‑is not at all exclusive". The Prophet ﷺ called his community to the place where he himself stood and that call "implies the possibility of travelling towards that purpose". Tabatabaʾi cites a verse of Surah al-Ahzab for that possibility:

[verse]

¶24. The verse offers the Prophet ﷺ as an exemplar to all who look forward to God and the Last Day and remember Him much. Following him in the return asks something of us from the start. The lectures say that at every stage of the road the traveller remains involved in society. Mystics have sometimes been drawn to retreat from this life, whereas Islam tells them not to and asks them to stay in society and travel the road at the same time.

¶25. For the Sheikh the third journey is open to mystics of high calibre and for Tabatabaʾi the rank may belong to "whoever God wishes". Both views therefore place the whole return in the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as) and both leave the way towards it open as far as God gives.

## Section 5: From ʿArafah to Karbala

**Point:** Imam al-Husayn (as) shows the return in one life: before God among his people at ʿArafah, and with them and for them at Karbala.

¶26. What the whole way looks like can be seen in a life we know and Ayatullah Khomeini points to one in a passage on the duʿas the Imams (as) left us. The prophets prayed, he writes and it was they who established justice in the world and rose against the wrongdoers. "Imam Husayn also did the same thing. Just see his Supplication of the Day of Arafah and ponder over it". This section does as he asks. What it adds about Karbala is the book's own reflection rather than part of the lectures.

¶27. The Mafatih records how Imam al-Husayn (as), whom it calls the Chief of Martyrs, began that supplication. Bishr and Bashir, the sons of Ghalib al-Asadi, were with him at ʿArafah. They recall that he left his tent "with submission and reverence" and walked slowly, "accompanied by a group of his household, sons and servants". He stopped beside Mount ʿArafat and turned his face towards the Kaʿba. There he raised his hands to his face "just like a poor man begging food". Among the requests he made that day is one about his own heart:

[quotation]

¶28. Standing in front of his household, Imam al-Husayn (as) asked to fear God as if he could see Him. His heart was turned to God while his family and servants stood around him and in that one moment he was among his people and before God as a beggar.

¶29. The Imam who prayed this among his household at ʿArafah was martyred with his companions at Karbala on the day of ʿAshura. He stayed with his people to the last. On Ayatullah Khomeini's reading he also stood for them, as the prophets did when they established justice and rose against the wrongdoers. Recited properly, Ayatullah Khomeini writes, such prayers lessen a person's attachment to himself and he "becomes more active and ever ready to render service to his fellow human beings". Of the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah he adds, "Those who recited the Munajat Sha'biniya, wielded the sword also".

¶30. The traveller who completes the journey therefore comes back to people while staying with God. Losing the self ends only the first of four journeys and the last two return the traveller to creation to guide it. The Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as) walked them to the end, which for Imam al-Husayn (as) lay at Karbala. Every year on ʿAshura, the Mafatih asks those of us who mourn him to "express sympathy to each other".

¶31. The friends of God come back to people. Where does every soul go back to?
