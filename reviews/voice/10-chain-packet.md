# Chain packet 10

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

[quotation]

¶1. These words come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, which many of us recite in the month of Shaʿban, on the threshold of Ramadan. The line asks God for a tongue whose truthfulness rises to Him. A month later, in Ramadan, we keep most of the disciplines of this chapter without naming them. We go hungry by day and wake before dawn. We speak fewer idle words and we give more of our time to the Qur'an and the remembrance of God.

¶2. ʿIrfan keeps a measure of these disciplines all year, because they are the tools of the road's second stage. The road has four stages. The first two are the traveller's own work, polishing the self and then emptying the heart (takhliya) and the last two are God's gifts to the emptied heart. This chapter sets out emptying and its five disciplines. It says where the stage begins, how each discipline works and how much of each to take.

## Section 2: Where ʿirfan begins

**Point:** ʿIrfan proper begins with emptying the heart, and five disciplines are its tools.

¶3. Polishing (tajliya), the road's first stage, is open to any believer. It begins with repentance and goes on in keeping the law strictly, the recommended acts as well as the obligatory and it stays with the traveller to the end of the road. Sheikh Bahmanpour places the beginning of ʿirfan proper at the next stage, since polishing is what ordinary people can start doing. ʿIrfan begins when the traveller, with polishing in place, moves on to emptying the heart. <!-- src: L07 ¶21-22 -->

¶4. Emptying clears the heart of whatever occupies it other than God. Keeping the practices of the law is easy by comparison. Abstaining from wine or from the company of certain people, is a decision a person can make and keep; abstaining from thinking ill of others is far harder and keeping the heart on God alone is harder still. Anyone who has tried to keep every other thought out of salah knows this. <!-- src: L07 ¶22-24 -->

¶5. Keeping the heart on God alone does not mean loving nothing else. The test is what occupies the heart, not what it loves. We may love many things that do not compete with the love of God and some of them are wholly in line with it. These loves stay. What emptying clears is whatever diverts the heart and stops us giving ourselves wholly to God. <!-- src: L07 ¶23 -->

¶6. The philosopher Ibn Sina names four steps in emptying. The first is separating the heart from what diverts it and the second is dusting off the traces that remain. The third is leaving and the fourth is rejecting. Leaving is letting go with some reluctance, whereas rejecting is no longer wanting the thing at all. <!-- src: L07 ¶22-23, ¶29 -->

¶7. The last and hardest thing to leave behind is the self, since we worry about ourselves and think we must keep some independence from God. The lectures compare leaning the self on God to a non-swimmer thrown into the sea. At first it feels like drowning. With practice, staying afloat becomes a pleasure; the soul learns that leaning on God is where it has to be. <!-- src: L07 ¶28-29 -->

¶8. Mystics of every faith name the same five disciplines for this work. They are silence, hunger, the night vigil, seclusion and the constant remembrance of God. Lantern of the Path, attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), names four of them as the way to rest. It says that wakefulness "illuminates your heart, purifies your nature and cleanses your spirit". The lectures do not place the five inside the four stages, so the link drawn here is the book's own. Each discipline is an act the law recommends and keeping it is still polishing. What makes the five the tools of emptying is their target, the heart. <!-- src: L08 ¶55, ¶58; L10 ¶6 -->

¶9. Emptying is therefore the second stage of the road and the five disciplines are its tools. Three of them shut the doors through which the world fills the heart and two work on the heart itself. The next two sections take them in that order.

## Section 3: Silence, hunger, and the night

**Point:** Silence, hunger, and the night prayer shut the doors through which the world fills the heart: the tongue, the stomach, and sleep.

¶10. The first door is the tongue and silence (samt) shuts it. The silence meant here is not a lifelong vow, since the tongue was given to us to speak. It means speaking only of what concerns us and what benefits us or others and leaving aside idle talk and other people's business. In Kernel of the Kernels, ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi cites a saying of Imam al-Rida (as) on this silence. <!-- src: L08 ¶59 -->

[quotation]

¶11. The saying ties silence to wisdom. The line of the Munajat at the head of this chapter asks for a tongue of the same kind, whose words answer to God. A fast of silence was known before Islam among the Children of Israel. Maryam (as) was told that if she saw anyone she should say, "Indeed I have vowed a fast to the All-beneficent, so I will not speak to any human today". <!-- src: L08 ¶74-75 -->

¶12. The second door is the stomach and hunger (juʿ) shuts it. The hunger meant here is not going without food for days. It means never eating to the point of heaviness. A full stomach draws the soul's whole attention to itself and then, in the Sheikh's words, "the mind goes dizzy and you cannot remember God". In the story told in Chapter Seven, the first counsels Imam al-Sadiq (as) gave ʿUnwan al-Basri were rules of this kind. He was to eat only when hungry and to eat only what is lawful. <!-- src: L08 ¶72-73, ¶77-79 -->

[box] ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's teacher, Mirza ʿAli Aqa Qadi, used to tell a story about hunger, which Kernel of the Kernels records. In the days of the earlier prophets, three friends arrived as strangers in a town. At nightfall they went their separate ways to find food, and agreed to meet at a set place the next day. One of them received an invitation, and another became someone's guest. The third had nowhere to go, and said to himself that he would go to the mosque and be God's guest. He stayed there until morning and remained hungry. When the three met and told one another what had happened, God revealed to the prophet of that time a message for the third man: "We accepted the hospitality of this dear guest. We were his host and we desired to offer him the best of foods. But on searching Our stores We did not find any better food for him than hunger".

¶13. Silence and hunger guard the heart against the chain of idle thoughts, which is harder to stop than speech or appetite. We imagine something, then imagine wanting it, then imagine ways of getting it and the chain drags us towards sin and away from worship. Silence and hunger give the strength to block it; they also open the way to watchfulness of God (muraqaba), the sense that He is present and sees us. In the hadith of the Prophet's ascension, which Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi records, God says that "the result of silence is wisdom; the result of wisdom is enlightenment; the result of enlightenment is certainty". <!-- src: L08 ¶46, ¶50-51, ¶75 -->

¶14. The third door is sleep and the night prayer (salat al-layl) shuts it. The Qur'an says little about silence or hunger, but it speaks often of the night vigil. Surah al-Muzzammil opens by telling the Prophet ﷺ to rise at night. <!-- src: L10 ¶7, ¶18-19 -->

[verse]

¶15. The command is for half the night, a little less or a little more. Two verses later the surah gives the reason.

[verse]

¶16. The day is full of drawn-out engagements, so the pure remembrance of God becomes possible at night. After sleep the body is rested and the memory is fresh. The attention is gathered rather than scattered and the stomach is empty. Rising in the middle of the night also shows how seriously a person holds their faith and a command the Prophet ﷺ was given is an example for the rest of us. Surah al-Dhariyat counts the same habit among the marks of the righteous. <!-- src: L10 ¶9, ¶19-20 -->

[verse]

¶17. The righteous of this verse chose to sleep little and their choice works as silence and hunger do. Each of the three loosens the body's hold on the soul by choice, as sleep loosens it by itself every night. A held tongue, a light stomach and an hour awake before dawn leave less of the soul's attention taken up by the body.

¶18. The tongue, the stomach and sleep are therefore the doors these three disciplines shut. What the world has already left inside the heart is the work of the other two.

## Section 4: Seclusion and remembrance

**Point:** Seclusion separates the heart from what distracts it, and remembrance dusts off what remains.

¶19. The last two disciplines work on the heart itself and seclusion (khalwa or ʿuzla) comes first. The Sufi orders differ over it. Some ask beginners for forty days of retreat, whereas others do not advise it at all. In Shia ʿirfan, as Sheikh Bahmanpour describes it, seclusion is taken from the Qur'an and it is "seclusion of the heart rather than seclusion of the body". Some seclusion of the body may help, as in the retreat in the mosque (iʿtikaf) that the Prophet ﷺ kept in the last ten days of Ramadan. Seclusion of the heart is Ibn Sina's first step, separating the heart from what diverts it. <!-- src: L10 ¶41-43 -->

¶20. Surah al-Muzzammil, which asks for the night prayer, also asks for this seclusion.

[verse]

¶21. The verse asks for total dedication, yet the Prophet did not cut himself off from people after it came down. His whole life was lived among them, with his family, his companions and his battles. What he turned wholly to God was his heart. A heart turned this way, in the lectures' image, can live in a big industrial city "just as if you're living in a cave". A person in a real cave whose thoughts are always elsewhere has no seclusion at all. <!-- src: L10 ¶49-51 -->

[box] The verse joins a verb to its own noun, tabattal ilayhi tabtila, which the translation used here renders "dedicate yourself to Him with total dedication". The lectures gloss tabattul as cutting oneself off from everyone and everything towards the Lord. The verse names only the One the heart turns to, and not what it leaves. Its first hearer went on living among people, so the cutting off is in the heart. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah makes the same request in a line Chapter Three quoted: "My Lord, grant me complete severance of my relations with everything else". The lectures call this one of the paradoxes of ʿirfan, in which the traveller is cut off and not cut off, social and not social. <!-- src: L10 ¶50, ¶53-54 -->

¶22. The night prayer of the last section is the traveller's daily seclusion. In salat al-layl a believer is completely alone with God, every night, for half an hour or more. A believer who keeps it up has a seclusion that never ends, renewed each night in the middle of an ordinary life. <!-- src: L10 ¶44 -->

¶23. Separating leaves traces behind, as roots can remain when a diseased growth is cut out. The remembrance of God (dhikr) dusts off those traces and this is Ibn Sina's second step. For the mystic its effect is real and present, not only a reward stored up for the next world. Ten repetitions of subhan Allah, al-hamdu lillah, la ilaha illa Allah and Allahu akbar each morning and evening dust off what remains of the heart's desire for this world. The soul takes the shape of whatever it is given, as water takes the shape of its vessel. The Qur'an asks that this remembrance be frequent. <!-- src: L07 ¶24-27 -->

[verse]

¶24. The Mafatih records, according to validly reported traditions, that this "frequent remembrance" is the Tasbih of al-Zahraʾ. This is the glorification named after Lady Fatima (as) and recited after each obligatory prayer and "whoever abides by it will have remembered Almighty Allah frequently". The lectures call it the minimum, provided it is said with attention and not out of habit. Beyond that minimum, remembrance has no limit. <!-- src: L10 ¶54-55 -->

¶25. Remembrance is also answered. In Surah al-Baqarah God promises a return for it.

[verse]

¶26. God forgets nothing, so His remembering cannot mean recalling what He had forgotten. The lectures read it as His favour coming down on the one who remembers Him. That favour is present everywhere and at every moment and constant remembrance is how a person comes to receive it. <!-- src: L10 ¶60-63 -->

¶27. The words of this remembrance are not made up. In the Sufi orders the shaykh of the order assigns a remembrance to the disciple, whereas in the Shia tradition the formulas are already in the hadith, gathered in books such as the Mafatih. A teacher only chooses which of them suit the traveller at each stage and which must not be missed. <!-- src: L10 ¶58-60 -->

¶28. Seclusion therefore separates the heart from what distracts it and remembrance dusts off what remains. With silence, hunger and the night prayer, these are the five tools of emptying.

## Section 5: A medicine, measured to the patient

**Point:** Each discipline is taken in a dose measured to the person, small and kept up.

¶29. None of the five is kept to the full. Lifelong silence is asked of no one, since the Prophet ﷺ and Imam ʿAli (as) spoke a great deal and their words fill the books of hadith. Fasting is commanded, but fasting two days on end without breaking the fast is forbidden. Seclusion and constant remembrance have to fit lives with families and duties. Even the night prayer was eased within the Qur'an. The last verse of Surah al-Muzzammil recalls that the Prophet ﷺ stood for two thirds of the night, a half or a third. Then it eases the command. <!-- src: L08 ¶55-57 -->

[verse]

¶30. The verse grants leniency because God knows what His servants can keep up. Of each discipline, Sheikh Bahmanpour asks the same question, "where is the balance". The answer is set by what the person can carry and not by imitating the Prophet ﷺ, whose practice called for a personality like his. <!-- src: L08 ¶58; L10 ¶24-25 -->

¶31. To begin the night prayer, half an hour is enough. If eagerness comes, it can grow gradually to an hour or an hour and a half and probably no more. Going past the limit has the opposite effect, until "we just stop and we just quit". The teachers of this road asked a student to keep one practice for at least a year, such as reciting Surah al-Muzzammil each night, which takes about three minutes. The body shows why the dose must grow slowly. Fifteen minutes in prostration tires our limbs, because we are not yet used to it. <!-- src: L10 ¶23-27; L15 ¶42 -->

¶32. The written guides of the road give the same measures. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's programme in Kernel of the Kernels keeps hunger "to the extent that it does not lead to weakness". It asks for wakefulness at dawn "to the extent permitted by the wayfarer's constitution". In the same programme, as Light Within Me gives it, the words of remembrance a teacher sets are weighed one person at a time: "The liturgies are just like a medicine which may suit some and may not suit others".

¶33. Lantern of the Path gives the measure in a single line. The best form of worship, it says, is the one "most free of harm and the most consistent, be it ever so small".

¶34. On seclusion the sources weigh the dose differently. Sheikh Bahmanpour prefers the nightly hour to forty days of retreat. Long retreats and hard practices can stir unusual powers in the soul and the lectures call that possible but "not desirable". Tabatabaʾi's programme calls a special seclusion from all people "essential by the masters (mashayikh) of the Way" for the spoken remembrances. Lantern of the Path says, "There is no prophet nor regent (wasi) who has not chosen retreat in his lifetime". The sources differ on the longer retreat and all of them keep the hour alone with God before dawn. <!-- src: L10 ¶44-47 -->

¶35. Emptying therefore clears the heart of everything but God. Silence, hunger and the night prayer shut the doors of the tongue, the stomach and sleep. Seclusion separates the heart from what distracts it and remembrance dusts off what remains. Each of the five is taken in a dose measured to the person, small at first and kept up for a year or more.

¶36. Emptying ends at the self, the last thing left behind. What follows is no longer the traveller's work. What does God give the emptied heart?
