# Chain packet 02y

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

¶1. On a day of fasting in Ramadan, by late afternoon, no one has to be told that they are hungry. They do not read it in a book or work it out from the clock. The hunger is known from the inside. Sheikh Bahmanpour uses hunger to describe a kind of knowing that study cannot give.

¶2. Most of what we know of God is of the kind gained by study. We learn the rulings of fiqh, the beliefs the scholars set out and the history of the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as); all of it is knowledge about God. The Qur'an's invitation to more than the minimum is an invitation to know and love God. That knowing is like the fasting person's knowledge of hunger. It is knowing God directly, not only knowing about Him. This chapter sets out why knowing about God is not knowing Him and how He comes to be known.

## Section 2: Knowing about, and knowing

**Point:** The ʿarif knows God by presence, not by definition.

¶3. Let's start with the two kinds of knowing. The first is acquired knowledge (ʿilm al-husuli). It reaches us from outside, through the senses or by reasoning. We form concepts of the things around us and then analyse those concepts. Almost everything we learn from teachers and books is of this kind.

¶4. Acquired knowledge works by defining its object. To understand a thing, we sort it into a category and mark where it ends and something else begins. The Arabic word for a definition (hadd) also means a limit: to define a thing is to limit it. The old definition of a human being as "the rational animal" works by drawing a line round us.

¶5. The second kind is knowledge by presence (ʿilm al-huduri). It does not come from outside and needs no concept to carry it; it is with the soul already. The plainest case is our knowledge of our own self. Hunger is another and so are pain and anxiety. Anxiety has a cause outside us, yet the feeling itself arises inside the soul.

¶6. Knowledge by presence is complete. When the body is hungry, the soul knows it at once and without effort and nothing of the hunger is missed. The hunger may even be false and yet what is felt is real. A person may be wrong to think that their body needs food. They cannot be wrong that they feel hungry.

¶7. Knowledge by presence is what the mystics are after. Sheikh Bahmanpour gives a definition of mysticism: the urge to reach out to the infinite through knowledge by presence. The two kinds of knowing aim at different things: acquired knowledge (ʿilm) aims at understanding and knowledge by presence (maʿrifah) aims at arriving. A person does not try to understand their hunger; they arrive at it and feel it. In the same way, the mystic's aim is to arrive at God, not to define Him. A definition draws a limit and God has none.

¶8. Imam Khomeini records a view that draws the same line between two kinds of people. On this view, a gnostic of God (an ʿarif) "knows God through direct witnessing", while a scholar of God "attains the knowledge of God through philosophical proofs". The gnostic's witnessing is knowledge by presence and the scholar's proofs are acquired knowledge.

[box] Husul means acquiring or obtaining, whereas hudur means presence. In acquired knowledge (ʿilm al-husuli), what reaches the mind is a concept or an image of the thing, while the thing itself stays outside. In knowledge by presence (ʿilm al-huduri), the thing known is itself present to the soul, as our own self, our hunger, and our pain are present. The phrase Imam Khomeini uses for the gnostic's witnessing, mushahada huduriyya (witnessing by presence), comes from the same root. Sheikh Bahmanpour notes that the religious texts use ʿilm and maʿrifah for the same thing, whereas Imam Khomeini writes of "an evident difference" between ʿirfan and ʿilm. This book keeps the two apart to make the two kinds of knowing easier to see. Chapter One's Word Study noted that there is no way to God except through knowledge, and left open what type of knowledge it refers to. If maʿrifah is arriving, as this chapter describes it, that knowledge is knowledge by presence.

## Section 3: Where reason stops

**Point:** Reason cannot reach God Himself, so He must be known within the soul.

¶9. Acquired knowledge is the work of reason (ʿaql), the faculty by which we understand and argue. Reason has a limit. When we know a thing, we know its attributes: its colour, its size and what it does. Every description says that it is brown or square or useful and none says what the "it" is. Ibn Sina, the master of philosophy, granted this. The reality of things, he held, is beyond the reach of human reason.

¶10. If reason cannot reach what the things around us are, it falls still further short of God. God has no colour or size for the mind to take hold of. Imam ʿAli (as) speaks to this at the opening of the first oration in Nahj al-Balagha, where he praises God.

[quotation]

¶11. The two images can be read as reason at its widest range and reason at its deepest and Imam ʿAli says that neither reaches God. The next sentence of the oration continues: God "cannot be described, for he is beyond the limits of demarcated boundaries". A definition draws a line round its object and there is no line to draw round God.

¶12. Reason still has work it can do. It can argue that God exists and it can say what He is not: God cannot be an object and He is not in one place and absent from another. All of this is knowledge about God. Apart from the proof that He exists, it is negative knowledge: each statement says what God is not and none says what He is.

¶13. ʿIrfan asks for the positive side. We cannot know God's essence; what ʿirfan seeks is His attributes, known positively: God as the Merciful, the Compassionate and the Forgiving. The names can be learned from a book. To know God as Merciful from within, as a hungry person knows their hunger, is knowledge by presence. Mulla Sadra held that knowledge by presence is the only way to know the reality of anything and the only way to know God. The comparison with hunger shows how this knowledge is had, not how much is known. It has degrees and in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "the more you know, the more bewildered you will become".

¶14. We have knowledge by presence only of what is within us: our own self and some of its states. A sense of God already lives there, however faint: a vague idea of God that we carry inside us. If God is to be known and not only known about, the knowing must happen within the soul.

[box] Mulla Sadra died in 1050/1640. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's account, Islamic philosophy before him had two branches: the Peripatetic (al-hikma al-mashshaʾiyya) and the Illuminationist. Mulla Sadra changed its course. He called his own philosophy neither of these but the transcendent philosophy (al-hikma al-mutaʿaliya). He claimed for it a rationalised mysticism: he gave rational argument to what the mystics know only by experience. Murtada Mutahhari sets out the difference between the two paths. The philosopher works with "reason, deductions and proofs", whereas the ʿarif works with "the heart and the purification, disciplining and perfecting of the self". Mulla Sadra studied the ʿirfan of Ibn ʿArabi deeply without belonging to any Sufi order, and rated Ibn ʿArabi above Ibn Sina. Kernel of the Kernels records that after a lifetime in philosophy he became absorbed in worship and the purification of his inner being. He asked God's pardon for the part of his life spent studying "the opinions of the pseudo-philosophers and polemicists from among the theologians", until "it became clear to me that their syllogisms were barren and their path devoid of straightness".

## Section 4: Light in the heart

**Point:** Knowledge of God is a light God gives to a purified heart, not something gathered by study.

¶15. A knowledge that analysis cannot produce must be given. A hadith that Imam Khomeini quotes in his Forty Hadith says who gives it.

[quotation]

¶16. The hadith sets the two kinds of knowing side by side. Extensive learning is acquired knowledge, gathered from outside, whereas the light is not gathered at all: God places it in the heart.

¶17. The words "whomever He wills" can be read as saying that God chooses some hearts for His light and passes over others. Sheikh Bahmanpour speaks of the light through a verse of Surah al-Nur.

[verse]

¶18. Allah's light is everywhere, in the heavens and in the earth and He is with us wherever we are. Yet the same verse goes on, "Allah guides to His Light whomever He wishes" and God does choose: He chooses those He loves. Both are true. The lighting of any heart is God's act and His will is not to withhold: nothing is held back on His side. What keeps a heart dark is that it is closed and the traveller's work is to open it in the hope of being chosen.

¶19. Opening the heart is purifying it and Imam Khomeini explains what follows once that is done. When the soul is purified, he writes, divine teaching follows, "for there is no niggardliness in the higher realms, which are bounteous by necessity". The teaching remains God's gift and the purified heart waits for it. Imam Khomeini grounds this in a verse of Surah al-Baqarah that links God's teaching to wariness of Him.

[verse]

¶20. In the verse, God's teaching follows on from wariness of Him. In the same way, the light comes to the hearts of the pure and darkness covers the impure.

¶21. A knowledge given to the heart also travels differently. The knowledge of books passes from mind to mouth, through language, whereas the knowledge of the ʿurafaʾ passes "from heart to heart", in the Sheikh's words. Receiving it directly from God is possible but difficult and one usually has to start through someone else. Chapter Seven describes the guide.

## Section 5: The path is purification

**Point:** The path to knowing God is purification, which study serves but cannot replace.

¶22. If the light is everywhere and the heart is closed, the path to knowing God is the opening of the heart. Two Arabic words mark the difference between this path and study. Knowledge learned at a university or a seminary comes through thought (fikr): we think, put things together, analyse and find out. Knowledge of God comes through wayfaring (suluk), the path to purity.

¶23. Wayfaring has two parts and the Sheikh names both: "You purify yourself and then wait for Allah to put the light in your heart." The light is not absent while the heart waits. The waiting is the patience of a heart that has done its part and leaves the rest to God. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah ties the light to the path in one line.

[quotation]

¶24. The same prayer asks, in the line this book opened with, for the eyes of the heart to be lit until they reach the source of God's grandeur. This line says who receives that light: the one who follows God's path.

¶25. Following that path begins with the shariʿa. Its first step is to polish the soul by doing whatever the Prophet ﷺ has asked. Without this there is no maʿrifah. The Qur'an calls the believers to answer Allah and the Apostle when he summons them to what will give them life. That life comes when a person follows the Prophet completely.

¶26. Practice depends on knowing what to practise and this is where study comes in. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, in the lectures recorded in Kernel of the Kernels, gives study an early place. Once the traveller has set out with a sincere intention, the first essential, he says, is knowledge of the rules of the shariʿa, learned from a jurist. The traveller puts that knowledge into practice and persists until conviction and gnosis (maʿrifah) grow by degrees, "for knowledge gives birth to action and action in turn brings knowledge".

¶27. Imam Khomeini gives learning its place, with a warning. A learning that does not bring faith with it, he writes, "is the greatest of veils"; yet "one has to approach a veil in order to tear it into shreds". He adds that the sciences are "seeds of (spiritual) experience". Study prepares the ground, but without the polishing of the soul there is no maʿrifah, so study serves purification and cannot replace it.

[box] Murtada Mutahhari records a meeting between Ibn Sina and Abu Saʿid ibn Abi al-Khayr, one of the most famous of all the ʿurafaʾ. Abu Saʿid was preaching on the necessity of deeds, and on obedience and disobedience to God. Ibn Sina answered him with a quatrain (a poem of four lines). Wherever God's favour and grace are found, it ran, "Let the not-done be like the done, the done like the not-done." The verse leaned on God's forgiveness in place of deeds. Abu Saʿid replied at once with a quatrain of his own, which ends: "Do not rely on forgiveness, for never / Was the not-done like the done, the done like the not-done". In Sheikh Bahmanpour's account, Ibn Sina later wrote to Abu Saʿid asking to learn from him. Mutahhari, who calls Ibn Sina "mainly a philosopher, not a mystic", notes that "especially towards the end of his life he developed mystic inclinations".

## Section 6: Knowing by drawing near

**Point:** Knowing God is a different kind of knowing, reached by purification, which study serves but cannot replace.

¶28. What study cannot give comes to the ʿurafaʾ through purification. They are brought near to God and know Him by presence: a veil is removed, rather than a definition learned. Their knowing is present to the soul, as hunger is present to the one who fasts. It comes as a light that God gives to a purified heart and so it differs in kind from knowing about God.

¶29. Where, then, does an ordinary believer already stand on this path?
