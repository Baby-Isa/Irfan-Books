# Chain packet 08b

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

[quotation]

¶1. Many of us save for years to travel to Makka, Najaf or Karbala. That journey is measured in miles. The line above, from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah (a prayer of the month of Shaʿban), speaks of another journey. It calls God the Near One, who is not far even from the one who is away from Him.

¶2. If God is near even to the one who is away from Him, something on our side must make a person "away". Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali, the supplication Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as) taught for the nights of Ramadan, names it. It first says that whoever travels towards God has only a short way to go. Then it says what stands in the way.

[quotation]

¶3. God conceals Himself from no one. What keeps a person away is their own wrong deeds, which stand between them and Him like a veil. The journey to God removes what stands between and it has an order of its own. This chapter sets out the stages of the journey: why it covers no distance, what its four stages are and why they come in order.

## Section 2: Nearer than the jugular vein

**Point:** God is already near, so the journey covers no distance; it removes veils inside us.

¶4. The journey covers no distance because God is already near. Surah Qaf says this of every human being. God created him and knows what his soul whispers to him and the verse ends with these words. <!-- src: L08 ¶8-9 -->

[verse]

¶5. Few things are closer to a person than the vein in their own neck and God is nearer than that. A traveller towards Him has no distance to cross. <!-- src: L08 ¶9 -->

¶6. With no distance to cross, the journey runs inward. The mystics call it wayfaring (suluk) and the one who travels is the wayfarer (salik). The Arabic word for wayfaring also means a person's conduct, the way they carry themselves through life. Sheikh Bahmanpour describes the journey simply. "We are not traveling from one place to another place. We are actually finding ourselves." Its purpose is to see what is already around us: the presence of God and our own selves. <!-- src: L08 ¶3-4 -->

¶7. The inward journey removes a veil. In the lectures, the journey means lifting the veil that blocks the heart's vision and keeps the light of God from shining in it. The many methods the mystics have devised serve this one aim. It is the journey we were created for, since human beings were made to see the glory and beauty of Allah and it is the veil over the heart that stops them. <!-- src: L08 ¶9; L07 ¶4 -->

¶8. The veils are not all of one kind. The mystics speak of dark veils and veils of light and the stages of the journey deal with them differently. <!-- src: L08 ¶10 -->

¶9. Dark veils are the ones we make ourselves. Our own actions create them and they belong to the lower self (nafs). This is what Duʿa Abu Hamza means when it says that God conceals Himself from no one unless their wrong deeds stand between them and Him. Each wrong act lays a dark layer over the heart and a heart under such layers cannot see Allah. <!-- src: L09 ¶72-73 -->

¶10. Veils of light are finer and we do not make them. They belong to the higher levels of the soul, the heart (qalb) and the spirit (ruh). They come from our existence as creatures, not from anything we do. The outer veils of light lift at the end of the journey this chapter describes. Some never lift, even between the Prophet ﷺ and God, because without them creation would perish before the glory of Allah. <!-- src: L09 ¶72-74; L07 ¶59 -->

¶11. Whatever the kind of veil, the hindrance lies on our side, not God's. In the lectures' words, "There is no hindrance from the other side, the hindrance is inside us; we have to remove it." The journey therefore covers no distance. It removes the veils inside us that can be lifted, first the dark ones our deeds have made and then the outer veils of light. <!-- src: L10 ¶21; L09 ¶72-74; L07 ¶59 -->

## Section 3: Four stages: two ours, two God's

**Point:** The veils come off in four stages: we polish the self and empty the heart, then God adorns it and the self passes away.

¶12. The veils come off in stages and the mystics count four of them to the end of the soul's first journey towards God. In English they are polishing, emptying, adorning and passing away. Each of the next four chapters takes one stage in full, so the aim here is only to see the whole road in order. <!-- src: L07 ¶11-12 -->

¶13. The first stage is polishing (tajliya), from a word for burnishing something until it shines. What is polished is the body and the lower self. The stage opens when a person wakes from heedlessness and repents, not only of sins but of taking everything for granted. It continues in doing whatever the Prophet ﷺ asked. The obligatory and recommended acts are done, the forbidden and the disliked are avoided and the Qur'an and the Sunna are followed "in the strictest sense". Polishing begins the work on the dark veils our deeds have made. It is the subject of Chapter Nine. <!-- src: L07 ¶12-13; L08 ¶10; L09 ¶72-74 -->

¶14. The second stage is emptying (takhliya). Here the work moves from the outward acts to the heart, which must be cleared of every "occupation other than Allah". Outward practice is the easier part, whereas what occupies the heart is harder to remove. A person can give up wine without much difficulty, but giving up evil thoughts about other people is another matter. The mystics teach five disciplines: silence, hunger, night vigil, seclusion and remembrance. This book treats them as the tools of this stage, since each is a recommended act aimed at the heart. Chapter Ten takes them in turn. <!-- src: L07 ¶22-24; L08 ¶55, ¶58; L10 ¶6 -->

¶15. The third stage is adorning (tahliya). Once the self is polished and the heart emptied, the soul is adorned from the spiritual world with what no effort of its own could make. The mystics speak of visions, of inspirations and of a deeper understanding of the Qur'an. One of these gifts is the love of God. No one can create it in themselves; God places it in a heart that has been purified. Chapter Eleven follows love as it carries the traveller on. <!-- src: L07 ¶10, ¶36 -->

¶16. The fourth stage is passing away (fanaʾ). When adorning reaches its height, God shows the traveller a glimpse of His glory, "at a very low level" as the lectures put it. Before it the traveller loses the perception of self and with it all selfishness. The soul is still there and so is the individual. Sayyid Haydar Amuli calls the station of passing away "the last of the levels and the highest of the stations of the path". Passing away is coupled with remaining (baqaʾ) and on the right path it is followed by a sober return. Chapter Twelve explains both. <!-- src: L07 ¶52, ¶54-57; L09 ¶45 -->

¶17. The four stages fall into two pairs. The first two are the traveller's work: the law is kept and the heart is guarded against whatever occupies it. The last two are God's act on the heart the traveller has prepared. In the lectures' image, the traveller prepares the soul for the food that God gives it. Of adorning they say, "It is Allah's act on the soul" and add that it comes not directly but through the angels around us. No effort brings about the sight of His glory either. The box below sets the four stages side by side. <!-- src: L07 ¶10, ¶36-37, ¶55 -->

¶18. There are therefore four stages to the end of the first journey. Two are ours, polishing and emptying and two are God's, adorning and passing away.

## Section 4: In order, and none dropped

**Point:** Each stage rests on the one before, and none is ever dropped.

¶19. The four stages come in a fixed order, because each needs the one before. The Shia mystics hold that until the soul and body have been polished with what the law asks, "you cannot move to the next step". The gifts God gives in adorning are all around us, yet only a purified soul can catch them. The mystics see and hear what others cannot, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "not because … they are chosen ones but because they have furbished their souls". Passing away comes last. It happens when adorning reaches its height, when God shows a glimpse of His glory to a heart already adorned. <!-- src: L07 ¶13, ¶37, ¶55-56 -->

¶20. None of the stages is dropped when the next begins. The lectures say this of polishing, which "should accompany a mystic until the end". Even the Prophet ﷺ, at the highest of stations, was told to stand vigil through the night and recite the Qur'an in a measured tone. As Chapter Six showed, no station on the road releases the traveller from the law and once the truth is unveiled the law is kept more carefully, not less. This book draws a wider point from it. Each stage is added to those before it, so a traveller who has reached adorning is still polishing the self. <!-- src: L07 ¶20-21 -->

¶21. Since the first stage is the law, any believer can already start on the road. The lectures say this of polishing and they place the start of ʿirfan proper at the second stage. ʿIrfan proper means the deeper understanding the mystics seek and it begins when the work turns from the outward acts to the heart. <!-- src: L07 ¶22 -->

[box] Ibn Sina was mainly a philosopher, not a mystic, yet in his al-Isharat, which appears to be his last work, he gave a whole section to the stations of the ʿurafaʾ. His count begins with will (irada), the longing to return to God, and training (riyada), which clears the path of all but God. When these reach a certain degree, flashes of God's light descend on the traveller, "momentary like flashes of lightning appearing and instantly vanishing". Sheikh Bahmanpour, reading the passage with his students, adds that the light comes at first from behind the clouds, because the heart could not yet bear it uncovered. With more training the flashes become "a prolonged blaze" and a lasting state. At arrival the traveller "becomes oblivious to his own self and views only the Holy". Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, commenting on the Isharat, counts nine stages in three groups of three, for the beginning, the way, and arrival. The numbers differ, but the order is close to this chapter's. Will and training come first, the flashes come once training reaches a certain degree, and arrival comes last. <!-- src: L15 ¶4, ¶9-11, ¶20-23 -->

¶22. The journey to God therefore covers no distance. It removes veils inside us in four stages. Polishing and emptying are ours; adorning and passing away are God's. Each stage rests on the one before and none is dropped when the next begins. Together they complete only the first of four journeys the mystics describe, from creation to God; the return that follows belongs to Chapters Twelve and Sixteen. <!-- src: L09 ¶70-75 -->

¶23. The first stage asks for the law before anything else, so a believer need not wait for a teacher to begin, though a teacher helps to say which of the recommended acts matter most. Its first step is one any believer can take. What is it? <!-- src: L07 ¶14-15 -->
