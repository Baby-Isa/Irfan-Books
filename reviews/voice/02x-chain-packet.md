# Chain packet 02x

## Section 1: Opening

**Point:** Hook the reader and say, in the words of the chapter title, what the chapter will establish; recall earlier ground in one or two sentences at most.

¶1. On a day of fasting in Ramadan, by late afternoon, no one has to be told that they are hungry. They do not read it in a book or work it out from the clock. The hunger is known from the inside. Sheikh Bahmanpour uses hunger to describe a kind of knowing that study cannot give.

¶2. Most of what we know of God is of the kind gained by study. We learn the rulings of fiqh, the beliefs the scholars set out and the history of the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as); all of it is knowledge about God. The Qur'an's invitation to more than the minimum is an invitation to know and love God. That knowing is like the fasting person's knowledge of hunger. It is knowing God directly, not only knowing about Him. This chapter sets out why knowing about God is not knowing Him and how He comes to be known.

## Section 2: Knowing about, and knowing

**Point:** The ʿarif knows God by presence, not by definition.

¶3. Let's start with the two kinds of knowing. The first is acquired knowledge (ʿilm al-husuli). It reaches us from outside, through the senses or by reasoning. We form concepts of the things around us and then analyse those concepts. Almost everything we learn from teachers and books is of this kind.

¶4. Acquired knowledge works by defining its object. To understand a thing, we sort it into a category and mark where it ends and something else begins. The Arabic word for a definition (hadd) also means a limit: to define a thing is to limit it. The old definition of a human being as "the rational animal" works by drawing a line round us.

¶5. The second kind is knowledge by presence (ʿilm al-huduri). It does not come from outside and needs no concept to carry it; it is with the soul already. The plainest case is our knowledge of our own self. Hunger is another and so are pain and anxiety. Anxiety has a cause outside us, yet the feeling itself arises inside the soul.

¶6. Knowledge by presence is complete and sure in a way that acquired knowledge is not. When the body is hungry, the soul knows it at once and without effort and nothing of the hunger is missed. The hunger may even be false and yet what is felt is real. A person who has eaten enough can still feel hungry. They may be wrong to think that their body needs food. They cannot be wrong that they feel hungry.

¶7. Knowledge by presence is what the mystics are after. Sheikh Bahmanpour defines mysticism as the urge to reach out to the infinite through knowledge by presence. The two kinds of knowing aim at different things: acquired knowledge (ʿilm) aims at understanding and knowledge by presence (maʿrifah) aims at arriving. A person does not try to understand their hunger; they arrive at it and feel it. In the same way, the mystic's aim is to arrive at God, not to define Him. A definition draws a limit and God has none.

¶8. Imam Khomeini draws the same line between two kinds of people. He cites the view that a gnostic of God (an ʿarif) "knows God through direct witnessing", while a scholar of God "attains the knowledge of God through philosophical proofs". The gnostic's witnessing is knowledge by presence and the scholar's proofs are acquired knowledge.

[box] Husul means acquiring or obtaining, whereas hudur means presence. In acquired knowledge (ʿilm al-husuli), what reaches the mind is a concept or an image of the thing, while the thing itself stays outside. In knowledge by presence (ʿilm al-huduri), the thing known is itself present to the soul, as our own self, our hunger, and our pain are present. Imam Khomeini's word for the gnostic's witnessing (huduriyya) comes from the same root. The religious texts use ʿilm and maʿrifah for the same thing; this book keeps them apart only to make the two kinds of knowing easier to see. Chapter One's Word Study noted that there is no way to God except through knowledge, and left open what type of knowledge it refers to; it assuredly refers to knowledge by presence.

## Section 3: Where reason stops

**Point:** Reason cannot reach God Himself, so He must be known within the soul.

¶9. Acquired knowledge is the work of reason (ʿaql), the faculty by which we understand and argue. Reason has a limit. When we know a thing, we know its attributes: its colour, its size and what it does. Every description says that it is brown or square or useful and none says what the "it" is. Ibn Sina, the greatest of the Muslim philosophers, granted this. The reality of things, he held, is beyond the reach of human reason.

¶10. If reason cannot reach what the things around us are, it falls still further short of God. God has no colour or size for the mind to take hold of. Imam ʿAli (as) says as much at the opening of the first oration in Nahj al-Balagha, where he praises God.

[quotation]

¶11. Imam ʿAli's two images name the two strengths of reason. Soaring thoughts are reason at its widest range; deep minds are reason at its most penetrating. Neither arrives at God. The next sentence of the oration explains why. God, it says, "cannot be described, for he is beyond the limits of demarcated boundaries". A definition draws a line round its object and there is no line to draw round God.

¶12. Reason still has work it can do. It can show that God exists and it can say what He is not: He is not a body, He is not in one place and absent from another and He has no partner. All of this is knowledge about God and all of it is negative knowledge: each statement says what God is not and none says what He is.

¶13. ʿIrfan asks for the positive side: knowing God as the Merciful, the Compassionate and the Forgiving. The names can be learned from a book. To know God as Merciful from within, as a hungry person knows their hunger, is knowledge by presence. We have knowledge by presence only of what is within us: our own self and some of its states. A sense of God already lives there, however faint; Sheikh Bahmanpour calls it a vague idea of God that we carry inside us. If God is to be known and not only known about, the knowing must happen within the soul.

[box] Mulla Sadra died in 1050/1640. Before him, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's account, Islamic philosophy had two main schools, of which the Peripatetic (al-hikma al-mashshaʾiyya) is the one that matters here. The Peripatetic school is the way of reasoned proof that Murtada Mutahhari describes: the philosopher works with "reason, deductions and proofs". It is the school of Ibn Sina. Mulla Sadra called his own philosophy neither of the two but the transcendent philosophy (al-hikma al-mutaʿaliya). He took what the mystics know only by experience and gave it rational argument, a rationalised mysticism. He held that the reality of anything, God above all, is known only through knowledge by presence. The Peripatetics reason their way to the truth, whereas Mulla Sadra held that reasoning must end in the soul's own presence to what it knows. He studied the ʿirfan of Ibn ʿArabi deeply without belonging to any Sufi order, and rated Ibn ʿArabi above Ibn Sina. Kernel of the Kernels records that after a lifetime in philosophy he became absorbed in worship and the purification of his inner being, until "it became clear to me that their syllogisms were barren and their path devoid of straightness".

## Section 4: Light in the heart

**Point:** Knowledge of God is a light God gives to a purified heart, not something gathered by study.

¶14. A knowledge that analysis cannot produce must be given. A hadith that Imam Khomeini quotes in his Forty Hadith says who gives it.

[quotation]

¶15. The hadith sets the two kinds of knowing side by side. Extensive learning is acquired knowledge, gathered from outside, whereas the light is not gathered at all: God places it in the heart.

¶16. The words "whomever He wills" seem to say that God chooses some hearts for His light and passes over others. Sheikh Bahmanpour answers with a verse of Surah al-Nur.

[verse]

¶17. Allah's light is everywhere, in the heavens and in the earth and He is with us wherever we are. The hadith and the verse are both true. The lighting of any heart is God's act, as the hadith says. Yet His will is not to withhold: nothing is held back on His side. What keeps a heart dark is that it is closed and the traveller's work is to open it to a light that was always there.

¶18. Opening the heart is purifying it and Imam Khomeini explains what follows once that is done. When the soul is purified, he writes, divine teaching follows, "for there is no niggardliness in the higher realms, which are bounteous by necessity". He grounds this in a promise of Surah al-Baqarah.

[verse]

¶19. In the verse, God's teaching follows on from wariness of Him. In the same way, the light comes to the hearts of the pure and darkness covers the impure.

¶20. Because it is a light given by God and not something gathered by study, this knowledge also travels differently: a light received in one heart can reach another. The knowledge of books passes from mind to mouth, through speech and writing, whereas the knowledge of the ʿurafaʾ passes "from heart to heart", in the Sheikh's words. Receiving it directly from God is possible but difficult and most travellers begin through someone who has already received it. Chapter Seven describes that guide.

## Section 5: The path is purification

**Point:** The path to knowing God is purification, which study serves but cannot replace.

¶21. If the light is everywhere and the heart is closed, the path to knowing God is the opening of the heart. Two Arabic words mark the difference between this path and study. Knowledge learned at a university or a seminary comes through thought (fikr): we think, put things together, analyse and find out. Knowledge of God comes through wayfaring (suluk), the path to purity.

¶22. Wayfaring has two parts and the Sheikh names both: "You purify yourself and then wait for Allah to put the light in your heart." The light is not absent while the heart waits. The waiting is the patience of a heart that has done its part and leaves the rest to God. The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah ties the light to the path in one line.

[quotation]

¶23. The same prayer asks, in the line this book opened with, for the eyes of the heart to be lit until they reach the source of God's grandeur. This line says who receives that light: the one who follows God's path.

¶24. Following that path begins with the shariʿa. Its first step is to polish the soul by doing whatever the Prophet ﷺ has asked. Without this there is no maʿrifah. The Qur'an calls the believers to answer Allah and the Apostle when he summons them to what will give them life. That life comes when a person follows the Prophet completely.

¶25. Practice depends on knowing what to practise and this is where study comes in. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi places study at the start of the path. The first essential for the traveller is knowledge of the rules of the shariʿa, learned from a jurist. The traveller puts that knowledge into practice and persists until conviction and gnosis (maʿrifah) grow by degrees, "for knowledge gives birth to action and action in turn brings knowledge". Learning starts the circle. Practice purifies the heart, the purified heart receives a deeper knowledge and that knowledge leads to better practice.

¶26. Imam Khomeini gives study its place, with a warning. A learning that does not bring faith with it, he writes, "is the greatest of veils"; yet "one has to approach a veil in order to tear it into shreds". In his view, study is a veil the traveller has to pass through, so it serves purification but cannot take its place.

[box] Murtada Mutahhari records a meeting between Ibn Sina and Abu Saʿid ibn Abi al-Khayr, one of the most famous of all the ʿurafaʾ. Abu Saʿid was preaching on the necessity of deeds, and on obedience and disobedience to God. Ibn Sina answered him with a quatrain (a poem of four lines). Wherever God's favour and grace are found, it ran, "Let the not-done be like the done, the done like the not-done." The verse leaned on God's forgiveness in place of deeds. Abu Saʿid replied at once with a quatrain of his own, which ends: "Do not rely on forgiveness, for never / Was the not-done like the done, the done like the not-done". The ʿarif would not let a verse, however well turned, take the place of deeds. Mutahhari adds that towards the end of his life Ibn Sina "developed mystic inclinations".

## Section 6: Knowing by drawing near

**Point:** Knowing God is a different kind of knowing, reached by purification.

¶27. Those brought near to God, the ʿurafaʾ, know Him by presence: a veil is removed, rather than a definition learned. Their knowing is present to the soul, as hunger is present to the one who fasts and it comes as a light that God gives to a purified heart. Knowing God therefore differs in kind from knowing about God. It is reached by purification. Study, as Imam Khomeini and ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi hold, serves that purification but cannot replace it.

¶28. Where, then, does an ordinary believer already stand on this path?
