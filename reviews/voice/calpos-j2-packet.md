# Voice packet calpos-j2

## Reference: Author A (all of this is by Author A)

[verse]

There is a verse often recited at weddings, one most of us know. In Surah al-Rum, God says that He created spouses for us from among ourselves "that you may take comfort in them" and that He ordained "affection and mercy" between husband and wife. The verse ends with the words above: this is a sign for a people who reflect. Whether a person sees it depends on something in the person doing the looking. That something and why it is worth developing, is the subject of this chapter.

Let's start with two claims. The first is that a believer who prays, fasts, pays what is due and goes on hajj can hope for jannah, provided they do what is wajib and avoid what is forbidden. This claim is not contested. Sheikh Bahmanpour accepts it as fair for ordinary believers. Mohammad Ali Shomali makes the same point, describing the obligatory rules of fiqh as "the minimally necessary conditions of human perfection".

The second claim goes further and it is the one this book disputes. Some jurists and activists hold that the obligatory acts are the whole of the religion; anything beyond them, they say, is made up by the whims and wishes of certain mystics.

To see why there is more than the obligations, let's turn to the Qur'an and read on from the verse this chapter began with. Surah al-Rum names three more sets of signs: the creation of the heavens and the earth, with the variety of our languages and colours; our sleep and our work; the lightning and the rain that revives the dead earth. Each verse ends as the first did, by saying who the sign is for.

[verse]

[verse]

[verse]

Four times in a row, the Qur'an makes the same point. The signs are all around us: in marriage, in the people we meet, in sleep, in work, in the weather. Yet they are for those who reflect, know, listen and reason. This reflecting, knowing, listening and reasoning is what the Qur'an asks of us beyond the minimum.

For the avoidance of doubt, this invitation from the Qur'an is not an invitation to circumvent the law or to abandon the prescribed wajibat. Imam Khomeini insists that a genuine seeker's devotion "is within the bounds of the Shari'ah". Within those bounds, the Qur'an is just as insistent, in these verses and others, that it invites us to more than the obligatory acts.

An invitation addressed to those who reflect, know, listen and reason implies that many people do none of these things. Sheikh Bahmanpour does not limit this to the careless. Most pious people, he says, even those careful about the shariʿa, do not find a sign in these verses. Looking at the variety of languages and colours, we are more likely to see a reason for division than a sign of God.

We have heard these verses many times and we accept that there are signs in them because the Qur'an says so; yet accepting that a sign exists is not the same as perceiving it. The Qur'an describes the gap in one verse.

[verse]

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi explains what passing by a sign means; a person who sees a red light thinks of the danger it warns of, not of the colour and shape of the lamp. In the same way, the things of the world are signs and "what the signs indicate is not this world, but the person of its Creator". Most of us see only the lamp. We witness the rain as rain and the marriage as a marriage and we pass them by without perceiving what they say about their Creator.

If the signs are present and plain and yet we still pass them by, the fault is on our side. The Qur'an says where it lies, speaking of people who have every faculty and use none of them.

[verse]

Cattle see and hear perfectly well, so the comparison cannot be about the senses. Sheikh Bahmanpour reads it as being about what lies behind the senses. Eyes and ears take in the surface of things, whereas inside a human being there should be something more: a faculty, which animals lack, that turns what the senses take in into understanding. In most of us, he says, it is not working.

He compares this faculty to a machine. The ear catches the sound, but "there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is [rusted]." That machine is the soul. It has to be repaired before it can catch the signals of God. For Sheikh Bahmanpour, this is the whole subject in one sentence: mysticism is about making the soul hear, see and reflect. He adds that this is not easy and requires a great deal of training.

Placing the fault in the receiver has two consequences. The first is that the problem is not a shortage of evidence. God is not withholding signs; we are failing to receive them.

The second is that the problem can be solved. A faculty that exists but has rusted can be repaired; one that is absent cannot be made fit for purpose. The Kernel of the Kernels, a record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's early lectures on the path, opens by saying that the pull towards the unseen is "part of the innate instincts of the human being". The longing for God and the faculty that perceives Him, are part of how we are made.

Repair is only the start. Once the inner faculty begins to work, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, the journey begins and it does not end; it is a path that "has a beginning and no end".

### Passage A

Imam Khomeini reports the test his teacher Ayatullah Shahabadi used: the measure of true spiritual effort is how much selfishness is in it. If a traveller's "spiritual exercises are meant for acquiring powers for worldly ends, his efforts are rendered invalid".

Murtada Mutahhari uses the same word as Sheikh Bahmanpour. The ʿurafaʾ, he writes, consider it "a kind of shirk (polytheism) for one's goal in life and particularly in worship to be something other than God Himself" and he recalls Imam ʿAli (as) saying that he worshipped God not from fear of His Fire or desire for His Paradise but because he found Him worthy of worship.

The scholars differ in emphasis but agree that powers are not the aim. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls such powers shirk. Mutahhari explains why seeking them would be so: the goal has become something other than God.

### Passage B

The third principle is the most important: no part of the journey towards ʿirfan requires stepping outside the shariʿa and nothing on that journey is gained by doing so. The beginning of ʿirfan, as the Sheikh puts it, is the shariʿa. The inner path does not go around the law or rise above it; it is the law lived from the inside. Nothing in this book asks a reader to set aside the rulings of the jurists or the guidance of their marjaʿ. A fear that mysticism might do exactly that is one reason some scholars have been wary of ʿirfan. Chapter Eight sets out their wider concerns, with the answers given to them.

By the end, a reader should feel able to say what ʿirfan is and is not, why it belongs to us and what the stages and dangers of the path entail. For those who wish to go further, each chapter and the epilogue offer small, practical steps for bringing this into daily life alongside the wajibat.

### Passage C

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads "those who possess knowledge" as another name for the ʿurafaʾ. Every Muslim knows that there is no god but Allah. Our knowledge of tawhid, God's oneness, is still not the knowledge of those who witness it. Tawhid is simple to say and hard to grasp; in the Sheikh's view, the intellect alone cannot reach its depths and only the awakened soul can. Surah Fatir describes the same people by what they feel.

[verse]

Most of us fear Allah's punishment. The people of this verse fear Him with a fear that is not about punishment at all.

Surah Yunus adds to their names, which Sheikh Bahmanpour treats as the same people: the awliyaʾ Allah, the friends of God. Imam ʿAli (as) describes what these friends see that others miss.

### Passage D

If the faculty can be repaired, there should be people in whom it already works. The Qur'an describes them more than once. Sheikh Bahmanpour divides people into two groups: those whose souls work and those whose souls do not. He calls the first group the ʿurafaʾ, the plural of ʿarif. He sets the prophets and the Imams (as) aside as a class of their own and looks at ordinary believers who see more than the rest. The first description of them is in Surah al-Anfal.

[verse]

A heart trembles only at the mention of someone it has some experience of; a name means little to a person who does not know whom it names. The believers of this verse are thus the believers who know Allah. A verse in Surah Al ʿImran goes further, giving them another name.

### Passage E

Imam Khomeini records the same line between the two kinds of knowing. In his commentary on a saying of Imam ʿAli (as), he reports the view that a gnostic of God "knows God through direct witnessing", while a scholar of God "attains the knowledge of God through philosophical proofs". The word Imam Khomeini uses for that witnessing, huduriyya, shares its root with ʿilm al-huduri. The gnostic's knowledge of God is knowledge by presence.

Murtada Mutahhari draws the contrast himself, with an image from the sciences. The philosopher's knowledge, he writes, is like that of a mathematician who has thought a problem through. The knowledge sought by the ʿarif "is experienced and witnessed", like the knowledge a scientist gains in his laboratory. A result in the laboratory is known because it has been observed, not because it has been deduced.

### Passage F

This is the case for pursuing ʿirfan. The Qur'an invites more than the minimum and what stands in the way is a faculty in us that can be repaired. On the other side of that repair lie nearness to God, a good life and freedom from fear and grief. That rank is open to any believer who obeys God and the Prophet and acts righteously.

Pursuing ʿirfan means training the soul and soul-training is not unique to Islam. People take up the inner life for different reasons. Some train the soul in an attempt to gain power: to be in two places at once, to see the future or the past or to master the hidden world of the soul. Sheikh Bahmanpour says this kind of mysticism must be held apart from the real thing. Islamic mysticism is about catching the signs of Allah: becoming sensitive to them and learning to hear and see.

### Passage G

This book argues that this side of the religion is worth every believer's pursuit. ʿIrfan travels through the self and never leaves the shariʿa. It is rooted in the Qur'an and the Imams. It is walked by a tested and ordered method. And it ends not in withdrawal from the world but in return: to people, with God and to God Himself.

The argument is addressed to anyone who prays, fasts and recites the duʿas and who has wondered whether there is more to these acts than getting them right; whether the religion has another dimension. No knowledge of ʿirfan, of philosophy or of Arabic is assumed. The book is meant as a welcoming introduction to ʿirfan, a guide to beginning the journey that strives for brevity and clarity. Each chapter opens with a section that details some of the words you'll meet, along with an explanation; all terms are gathered in the glossary at the end. Additional call-out sections are spread throughout with helpful context and short side notes.

### Passage H

Part Five, The Return (Chapters 16–17), asks where the journey ends. The friends of God return to people, with God; every soul returns to God.

This book is inspired by a lecture series on Islamic mysticism given by Sheikh M. S. Bahmanpour. The content and structure of those lectures are the core of the book. Wherever the Sheikh is quoted directly, the words are his own, from that series.

Turning spoken lectures into a book has called for some editorial adaptation. To suit a wider readership, the book also adds context and explanation to support its main arguments. It draws on a wider literature as well: the words of the Imams in Nahj al-Balagha, al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya and the supplications of the Mafatih; then the great Shia teachers of the path, from Sayyid Haydar Amuli in the fourteenth century to modern scholars such as Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi, ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, Imam Khomeini and Ayatullah Mutahhari. Every source is given in a footnote, for readers who want to go further.

### Passage I

The second request is love. The Munajat asks for a place among God's friends and it defines that place by love.

[quotation]

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads the friendship asked for here, wilaya, as having two parts. One is maʿrifah: understanding God, looking at Him, knowing Him. The other is love for Him. In his reading neither is fixed; the love is incessant and the maʿrifah keeps increasing. These are the two aims Chapter One gave ʿirfan and the Sheikh finds both in one line of the Munajat.

The third request is nearness and the Munajat makes it earlier in the same prayer.

[quotation]

In this request the heart is brought near by its own passion for God. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali goes further and asks to love the meeting with God itself. "O Lord of Glory and Honor, (please do) make me love meeting You," it says (يَا ذَا ٱلْجَلالِ وَٱلإِكْرَامِ حَبِّبْ إِلَيَّ لِقَاءَكَ).

### Passage J

These words come from the Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah, a prayer many of us recite in the month of Shaʿban. The line asks God to light up the eyes of our hearts with the light of looking at Him, until those eyes pass through even the veils of light and reach the source of His grandeur. It is a request to see God with the heart and to be near Him. Requests like this one were written for us, by the Imams (as), as an endeavour for the ordinary believer.

Despite saying these words in our munajats and on auspicious occasions, we find that seeking to know God does not feature in our day-to-day routine. Praying, fasting and paying what is due, the wajibat, are the dominant undertaking and they are what most of us mean by practising the religion. The other side, the one the Munajat describes, in which the believer seeks to know God and to come near Him, is often less practised. That side of the religion is called ʿirfan.

### Passage K

The severance the prayers ask for is therefore in the heart, not in the household or the marketplace. It is the severance Chapter One found in the friends of God, who keep their families and their place in society while their hearts are cut off from everything else.

Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali makes the Munajat's request in its own way, naming the attachment it wants removed. "O my Master, (please) expel love for the world out of my heart," it asks (سَيِّدِي أَخْرِجْ حُبَّ ٱلدُّنْيَا مِنْ قَلْبِي). What the prayer asks to lose is the heart's love for the world, not the world itself or the people and the work in it.

Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, describes the same severance from the other side, as something God has already done for those He loves.

### Passage L

Other people look at the surface of the world; the friends of God look at what lies inside it. Sheikh Bahmanpour is careful about what this means. There is nothing wrong with pursuing material wealth in this life; the trouble is being solely occupied with it. The friends of God have families, friends and a place in society, as we do. They think not about themselves but about God and in their hearts they are cut off from everything else.

The Qur'an also tells us what these people receive. The first gift is nearness: Surah al-Waqiʿa sorts people on the Last Day and at the top it places the Foremost Ones.

[verse]

The Arabic for "brought near" is muqarrabun. God is not far from anyone, Sheikh Bahmanpour points out; being brought near means that a veil between the person and God has been removed, so that they understand far more.

### Passage M

ʿIrfan is the Arabic name for what English books call Islamic mysticism and the two words do not say the same thing. "Mysticism" suggests mystery, something hidden and hard to pin down. The Arabic word names a kind of knowing. It shares its root with maʿrifah, knowledge and an ʿarif is, in Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "someone who knows".

The knowing meant here is not information about God. It is knowing Him from close, with the heart, which is what the Munajat asks for. In Murtada Mutahhari's words, the ʿarif "wishes to reach the very kernel and reality of existence, God, to become connected to it and witness it". ʿIrfan is that knowing and the training of the soul that makes it possible. It is part of the Islam we already know: its sources are the Qur'an and the words of the Imams and the prayer at the head of this page is one of them.

### Passage N

Imam Khomeini, in one of his talks, takes this familiarity for granted. "You are already familiar with the words used in Imams' supplications," he tells his listeners, before reading them lines from the Shaʿbaniyyah.

Familiarity with the words is not the same as reaching what they describe and the scholars say so. Maliki Tabrizi, having called the supplications an Ascending-Qur'an, writes that this "is not accepted and comprehended by any one, except a small number of believers who are thoroughly familiar and enlightened". Imam Khomeini makes a narrower point. On one hard question, how to put God's relation to His creation into words, he says that "those who are fit to do so" should pray in the style of the Shaʿbaniyyah for God to enlighten them.

### Passage O

The finer questions of ʿirfan remain an ongoing conversation among our ʿulamaʾ: whether a seeker needs a guide from the first step, how much solitude is wise, how far ordinary believers can travel. Where they differ, this book presents each view with neutrality.

Three guiding principles frame the discussions in the chapters that follow. The first is that ʿirfan is never pursued for the sake of powers, such as reading minds or foreseeing future events. Sheikh Bahmanpour and the wider literature agree on this, as Chapter One shows.

The second is that there is no separate sect or order through which ʿirfan must be gained. Shia ʿirfan has no institution of authority beside the Imam. How this position differs from that of the Sufi orders is explored in Chapter Nine.

### Passage P

The first step of purification is to polish the soul by doing whatever the Prophet ﷺ has asked; "without this", the Sheikh says, "there is no maʿrifah". Purification, in his account, is polishing the soul so that it starts working. It is the repair of the rusted faculty that Chapter One described.

Having done this, the traveller waits for Allah's help and looks to nothing else. The Sheikh points to the verse of Surah al-Anfal that calls the believers to answer Allah and the Apostle when he summons them to what will give them life. That life comes, he says, when a person follows the Prophet completely and then waits for the help of Allah.

The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah ties the light to the path in one line.

### Passage Q

The second kind is knowledge by presence, ʿilm al-huduri. It does not come from outside and needs no concept to carry it; it is with the soul already. The plainest case, in the Sheikh's account, is our knowledge of our own self. Hunger, the example this chapter opened with, is another and so is pain.

Knowledge by presence is complete in a way that acquired knowledge is not. When the body is hungry, the soul knows it at once and without effort and nothing of the hunger is missed. The hunger may even be false, the Sheikh notes, yet the feeling is real and drives us. Anxiety works in the same way. It has a cause outside us, but the feeling comes from inside the soul; the cause is not the feeling.

### Passage R

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads this verse as naming four ranks of those God has blessed: the prophets; the truthful (siddiqin); the shuhadaʾ, which the translation used here renders "martyrs" and which he takes as "the witnesses"; and the righteous (salihin). The first three ranks, he says, are beyond us; the fourth is a doorway. The salihin are those able to receive this favour and from among them God chooses the truthful and the prophets. The way in is righteous action, as the verse on the good life also says.

That doorway is open to people like us: ordinary people, he says, can also enter the realm of the muqarrabun. It is difficult, he says, but it is open, because we are made for it. We call ourselves human beings, yet he says "we are not yet human beings". We begin with the potential to become human in the full sense that the prophets are. He means this as a statement of what we were made for. A human being, he says, has the capacity to become a friend of God; God created us with that capacity and wants us to reach it.
