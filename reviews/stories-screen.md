## The four adds

### #42 Hatim "the Deaf" (Ch 9, 9.3, box)
- **What the transcript says (L12 ¶25):** Hatim was not deaf. A woman came to him with a question and, as she asked it, let out a sound she could not help, and was mortified. He felt her embarrassment and said, "What did you say? I didn't hear." She asked again, louder; he said again that he had not heard. She left sure that he was deaf, and relieved. From then on he was called Hatim al-Asamm, Hatim the Deaf. The Sheikh: "It is actually praise for his personality, the way he treated just an ordinary lady coming to him." He adds that Hatim reports from Shaqiq (Ch 13's Shaqiq).
- **Leave out:** the physical detail itself; say only that an embarrassing sound escaped her. Keep it to the exchange and the nickname.
- **Flags:** no held source; told as the Sheikh's account. Sensitivity: earthy. Tell it in two or three plain sentences, no comedy in the wording; the point is that he carried the nickname for life to spare one woman a moment's shame. Room: Ch 9 would then have a Story box in each of its three sections (Fudayl, told to stop, Hatim). If that reads heavy, the told-to-stop box is the shortest and could become 9.2 prose.

### #26 The Prophet's prayer at the Miʿraj (Ch 12 opener, prose)
- **What the transcript says (L09 ¶26–28):** The Sheikh sets it at the top of the four levels of dhikr, the fourth being when dhikr "flows out of the heart" to the tongue and the whole body (¶26). At the Miʿraj the Prophet ﷺ saw such glory that he said *Allahu akbar*, then recited what God had revealed to him (¶26–27). Then "such a glory tend to the heart" that he was overwhelmed. Two readings: he bowed of himself before that greatness, glorifying "my Lord, the Great", or, as some hadith have it, Jibraʾil told him to bow (¶27). When he rose he saw a greater glory still, fell into prostration glorifying "my Lord, the Most High", and did so twice; and this "was legislated for all of muslims" (¶27). "That salaat that Prophet performed is the culmination of human zikr" (¶28).
- **Leave out:** "he didn't know what to do" (say "overwhelmed", theological care, LESSONS 15); "and not that legislated that he didn't know…" (garbled); ¶28's aside that his attitude on earth differed and "just anything and you go to paradise". Word the two readings so the sentence is true on either: whether he bowed of himself or at Jibraʾil's word, he bowed because of what he saw.
- **Flags:** source is "some hadith", not identified or held [VERIFY]; give it as the Sheikh's report. The tasbih formulas are not in a held extract, so paraphrase them in English (Arabic only if found held). It carries the opener's sujud frame and hands on to 12.1; it does not repeat Khidr at Badr (12.2, the *Huwa* point).

### #45 Rabiʿa and the twelve years without dates (Ch 11, 11.1, prose)
- **What the transcript says (L12 ¶52):** During an illness a friend asked Rabiʿa whether she desired anything. She answered: how can you ask me what I desire? By the glory of God, for twelve years I have wanted fresh dates and have not tasted them; I am a servant of God, and what has a servant to do with desires? The Sheikh: "this is austere asceticism", and "she moved from this later on, from this austere asceticism to mysticism of love"; her custom was to pray all night and sleep briefly before dawn.
- **Leave out:** nothing is garbled; keep it to four sentences. Do not add the torch here (it is 11.2's).
- **Flags:** the Sheikh says "I don't know whether this is a legend or not", so give it as told, not vouched (as the torch is). No held source. Ladder step: even the saint of love began in renunciation and left it behind, which is the section's claim in one life. Of the four adds this is the one to drop first if Ch 11 should carry only its torch.

### #53 The Sheikh finds Tabatabaʾi in Ibn ʿArabi (Ch 14, 14.2, prose)
- **What the transcript says (L13 ¶42–43):** The Sheikh was reading a book by ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi on the names of God and was amazed at how he had analysed them. Then he read Ibn ʿArabi's *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* and saw that Tabatabaʾi had taken it all from there. Tabatabaʾi never claimed it as his own: he was "very much fond of Ibne Arabi" and said everywhere that this knowledge came from him, and Hasanzadeh Amuli records him saying so (¶42–43). "The experiences that Allahma Tabatabai had matched very much with whatever Ibne Arabi had experienced", and "it's not for us to judge because it needs someone who has the experience" (¶43, the planned [14l]).
- **Leave out:** the Fusus dream at the end of ¶43 (#52); "no one had experienced and had theorizes" (garbled); the title of Tabatabaʾi's book is not given, so do not name one.
- **Flags:** the Sheikh's own account; clear transcript; no held source needed. No sectarian edge (14.3 handles the hostile passages). It is his own experience, which is the kind the uncle meant. Ch 14 would carry three short stories, two of them sayings-length in 14.1; this one gives 14.2 its own.
