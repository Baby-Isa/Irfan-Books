## 1D: the passage with the fourteen edits (paste this)

At many weddings, someone recites a verse most of us know. In Surah al-Rum, God says that He created spouses for us from among ourselves "that you may take comfort in them", and that He ordained "affection and mercy" between husband and wife (Q 30:21). The verse ends with the words above: this is a sign for a people who reflect. Whether a person sees it depends on something in the person doing the looking. That something, and why it is worth seeking more than the minimum, is what this chapter sets out.

Let's start with what nobody disputes. A believer who prays, fasts, pays what is due, and goes on hajj can hope for jannah, provided they do what is wajib and avoid what is forbidden. Sheikh Bahmanpour accepts this as fair for ordinary believers, and Mohammad Ali Shomali calls the obligatory rules of fiqh "the minimally necessary conditions of human perfection".

Some jurists and activists go further, and here this book parts company with them. They hold that the obligatory acts are the whole of the religion, and that anything beyond them is made up by the whims and wishes of certain mystics.

The Qur'an answers them a few lines after the wedding verse, where Surah al-Rum names three more sets of signs: the creation of the heavens and the earth, with the variety of our languages and colours; our sleep and our work; and the lightning and the rain that revives the dead earth (Q 30:22–24). Like the wedding verse, each of them ends by naming the people the sign is for.

Four times in a row, the Qur'an makes the same point. The signs are all around us: in marriage, in the people we meet, in sleep, in work, and in the weather. Yet they are for those who reflect, know, listen, and reason. This reflecting, knowing, listening, and reasoning is what the Qur'an asks of us beyond the minimum.

For the avoidance of doubt, this invitation from the Qur'an is not an invitation to circumvent the law or to abandon the prescribed wajibat. Imam Khomeini insists that a genuine seeker's devotion "is within the bounds of the Shari'ah". Within those bounds, the Qur'an is just as insistent: it invites us to more than the obligatory acts.

An invitation addressed to those who reflect, know, listen, and reason implies that many people do none of these things. This is not limited to the careless. Most pious people, even those careful about the shariʿa, do not find a sign in these verses. Looking at the variety of languages and colours, we are more likely to see a reason for division than a sign of God.

We have heard these verses many times, and we accept that there are signs in them because the Qur'an says so; yet accepting that a sign exists is not the same as perceiving it. The Qur'an describes the gap in one verse.

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi explains what passing by a sign means; a person who sees a red light thinks of the danger it warns of, not of the colour and shape of the lamp. In the same way, the things of the world are signs, and "what the signs indicate is not this world, but the person of its Creator". Most of us see only the lamp. We witness the rain as rain and the marriage as a marriage, and we pass them by without perceiving what they say about their Creator.

If the signs are present and plain, and yet we still pass them by, the fault is on our side. The Qur'an says where it lies, speaking of people who have every faculty and use none of them.

Cattle see and hear perfectly well, so the comparison cannot be about the senses. It is about what lies behind the senses. Eyes and ears take in the surface of things, whereas inside a human being there should be something more: a faculty, which animals lack, that turns what the senses take in into understanding. In most of us, it is not working.

Sheikh Bahmanpour compares this faculty to a machine. The ear catches the sound, but "there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is [rusted]." That machine is the soul. It has to be repaired before it can catch the signals of God. This is the whole subject in one sentence: mysticism is about making the soul hear, see, and reflect. It is not easy, and it requires a great deal of training.

If the fault is in the receiver, then evidence was never what we lacked. The signs have been given, and we have failed to receive them.

It also means the problem can be solved. A faculty that exists but has rusted can be repaired; one that is absent cannot be made fit for purpose. The Kernel of the Kernels, a record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's early lectures on the path, opens by saying that the pull towards the unseen is "part of the innate instincts of the human being". The longing for God, and the faculty that perceives Him, are part of how we are made.

Repair is only the start. Once the inner faculty begins to work, a journey begins that, in the Sheikh's words, "has a beginning and no end".

If the faculty can be repaired, there should be people in whom it already works. The Qur'an describes them more than once. People can be divided into two groups: those whose souls work, and those whose souls do not. The first group are the ʿurafaʾ, the plural of ʿarif. The prophets and the Imams (as) are a class of their own; the ʿurafaʾ are ordinary believers who see more than the rest. Surah al-Anfal gives the first of these descriptions.

A heart trembles only at the mention of someone it has some experience of; a name means little to a person who does not know whom it names. The believers of this verse are thus the believers who know Allah. A verse in Surah Al ʿImran goes further, giving them another name.

For Sheikh Bahmanpour, "those who possess knowledge" is another name for the ʿurafaʾ. Every Muslim knows that there is no god but Allah. Our knowledge of tawhid, God's oneness, is still not the knowledge of those who witness it. Tawhid is simple to say and hard to grasp; the intellect alone cannot reach its depths, and only the awakened soul can. Surah Fatir describes the same people by what they feel.

Most of us fear Allah's punishment. The people of this verse fear Him with a fear that is not about punishment at all.

In Surah Yunus they are the awliyaʾ Allah, the friends of God (Q 10:62). Imam ʿAli (as) describes what these friends see that others miss.

Other people look at the surface of the world; the friends of God look at what lies inside it. This does not mean turning away from the world. There is nothing wrong with pursuing material wealth in this life; the trouble is being solely occupied with it. The friends of God have families, friends, and a place in society, as we do. They think not about themselves but about God, and in their hearts they are cut off from everything else.

Surah al-Waqiʿa sorts people on the Last Day, and at the top it places the Foremost Ones, whose reward is nearness.

The Arabic for "brought near" is muqarrabun. God is not far from anyone. Being brought near means that a veil between the person and God has been removed, so that they understand far more.

Surah al-Nahl promises a different kind of life to whoever acts righteously, man or woman, while having faith.

This good life (hayat tayyiba), the life the mystics seek, is a clean life. Its people live in the world without being soiled by it, rising above the worries that occupy everyone else.

And the verse of Surah Yunus that names the friends of God promises them peace.

For the muqarrabun, who have no fear and do not grieve, the terms are entirely different from those of ordinary believers.

None of this would matter to an ordinary reader if it were reserved for prophets. Surah al-Nisaʾ answers that concern.

In this verse the Sheikh finds four ranks of those God has blessed: the prophets; the truthful (siddiqin); the shuhadaʾ, which the translation used here renders "martyrs" and which he takes as "the witnesses"; and the righteous (salihin). The first three ranks are beyond us; the fourth is a doorway. The salihin are those able to receive this favour, and from among them God chooses the truthful and the prophets. The way in is righteous action, as the verse on the good life also says.

That doorway is open to people like us. It is difficult, but we are made for it. We call ourselves human beings, yet, in the Sheikh's words, "we are not yet human beings": we begin with the capacity to become human in the full sense that the prophets are, and God wants us to reach it.
