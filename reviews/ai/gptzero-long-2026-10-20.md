## 1A. Chapter One, first draft (7 Oct, AI, before your reviews)

Few words in religious life are as easily misunderstood as "mysticism". The lecture series behind this book opens by admitting as much. Mysticism, it says, is very mysterious: no one really knows what it is, and even the mystics themselves are often unsure what they are doing, "except the real mystics". So this first chapter does not yet teach the method of ʿIrfan. It asks an earlier question. What is Islamic mysticism after? And why should a believer who already prays, fasts and gives what is due care about it at all?

The Islamic name for the subject is ʿIrfan. In this book it means the discipline of knowing God through spiritual experience and the purification of the soul. The word shares its root with maʿrifah, a word most readers will know from majalis and du'as: knowing God, and recognising Him. The Word Study below looks at the family of words more closely. For now it is enough to notice that the name itself points to knowledge, not to power, and that this is the theme of the whole chapter.

Part of the difficulty is that mysticism is not limited to Islam. There are Christian, Hindu and Buddhist mystics, and even some people with no religion at all feel drawn to it. People also come to mysticism for very different reasons. Some want extraordinary powers. They want, for example, to be in two places at the same time, and so they set out to explore the hidden world of the human soul in order to master the powers it contains. The lectures accept that this kind of mysticism exists in other traditions. Their point is that it must be clearly told apart from the real thing, and that this is "very important".

So the first questions are basic ones. What are we talking about? Who are the mystics? What do they want from a journey that the lectures describe as "exhausting, time consuming, life consuming"? The answer comes from two places: the Quran, and the words of the Imams (ʿa), especially their supplications. There, the lectures argue, we meet a kind of mysticism "very different" from what is found in other religions. This chapter follows that answer. Its conclusion can be given at the start, in the lecture's own closing words: Islamic mysticism "is actually seeking and nothing else".

The lectures begin with an honest challenge. Someone once came to the Prophet ﷺ and said, in effect: I don't have the eagerness to pray salat al-layl (the recommended night prayer) or to keep the recommended fasts. If I pray my five daily prayers, fast in Ramadan, pay my zakat and go to hajj once, will I go to janna (Paradise)? The Prophet said yes. If you do these things, you will go to janna [VERIFY: locate this hadith and its collection].

The lecture presses the point. If it is so easy to reach janna just by performing our wajib (obligatory) duties, why should anyone work so hard? Why take on the long, difficult striving that mysticism asks for? Why bother with what is mustahabb (recommended), with night vigils and inward training, if the door to Paradise is already open to those who do the minimum?

This is not just a thought experiment. The lectures note that some Muslim jurists and activists are "completely against mysticism". Their argument is simple: follow what the shariʿa, the outward religious law, has told you and you will reach janna; "there is no other way". On this view mysticism is something silly, invented by certain mystics "according to their vibes and wishes".

For most believers, religious life is organised around the shariʿa: knowing what is wajib and what is forbidden, and doing one's best to keep within those limits. That is no small thing, and the lectures never belittle it. But they ask whether it is the whole story. Their method of answering is worth noticing. They do not appeal to the authority of famous mystics or to anyone's experiences. They ask: "What does the Quran say about mysticism? Let's see."

The lecture turns to Surah al-Rum (chapter 30 of the Quran). It chooses a few verses and notes that "many other verses in the same line" could have been chosen. Each verse in this passage begins with the refrain "And among His signs…" and ends by naming a particular kind of person for whom the sign is meant. That ending is the key.

> And among His signs is that He created mates for you from yourselves, that you may dwell in tranquillity with them, and He has put love and mercy between you. Surely in that are signs for those who reflect. (Q 30:21) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation; text given here as rendered in the lecture]

What signs? the lecture asks. When we look at the relationship between a man and a woman, do we see a sign of God in it? "Usually," it says with some irony, "we will see the sign of shaitan." The signs are certainly there, but they are not for everyone. They are for "the people who reflect".

> And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your tongues and your colours. Surely in that are signs for those who know. (Q 30:22) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

Here too the lecture asks what we actually see. When we look at the variety of peoples and languages, we tend to see ethnic division, argument and even racism, not a sign of God. Yet the verse says there are signs in this "for the knowledgeable", "for the learned. Not for everyone." The lecture then adds a remark that cuts close to home: not most people, "not even most pious people", and not even most of those who are very strict about the shariʿa find signs in this.

> And among His signs is your sleep by night and by day, and your seeking of His bounty. Surely in that are signs for those who hear. (Q 30:23) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

"Aren't we hearing? Do we hear?" If we truly heard, the lecture asks, why are we not broken down when we meet the signs of Allah, in the way the prophets and the awliyaʾ Allah, the friends of God, break down?

> And among His signs is that He shows you the lightning, arousing fear and hope, and sends down water from the sky, and with it revives the earth after its death. Surely in that are signs for those who use reason. (Q 30:24) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

Put the four endings side by side: those who reflect, those who know, those who hear, those who use reason. The lecture draws a startling conclusion: "Actually Quran is insulting us." Read honestly, these verses tell us that we do not understand, do not hear, do not reflect and are not learned. "I don't know about you," the lecture says. "I feel that Quran is insulting."

Elsewhere the Quran says something similar even more sharply. The next lecture of the series returns to the point with a verse that most Quran readers will have met many times:

> They have hearts with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. They are like cattle; rather, they are more astray. It is they who are the heedless. (Q 7:179) [VERIFY: Qaraʾi translation]

"Like animals, and even worse." It is hard to imagine stronger language. But the verse also contains a puzzle, and the lectures use that puzzle to introduce the central image of the whole series.

The puzzle is this. Animals have eyes and ears. Do they not see and hear? Of course they do. An animal sees where it walks, and when it hears a sound it runs away. So what is the Quran comparing? If the people described in the verse are like animals, it cannot be because their eyes and ears have stopped working.

The lecture's answer is that the ear is only an instrument. Its job is to catch the sound, and it does that job perfectly well. "However there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is rust and doesn't see these signs." What we see and hear with our senses stays on the surface; it is "superficial". To reach the reality of what we see and hear, something inside the human being has to translate it. Animals see and hear, but they do not have that inner machine. Human beings do have it, but in most of us it is not working. It is, in the lecture's words, "dead and blind".

The lectures give an example that sharpens the point. Those who reject faith, the kuffar, also see and hear. If we tell them that they have eyes but do not see, they will laugh. They will say they see better than we do, in more detail and more artistically. And in one sense they are right. The Quran agrees that they see; what it denies is that they understand. The lectures promise that this "enigma of having and not having, listening and not listening, being and not being" will come up again and again in the course.

## 1B. Chapter One as approved by you and your uncle (12 Oct, after seven rounds)

There is a verse often recited at weddings, one most of us know. In Surah al-Rum, God says that He created spouses for us from among ourselves "that you may take comfort in them", and that He ordained "affection and mercy" between husband and wife (Q 30:21). The verse ends with the words above: this is a sign for a people who reflect. Whether a person sees it depends on something in the person doing the looking. That something, and why it is worth developing, is the subject of this chapter.

Let's start with two claims. The first is that a believer who prays, fasts, pays what is due and goes on hajj can hope for jannah, provided they do what is wajib and avoid what is forbidden. This claim is not contested. Sheikh Bahmanpour accepts it as fair for ordinary believers. Mohammad Ali Shomali makes the same point, describing the obligatory rules of fiqh as "the minimally necessary conditions of human perfection".

The second claim goes further, and it is the one this book disputes. Some jurists and activists hold that the obligatory acts are the whole of the religion; anything beyond them, they say, is made up by the whims and wishes of certain mystics.

To see why there is more than the obligations, let's turn to the Qur'an and read on from the verse this chapter began with. Surah al-Rum names three more sets of signs: the creation of the heavens and the earth, with the variety of our languages and colours; our sleep and our work; the lightning, and the rain that revives the dead earth (Q 30:22–24). Each verse ends as the first did, by saying who the sign is for.

Four times in a row, the Qur'an makes the same point. The signs are all around us: in marriage, in the people we meet, in sleep, in work, in the weather. Yet they are for those who reflect, know, listen and reason. This reflecting, knowing, listening and reasoning is what the Qur'an asks of us beyond the minimum.

For the avoidance of doubt, this invitation from the Qur'an is not an invitation to circumvent the law or to abandon the prescribed wajibat. Imam Khomeini insists that a genuine seeker's devotion "is within the bounds of the Shari'ah". Within those bounds, the Qur'an is just as insistent, in these verses and others, that it invites us to more than the obligatory acts.

An invitation addressed to those who reflect, know, listen and reason implies that many people do none of these things. Sheikh Bahmanpour does not limit this to the careless. Most pious people, he says, even those careful about the shariʿa, do not find a sign in these verses. Looking at the variety of languages and colours, we are more likely to see a reason for division than a sign of God.

We have heard these verses many times, and we accept that there are signs in them because the Qur'an says so; yet accepting that a sign exists is not the same as perceiving it. The Qur'an describes the gap in one verse.

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi explains what passing by a sign means; a person who sees a red light thinks of the danger it warns of, not of the colour and shape of the lamp. In the same way, the things of the world are signs, and "what the signs indicate is not this world, but the person of its Creator". Most of us see only the lamp. We witness the rain as rain and the marriage as a marriage, and we pass them by without perceiving what they say about their Creator.

If the signs are present and plain, and yet we still pass them by, the fault is on our side. The Qur'an says where it lies, speaking of people who have every faculty and use none of them.

Cattle see and hear perfectly well, so the comparison cannot be about the senses. Sheikh Bahmanpour reads it as being about what lies behind the senses. Eyes and ears take in the surface of things, whereas inside a human being there should be something more: a faculty, which animals lack, that turns what the senses take in into understanding. In most of us, he says, it is not working.

He compares this faculty to a machine. The ear catches the sound, but "there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is [rusted]." That machine is the soul. It has to be repaired before it can catch the signals of God. For Sheikh Bahmanpour, this is the whole subject in one sentence: mysticism is about making the soul hear, see and reflect. He adds that this is not easy, and requires a great deal of training.

Placing the fault in the receiver has two consequences. The first is that the problem is not a shortage of evidence. God is not withholding signs; we are failing to receive them.

The second is that the problem can be solved. A faculty that exists but has rusted can be repaired; one that is absent cannot be made fit for purpose. The Kernel of the Kernels, a record of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's early lectures on the path, opens by saying that the pull towards the unseen is "part of the innate instincts of the human being". The longing for God, and the faculty that perceives Him, are part of how we are made.

Repair is only the start. Once the inner faculty begins to work, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, the journey begins, and it does not end; it is a path that "has a beginning and no end".

If the faculty can be repaired, there should be people in whom it already works. The Qur'an describes them more than once. Sheikh Bahmanpour divides people into two groups: those whose souls work, and those whose souls do not. He calls the first group the ʿurafaʾ, the plural of ʿarif. He sets the prophets and the Imams (ʿa) aside as a class of their own, and looks at ordinary believers who see more than the rest. The first description of them is in Surah al-Anfal.

A heart trembles only at the mention of someone it has some experience of; a name means little to a person who does not know whom it names. The believers of this verse are thus the believers who know Allah. A verse in Surah Al ʿImran goes further, giving them another name.

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads "those who possess knowledge" as another name for the ʿurafaʾ. Every Muslim knows that there is no god but Allah. Our knowledge of tawhid, God's oneness, is still not the knowledge of those who witness it. Tawhid is simple to say and hard to grasp; in the Sheikh's view, the intellect alone cannot reach its depths, and only the awakened soul can. Surah Fatir describes the same people by what they feel.

Most of us fear Allah's punishment. The people of this verse fear Him with a fear that is not about punishment at all.

Surah Yunus adds to their names, which Sheikh Bahmanpour treats as the same people: the awliyaʾ Allah, the friends of God (Q 10:62). Imam ʿAli (ʿa) describes what these friends see that others miss.

Other people look at the surface of the world; the friends of God look at what lies inside it. Sheikh Bahmanpour is careful about what this means. There is nothing wrong with pursuing material wealth in this life; the trouble is being solely occupied with it. The friends of God have families, friends and a place in society, as we do. They think not about themselves but about God, and in their hearts they are cut off from everything else.

The Qur'an also tells us what these people receive. The first gift is nearness: Surah al-Waqiʿa sorts people on the Last Day, and at the top it places the Foremost Ones.

The Arabic for "brought near" is muqarrabun. God is not far from anyone, Sheikh Bahmanpour points out; being brought near means that a veil between the person and God has been removed, so that they understand far more.

The second gift is a different kind of life, promised in Surah al-Nahl to whoever acts righteously, man or woman, while having faith.

This good life, hayat tayyiba, is in Sheikh Bahmanpour's reading a clean life. Its people live in the world without being soiled by it, rising above the worries that occupy everyone else. This good life, he says, is what the mystics seek.

The third gift is peace, promised in the verse of Surah Yunus that names the friends of God.

For the muqarrabun, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, the terms are entirely different from those of ordinary believers.

None of this would matter to an ordinary reader if it were reserved for prophets. Surah al-Nisaʾ answers that concern.

Sheikh Bahmanpour reads this verse as naming four ranks of those God has blessed: the prophets; the truthful (siddiqin); the shuhadaʾ, which the translation used here renders "martyrs" and which he takes as "the witnesses"; and the righteous (salihin). The first three ranks, he says, are beyond us; the fourth is a doorway. The salihin are those able to receive this favour, and from among them God chooses the truthful and the prophets. The way in is righteous action, as the verse on the good life also says.

## 3. Chapter Three today

These words come near the end of Duʿa Kumayl, the prayer Imam ʿAli (as) taught to Kumayl ibn Ziyad, one of his close disciples. Many of us hear it on Thursday evenings, as the Mafatih recommends. The line asks God for a heart enthralled by His love.

A heart enthralled by God's love is one of the two things ʿirfan seeks. Other prayers we recite ask for the second, knowing God. This chapter shows that we already know these words.

The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah asks to be cut off from everything before it asks to see God. This book opened on its request for light in the eyes of the heart. In the prayer, that line follows straight on from a request for severance. The order fits what Sheikh Bahmanpour says of those who seek God: they want to know Him and to look at Him, but "they have to cut off from everything first".

The Munajat asks for this severance (inqitaʿ) in full. Taken at its word, the request could sound like a call to leave family, work, and society behind. The Sheikh raises the question himself: is it good to cut off from everything else completely? His answer is yes, "in the heart". Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali, recited on the nights of Ramadan, names what is to be cut away from the heart.

The prayer asks that love of the world leave the heart. Duʿa ʿArafah, as given in the Mafatih, describes the same severance from God's side: He has already made it in the hearts of those He loves.

Both prayers speak of love, and both remove it from the heart. The severance the prayers ask for is therefore in the heart.

A heart cleared of everything but God is free for Him. The prayers go on to ask for three things: to see God with the heart, to love Him, and to come near Him.

The first is sight. The Munajat asks God to light the eyes of the heart so that they look at Him and pass even through the veils of light. Yet the Qur'an says that sight cannot perceive God (Q 6:103). The difficulty is a real one, and people unfamiliar with the mystics' wording sometimes call such lines a mistake.

The prayer asks not for the eyes in our heads but for the eyes of the heart. Our physical eyes work perfectly well, and still we miss God's signs, because the inner faculty behind them is blind. The prayer asks God to light that faculty. It is the light the last chapter described, which God gives to a purified heart. Duʿa ʿArafah says He has already lit that light in the hearts of His intimate servants, the friends of God (awliyaʾ).

The second request is love. The Munajat asks for a place among God's friends, and it defines that place by love.

The Sheikh reads the friendship asked for here (wilaya) as having two parts. One is maʿrifah: understanding God, looking at Him, and knowing Him. The other is love for Him. The love never stops, and the knowing keeps growing. These are the two things ʿirfan seeks, and both are in one line of the Munajat.

The request is for a heart whose own passion brings it near, and that heart is itself asked of God. The Whispered Prayer of the Lovers, one of fifteen whispered prayers printed with al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya, asks for a place among those God has chosen for His nearness.

The difference nearness makes is felt in prayer. For some of us, calling on God can feel like calling into the sky from far away. Others feel Him present when they call, and feel His response. With this third request, the prayers have asked for all that ʿirfan seeks: to see God with the heart, to love Him, and to be near Him.

Requests this high raise a difficulty, which Sheikh Bahmanpour puts plainly. These words come from the peak of understanding and longing, from those brought near to God, yet ordinary believers are told to recite them. He compares it to telling a primary school boy to read university books.

Part of the answer is in the prayers themselves, which speak from need. Duʿa ʿArafah comes to God with nothing to offer but its need of Him.

Need is the only plea in this line, and the Munajat line at the head of Part One goes further. There the reciter admits to having had no strength to move away from disobedience "except when You awakened me to Your love", so even the turning towards God is His doing.

The Sheikh's answer to the difficulty is that we have been honoured to be allowed to recite these words, though they are not of our level. The prayers' own pleas from need fit that answer. They claim no level, so a reciter may say them without understanding many of their meanings. If the reciter is sincere, God may grant the request, and what the Imams wanted, He granted them.

The invitation to recite these words reaches us through the Mafatih al-Jinan, which most believers already own. Four of the prayers quoted in this chapter are in it, each set on a night or a day in the believer's year.

The Munajat Shaʿbaniyyah is listed among the acts of the month of Shaʿban, and the Mafatih reports it as the prayer Imam ʿAli (as) and the other Imams used to say in that month. It adds that it is "advisable to say it with presence of heart all the time". The full text, in Arabic and English, is given in the Appendix.

The other prayers have their own times. Duʿa Kumayl belongs to Thursday evenings and to the night of mid-Shaʿban. Duʿa ʿArafah is the prayer of Imam al-Husayn (as) on the Day of ʿArafah. Duʿa Abu Hamza al-Thumali belongs to Ramadan, when Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin (as) said it in the last hour of each night. Maliki Tabrizi tells the traveller not to forget it, and to say it every night or every other night, as his mood and situation allow.

Said on nights we keep, these words become familiar, and Imam Khomeini could count on that familiarity. "You are already familiar with the words used in Imams' supplications," he told his listeners, before reading them lines from the Shaʿbaniyyah.

Reciting the words is not the same as comprehending what they describe. Maliki Tabrizi sees the supplications as a servant's own words rising to God. That, he writes, "is not accepted and comprehended by any one, except a small number of believers who are thoroughly familiar and enlightened".

Sheikh Bahmanpour still recommends the Munajat to every listener, to be recited in Shaʿban as often as they can. The two positions answer different questions: how far a reciter reaches, and who may begin. This chapter claims only that anyone may begin these prayers, with access to their words rather than attainment of what they ask for.

Access to these words is where an ordinary believer already stands on the path. In the voice of someone in need, and on nights the ordinary believer keeps, the prayers ask for what ʿirfan seeks. The goal of ʿirfan is therefore not foreign to us: it is already on our lips. In saying these prayers, we have already begun to ask for it.

These prayers keep asking God to work on the heart, the self within us. What is this self?
