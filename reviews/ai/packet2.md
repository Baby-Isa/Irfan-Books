## P1

The argument is addressed to anyone who prays, fasts, and recites the duʿas, and who has wondered whether there is more to these acts than getting them right; whether the religion has another dimension. No knowledge of ʿirfan, of philosophy, or of Arabic is assumed. This book strives for brevity and clarity in its role as a welcoming guide to beginning the journey towards ʿirfan. Each chapter opens with a section that details some of the words you'll meet, along with an explanation, and all terms are gathered in the glossary at the end. Additional call-out sections are spread throughout with helpful context and short side notes.

Part One, The Call (Chapters One to Three), asks why a believer should bother. It answers that the Qur'an invites more than the minimum: not only knowing about God, but knowing Him. The goal of ʿirfan is already on our lips in our duʿas.

Part Two, Getting Ready (Chapters Four to Seven), describes the landscape a traveller passes through on the journey towards ʿirfan: the self. It also positions the shariʿa as the boundary that keeps the traveller safe, and the guide as the one who walks alongside them.

Part Four, Fellow Travellers (Chapters Thirteen to Fifteen), meets those who walked the road before us. It shows the road's roots in the teachings of the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ, and the Imams from his Ahl al-Bayt; where its travellers arrived; and how Shia ʿirfan relates to Sufism.

## P2

The first door is the tongue, and silence (samt) shuts it. The silence meant here is not a lifelong vow, since the tongue was given to us to speak. It means speaking only of what concerns us and what benefits us or others, and leaving aside idle talk and other people's business. In Kernel of the Kernels, ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi cites a saying of Imam al-Rida (as) that ties this silence to wisdom.

The second door is the stomach, and hunger (juʿ) shuts it. The hunger meant here is not going without food for days. It means never eating to the point of heaviness. A full stomach draws the soul's whole attention to itself, and then, in the Sheikh's words, "the mind goes dizzy and you cannot remember God". In the story told in Chapter Seven, the first counsels Imam al-Sadiq (as) gave ʿUnwan al-Basri were rules of this kind. He was to eat only when hungry, and to eat only what is lawful.

Silence and hunger guard the heart against the chain of idle thoughts, which is among the hardest things to stop. We imagine something, then imagine wanting it, then imagine ways of getting it, and the chain drags us towards sin and away from worship. Silence and hunger give the strength to block it; they also open the way to watchfulness of God (muraqaba), the sense that He is present and sees us. In the hadith of the Prophet's ascension, which Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi records, God says that "the result of silence is wisdom; the result of wisdom is enlightenment; the result of enlightenment is certainty" .

## P3

Acquired knowledge is the work of reason (ʿaql), the faculty by which we understand and argue. Reason has a limit. When we know a thing, we know its attributes: its colour, its size, and what it does. Every description says that it is brown, square, or useful, and none says what the "it" is. Ibn Sina, the master of philosophy, granted this. The reality of things, he held, is beyond the reach of human reason.

If reason cannot reach what the things around us are, it falls still further short of God. God has no colour or size for the mind to take hold of. Imam ʿAli (as) speaks to this at the opening of the first oration in Nahj al-Balagha, where he praises God.

The two images can be read as reason at its widest range and reason at its deepest, and Imam ʿAli says that neither reaches God. The next sentence of the oration continues: God "cannot be described, for he is beyond the limits of demarcated boundaries" . A definition draws a line round its object, and there is no line to draw round God.

Reason still has work it can do. It can argue that God exists, and it can say what He is not: God cannot be an object, and He is not in one place and absent from another. All of this is knowledge about God. Apart from the proof that He exists, the rest is negative knowledge, in which each statement says what God is not, rather than saying what He is.

## P5

If a guide is needed, the next question is what authority he holds. The Sufi orders give one answer. Each order calls itself a path (tariqa) and is built round an elder with his own method of instruction, and each has a chain of masters (silsila) that it traces back to the Prophet ﷺ. Through that chain, the orders hold, the Prophet's inner knowledge passed from heart to heart, from each master to the next.

Shia agree that the Prophet's inner knowledge passed on, but hold that it passed by inheritance to the Imams (as). Imam ʿAli (as) received it as the Prophet's heir, not as one learned companion among others. The orders keep the idea of someone who receives that knowledge, Sheikh Bahmanpour observes. Their chains go back to Imam ʿAli (as), some of them through Imam al-Rida (as) . Yet they do not hold, as Shia do, that the knowledge passed by inheritance to the Imams. For Shia the Imams hold that place, and so, in his words, "We don't have such a thing as tariqa in Shia."

The orders also ask for total submission. In an order, a disciple (murid) submits to the master (shaykh) completely, whatever he says and whatever he does. As a rule, Shia travellers do not join an order. They find an instructor for themselves, one who comes from no order and holds no special licence to instruct. What they look for is a person they can trust, someone to whom "you could entrust your soul".

## P6

That there is no authority but God's is something any believer can work out. A person can think about it for weeks and conclude that God created him and provides for him. The traveller at this degree tastes it. The difference is that between a recipe and the dish. Reading the recipe, we can imagine how the dish will taste, whereas only the cooked dish gives the taste.

The second degree is annihilation in attributes. Wherever the traveller looks, even at the material world, he sees the attributes of God at work. Life, knowledge, and power are in everything around him, and all of them are His. This is the lived truth of words we say often, there is no god but God (la ilaha illa Allah), because Allah is the name that gathers all His attributes. The Throne Verse opens on the attribute of life.

In this verse life belongs to God, and whatever lives does so through Him. The same is true of movement, knowledge, power, and energy, and at this degree the traveller sees them as His wherever he looks.

The third degree is annihilation in essence. Here the traveller no longer sees even the attributes. In the lectures' words, there is "just His glory, which is overshadowing everything". What is left to him is one word, Huwa, "He". The Qur'an uses this pronoun for God, and it points to Him without naming anything beside Him. In Surah al-Ikhlas it is the word after "Say".

The Sheikh reads the friendship asked for here (wilaya) as having two parts. One is maʿrifah: understanding God, looking at Him, and knowing Him. The other is love for Him. The love never stops, and the knowing keeps growing. These are the two things ʿirfan seeks, and both are in one line of the Munajat.

The request is for a heart whose own passion brings it near, and that heart is itself asked of God. The Whispered Prayer of the Lovers is one of fifteen whispered prayers printed with al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya. It asks for a place among those God has chosen for His nearness.

The difference nearness makes is felt in prayer. For some of us, calling on God can feel like calling into the sky from far away. Others feel Him present when they call, and feel His response. With this third request, the prayers have asked for all that ʿirfan seeks: to see God with the heart, to love Him, and to be near Him.

Requests this high raise a difficulty, which Sheikh Bahmanpour puts plainly. These words come from the peak of understanding and longing, from those brought near to God, yet ordinary believers are told to recite them. He compares it to telling a primary school boy to read university books.

Need is the only plea in this line, and the Munajat line at the head of Part One goes further. There the reciter admits to having had no strength to move away from disobedience "except when You awakened me to Your love" , so even the turning towards God is His doing.

## P8

Each depth is therefore reached only through the one before it, and none can be skipped. Sheikh Bahmanpour credits an image for this to Najm al-Din Kubra, the head of one of the Sufi orders. The law is a ship, the path is a sea, and the truth is a pearl in that sea. The pearl "cannot be found except by sailing through the sea, and you cannot sail without a ship".

If the truth is reached only through the law, someone who claims the truth while setting the law aside has left the ship. Kubra draws this conclusion from his own image. Suppose a person is seen flying through the air, walking on the sea, or eating fire. In the Sheikh's rendering of his words, if they have abandoned "even one injunction of God", then "you should know that they are liars".

"Liars" does not mean that the feats are faked, because such things can be done. The lie is in the claim to be a true ʿarif, one who knows God. The person works these wonders for their own desires, and what they do is not a wonder of the friends of God (karama) but magic (sihr).

The claim to know God fails without the law because of what the law does to the one who keeps it. Ayatullah Muhammad Bahari, an Iranian ʿarif, set out the order. Jurisprudence, he taught, is "an introductory means" to purifying one's character. Good character, in turn, is the way into knowledge of God's oneness (tawhid).

## P9

Dark veils are the ones we make ourselves. Our own actions create them, and they belong to the lower self (nafs). This is what Duʿa Abu Hamza means when it says that God conceals Himself from no one unless their wrong deeds stand between them and Him. Each wrong act lays a dark layer over the heart, and a heart under such layers cannot see Allah.

Veils of light are finer, and we do not make them. They belong to the higher levels of the soul, the heart (qalb) and the spirit (ruh), and they come from our existence as creatures. The outer veils of light lift at the end of the journey this chapter describes. Some never lift, even between the Prophet ﷺ and God, because without them creation would perish before the glory of Allah.

Whatever the kind of veil, the hindrance lies on our side, not God's. In the lectures' words, "There is no hindrance from the other side, the hindrance is inside us; we have to remove it." The journey therefore covers no distance. It removes the veils inside us that can be lifted, first the dark ones our deeds have made and then the outer veils of light.

The veils come off in stages, and the mystics count four of them to the end of the soul's first journey towards God. In English they are polishing, emptying, adorning, and annihilating. Each of the next four chapters takes one stage in full, so the aim here is only to see the whole road in order.

## P10

Sincere repentance brings more than forgiveness. In Forty Hadith, Imam Khomeini cites Imam al-Sadiq (as): when a servant turns to God with sincere repentance, "God loves him and covers him (i.e. his sins) in the world and the Hereafter" . Lantern of the Path says what it does to the heart, which God created pure and clean. "When the pure heart turns grey by being fed on heedlessness and vexation, it is polished by the burnish of repentance and cleaned by the water of regret" . This is the polishing that gives the first stage its name.

Once a traveller has begun with repentance, an old habit may still return, and when it does, the traveller repents again. Sheikh Bahmanpour calls repentance a continuous process. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi sets the standard beside it: a repented sin "must not be committed again" . The traveller holds to that standard, and repents again on falling short of it.

Old habits return because the self fights back. The traveller's struggle with the self (mujahada) goes on through every stage of the road. At each stage there is a danger that the self "would come back and … take revenge", and when it strikes hard, it can leave a person reeling for days.

Because the self can strike back, rushing ahead is dangerous. A traveller who pushes forward faster than the self can carry may suddenly fall. A traveller may also arrive quickly at something and mistake it for the end of the road, which is why the right instruction matters. A person first turns back to the self and sees that it must have a Creator. Fuller knowledge of the soul comes later, as the remembrance of God deepens.

A ruined soul loses faith because its owner can no longer see it. A soul that is seen points to its Maker. The body is put together from parts, and a person can suppose that it came about by an evolution of matter. The soul is not like that: as soon as it is seen, its Creator is seen.

## P15

The soul has a share in each of these worlds because it stands between the spirit God breathed into us and the body. The Qur'an says God breathed of His spirit into the first human being (Q 15:29). God is not made of parts, so the spirit is called His out of honour, as the Kaʿba is called His house. This spirit is the greatest of His creations. Pure spirit and matter cannot meet directly. The soul is the means by which that spirit reaches the body, as the world of images links intellect and matter. It therefore touches matter through the body and can enter the middle world.

For now, the body holds almost all of the soul's attention, so the soul lives in the material world and the deeper worlds pass unnoticed. That hold loosens at three times: in sleep, at death, and in watchfulness. Taking them one at a time shows where the soul goes when the hold loosens.

The first is sleep. When we sleep, the soul attends less to the body. With the senses at rest, it can touch the world of images. It does not leave the body completely. If it did, we would be dead. This explains how a dream can show more than memory holds. A person may dream of something that later happens, and memory cannot show the future. Time works differently in the middle world. The soul has seen there the counterpart of an event that has not yet happened here.

## P16

ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi explains what passing by a sign means; a person who sees a red light thinks of the danger it warns of, not of the colour and shape of the lamp. In the same way, the things of the world are signs, and "what the signs indicate is not this world, but the person of its Creator" . Most of us see only the lamp. We witness the rain as rain and the marriage as a marriage, and we pass them by without perceiving what they say about their Creator.

If the signs are present and plain, and yet we still pass them by, the fault is on our side. The Qur'an says where it lies, speaking of people who have every faculty and use none of them.

Cattle see and hear perfectly well, so the comparison cannot be about the senses. It is about what lies behind the senses. Eyes and ears take in the surface of things, whereas inside a human being there should be something more: a faculty, which animals lack, that turns what the senses take in into understanding. In most of us, it is not working.

Sheikh Bahmanpour compares this faculty to a machine. The ear catches the sound, but "there is something inside which doesn't catch it. There is a machine inside which is [rusted]." That machine is the soul. It has to be repaired before it can catch the signals of God. This is the whole subject in one sentence: mysticism is about making the soul hear, see, and reflect. It is not easy, and it requires a great deal of training.
