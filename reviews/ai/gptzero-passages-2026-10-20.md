## Chapter Twelve: what is lost is the false self

### A. First draft (raw AI, before any editing)

Passing away begins at the height of adornment. At the end of that stage God shows the traveller His glory, in small measure, and no one can watch that glory and perceive himself at the same time. The glory does not wait for an empty space to fill; it comes, and everything else goes from the traveller's sight, even his sense of himself. Something like it happens to a person seized by great fear or great joy, who can cut his hand and not feel it, because what is in front of him has taken all his attention.

Passing away is therefore a change in what the traveller sees, and not in what exists. Sheikh Bahmanpour defines it as "the annihilation of self perception of the mystics in perceiving the unveiling [of] the reality of God". The world goes on as before. What changes is the traveller's knowing, which makes passing away, in the lectures' phrase, "an epistemological issue". ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi says the same of the world around the traveller: "this does not mean that everything in the material world loses its existence. Actually, the devotee sees unity in plurality".

The traveller's own soul remains as well. What he loses is the sense of self, while "the soul is there, everything is there". Passing away is not nirvana, which the lectures describe as the Buddhist goal of ending individuality. In Islamic mysticism, "it is the individual who is going to be there forever getting more expanded, more capable". If the individual dissolved, no one would be left to taste what God shows, since only an individual can experience anything. The Qur'an keeps the individual even in paradise, where "Their Lord will give them to drink a pure drink" (Q 76:21).

## Chapter Nine: steady, not fast

### A. First draft (raw AI, before any editing)

Once a traveller has begun with repentance, an old habit may still return. When it does, Sheikh Bahmanpour says, the traveller repents again, because repentance is a continuous process and not a single act. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi sets the bar from the other side. Among his rules for the path is steadiness, which "means that after feeling penitence about a sin and asking Allah's forgiveness for it, it must not be committed again". The two views meet. At the moment of repenting, the resolve is never to return, and when a traveller falls, the answer is fresh repentance rather than despair.

Old habits return because the self fights back. The traveller's struggle with the self (mujahada) goes on through every stage of the road. At each stage there is a danger that the self "would come back and … take revenge", and when it strikes hard, it can leave a person reeling for days. Lantern of the Path asks the repentant, for this reason, to "seek Allah's help in showing repentance, and to protect him from returning to what he did before".

Because the self can strike back, rushing ahead is dangerous. A traveller who pushes forward faster than the self can carry may suddenly fall. Such a traveller may also arrive quickly at something that is not where the road ends, which is why the right instruction matters. Sheikh Bahmanpour asks his listeners to be aware of "the dangers of rushing things forward", and to walk the road at the pace the Imams (as) advised. He illustrates the point with a story a friend from Iran had told him that morning.

## Chapter Eleven: what God gives the emptied heart

### A. First draft (raw AI, before any editing)

Adornment (tahliya) is God's act on the soul, not the traveller's. Once the self is polished and the heart emptied, the soul begins to take its adornment from the spiritual world, and God feeds it with what others cannot take in. The mystics name what comes: visions, inspirations (ilham), and a deeper understanding of the Qur'an. In Sheikh Bahmanpour's words, "It is Allah's act on the soul." Sayyid Haydar Amuli describes the change of character that comes with it. The soul loses its viler features, among them vanity, arrogance, and envy, and takes on the finer ones, "gnosis, wisdom, forbearance, humility, generosity, chastity and courage".

These gifts are not withheld from anyone; what differs is the heart that receives them. They are all around us, and only a purified soul can catch them. A mystic sees and hears what others cannot, "not because … they are chosen ones but because they have furbished their souls". The soul that was blind begins to see, and the soul that was deaf begins to hear. Surah al-Anʿam describes such a person.

The verse joins the new life to a light, and the one who is given it walks with that light among the people.

Knowledge received in this way is a reward the Qur'an promises to those who do good. Surah Yusuf says so of the Prophet Yusuf (as), before he was given his mission.

10,11,12 & 13. Dhawq, Shurb, Sukr And Riyy The 'urafa' believe that mere conceptual knowledge of anything has no attraction; the attractiveness of a thing and the ability to inspire passion is subsequent to 'tasting'. At the end of the eighth section of his al-'Isharat Ibn Sina mentions this; he gives the example of a man who is impotent. He says that however much one may describe sexual pleasure to a person devoid of the sexual instinct, who has never had the taste of this pleasure, he will never be sexually aroused. Thus, dhawq is the tasting of pleasure. In the terminology of 'irfan it means the actual perception of the pleasure derived from manifestations (tajalliyat) and revelations (mukashafat). Dhawq is the beginning of this, its continuance is called shurb (drinking), its joy sukr (intoxication) and being satiated with it riyy (thirst-quenching). The 'urafa' are of the view that whatever is derived from dhawq is 'an appearance of intoxication' (tasakur) and not 'intoxication' (sukr) itself. Intoxication, they say, is obtained from 'drinking' (shurb). That which is obtained by 'becoming quenched' (riyy) is 'sobriety' (sahw), or the return to the senses. It is in this sense that the 'urafa' have talked much about sharab and mey that would ordinarily mean wine. 14, 15 & 16.
