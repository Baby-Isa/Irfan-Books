**Conclusion (one sentence the chapter proves):** The road later became the Sufi orders; Shia share the path and its source, but not the orders' authority, which belongs to the Imam.

**Prologue promise answered:** "Shia ʿirfan has no institution of authority beside the Imam. How this position differs from that of the Sufi orders is explored in Chapter Fifteen." (`book2/chapters/00-prologue.md`). §4 answers it; §1–§3 set up what the orders are and what Shia share with them.

## Chapter points
- **15.1** A Sufi order is a path that, centuries after the first seekers, became an institution of chain, method and obedience.
- **15.2** Almost every order runs its chain back to Imam ʿAli (as), so Sufi and Shia seekers draw on one source.
- **15.3** The path is shared, but no single method fits every soul, so a traveller needs a guide who has walked the road more than an order's technique.
- **15.4** Shia masters used teacher, robe and pledge, but gave none of them the authority that belongs to the Imam.

## Argument, in order
1. An order is a path made an institution: elder's method of stations, chain to the Prophet ﷺ (L14 ¶3–7; Ch 7 recalled).
2. It is late: no Sufis by name in the first century (`light` p. 22; L14 ¶7, ¶25).
3. It became "a vast and independent system" of obedience, liturgy, robes and music (`light` p. 6).
4. Its obedience is total, the corpse before the washer (L14 ¶20–21).
5. Its chains branched, were contested and loosened by licences (L14 ¶10–19).
   - **Interim conclusion A:** an order is held together by chain, method and obedience.
6. ʿIrfan derives from Imam ʿAli (as) and the chains culminate in him (`kernel` p. 87), even Suhrawardi's (`light` p. 34; `inner_secrets` p. 13; `light` p. 25); only the Naqshbandiyya differs (L14 ¶37, ¶63, ¶79).
7. The chain and the pole are the Shia idea of the Imam (L14 ¶8–10; `light` pp. 5–6).
   - **Interim conclusion B:** one source.
8. Shia share the dhikr and the books (L14 ¶57–58; `forty_hadith` p. 8).
9. The orders differ by technique (L14 ¶58–60, ¶64, ¶76–77), yet all produced ʿurafaʾ: no single method (¶65).
10. Each soul is a channel for grace, blocked by deeds, receiving it in its own shape (L14 ¶66–69; Q 11:119).
11. The ways are many inside Islam (`inner_secrets` p. 195; L14 ¶70) and inside the law (`kernel` pp. 86–87).
12. So a traveller needs someone who has walked the road (L14 ¶23–24, Hasanzadeh; Ch 7 §1 recalled).
   - **Interim conclusion C:** shared path, no binding method, a guide.
13. Obedience to a chain is not shared: Ch 7 recalled; the orders' chains contested, the Imams' line clear (L14 ¶9–11).
14. Shia masters stood outside the orders (`light` pp. 40–41), though they used robe, pledge and teacher (`inner_secrets` pp. 8, 192; `kernel` pp. 60, 64; Ch 4's box).
15. Those forms did not give what the traveller received; God did (`inner_secrets` p. 14), and the Imam accompanies the traveller at every rank, the most important condition of the road (`kernel` p. 81).
   - **Conclusion:** Shia share the source and the path, but the authority belongs to the Imam.

## Where scholars differ (D20, shown side by side and gently)
- The Sheikh: Shia have no *tariqa* (Ch 7). Amuli took the Sufi robe, made a contract with a shaykh, reports that the shaykhs agree a traveller needs "a shaykh of perfection", and writes that the soul at rest accepts the guidance of "the Prophet, the Imam or the shaykh without any further argument" (`inner_secrets` pp. 8, 192); Tabatabaʾi assumes a pledge to an aware shaykh; Ayatullah Khomeini was Shahabadi's murid. Resolved by Amuli's own words: his arrival came from God, not from the forms (§4 step 4), and his own order of names puts the shaykh after the Prophet and the Imam (§4 steps 3–5).
- The Naqshbandiyya is shown three ways, each as the source gives it (the exception in the chains; a model of silent remembrance, company and the law; the source of a method Akhund's line did not take); the hostility items stay cut.
