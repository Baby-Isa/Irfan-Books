**Conclusion (one sentence the chapter proves):** This road began in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as); its first seekers learned from the Imams and stood below them, so it was never brought in from outside.

## Chapter points
- **13.1** The road's material was in Islam from its first generation: in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him. [B4]
- **13.2** The first seekers after the companions learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and stood below them. [B4]
- **13.3** So the road was not brought in from outside; at most, some techniques were borrowed, and the later theory drew on outside ideas. [B4]

## Sections
1. **The Qur'an, the companions and the Imams** [13.1]
   - **Point:** The road's material was in Islam from its first generation, in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him.
   - **Paragraph points (the reader's questions):** 1. Some picture Islam as simple: a builder God, a piety of fear and hope, companions who were only pious men; Mutahhari rejects it (`light` pp. 16–17). 1a. Is God a builder outside His house? His essence "is everywhere and with everything" (`light` p. 17); Q 2:115; then the Qur'an's own words for the road already walked, meeting, the three selves, purification, love (`light` p. 18, callbacks). 1b. Was early piety only fear and hope? The Prophet's own life and prayers, "rich with spiritual enthusiasm and ecstasy" (`light` p. 19); Harithah recalled (Ch 3); "burning spiritual love" (`light` p. 19). 1c. Were the companions only pious men? Abu Dharr, Salman, Uways, named most often by Sunni and Shia alike (L11 ¶35, ¶38; box). 2. Whose words hold it most fully? Imam ʿAli's, "a treasure of knowledge" (`light` pp. 4–5); the chains run to him (pointer to Ch 15). 3. And after him? Imam al-Sadiq, one of the greatest early teachers (Schimmel, as the Sheikh reports, L11 ¶45–46); the Sheikh dates it further back, to Imam ʿAli (¶59–60, his view, the hadith left out). 4. Where does an ordinary believer meet it? In Kumayl, Abu Hamza, the Sahifa and the Shaʿbaniyya (`light` p. 20; recalls Ch 3). 5. Therefore: 13.1.
2. **The first seekers and the Imams above them** [13.2]
   - **Point:** The first seekers after the companions learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and stood below them.
   - **Paragraph points:** 1. Who carried it into the next century? Second-century ascetics who "conceded that they were introduced to spiritualism by the first group and trained by it" (`light` p. 5; "the first group" is Imam ʿAli's companions, said plainly); Ch 11's three temperaments recalled. 1a. Fear: Fudayl (Ch 9); the *Lantern of the Path* is said to be a seeker's lessons from Imam al-Sadiq, Fudayl's for Mutahhari (`light` p. 25), Shaqiq's for some the Sheikh reports (L12 ¶10), side by side. 1b. Renunciation: Ibrahim ibn Adham, taught by Imam al-Sadiq (L12 ¶11); his three levels of *zuhd*, the highest found already in Imam ʿAli's saying, Nahj 3.221 (L12 ¶12–16). 1c. Love: Maʿruf al-Karkhi, a Muslim at Imam al-Rida's hands (`light` p. 25); love a "divine gift" (L12 ¶39); Rabiʿa recalled (Ch 11), the exception with no master (L12 ¶55), measured in 3. 2. Why are the famous names Sunni? (`light` p. 5) 2a. The Imams' names were dangerous: Hasan al-Basri hid Imam ʿAli's name (L11 ¶67–68); Shaqiq did not name the Ahl al-Bayt (L12 ¶24). 2b. The Imams' followers spoke only of the Imams (L11 ¶56–57). 3. Were they the Imams' equals? No: "an echo" (L11 ¶58); the Lovers' prayer above Rabiʿa's verses; love missing in the other mystics but not in the Imams' teaching (L12 ¶56, paraphrased); the definition of love the mystics repeat is the Imam's (L12 ¶38; Ch 11). 4. Told story: Shaqiq and the young man on the Mecca road, who was Imam al-Kazim (L12 ¶30–37; Q 49:12 inline as the Qur'an's words, Q 20:82 as a verse block). 5. "completely different in terms of their calibre" (L12 ¶37), the Sheikh's sentence only. 6. Therefore: 13.2.
3. **Not brought in from outside** [13.3]
   - **Point:** So the road was not brought in from outside; at most, some techniques were borrowed, and the later theory drew on outside ideas.
   - **Paragraph points:** 1. Why look elsewhere? They "cannot believe that Islam as a religion of law and jurisprudence can produce mystics" (L11 ¶3); three candidates. 1a. Monks: "Sufi" from wool (`light` p. 22); but the Qur'an, the road's own source, had named monasticism an innovation not prescribed, Q 57:27, keeping its balance that the faithful among them had their reward; the Sheikh: "this is not the way" (L11 ¶10–11); celibacy and lodges differ. 1b. Neoplatonism: came late, with the theory (L11 ¶20–21; `light` p. 16). 1c. Buddhism: Khurasan and the Buddha-like prince; but the concepts differ, renunciation is in the Qur'an, and the prince learned from Imam al-Sadiq (§2); at most techniques (L11 ¶21–23, ¶74). 2. What is certain: "from Islam itself and from nowhere else" (`light` p. 16). 3. Western scholarship came round: Nicholson and Massignon; "Surely the seeds of mysticism are here." (`light` pp. 20–21). 4. Jurdaq's pitcher, back to Abu Dharr; "the great ocean of Islam" (`light` p. 20). 5. Therefore (section and chapter).

## Where scholars differ (D20, shown side by side and gently)
- Hasan al-Basri: Mutahhari begins the history with him and orders trace through him to Imam ʿAli (`light` pp. 5, 23); the Sheikh finds him ambivalent (L11 ¶64). §2 box.
- Dhu'l-Nun: Nicholson saw Neoplatonic influence; Schimmel saw the Imams' teaching surfacing in him (both as the Sheikh reports, L11 ¶58; L12 ¶63–64). §2 box.
- Outside influence: the Sheikh allows some techniques (L11 ¶22–23); Mutahhari records a view that philosophy shaped the later structure (`light` p. 16). Both are certain of the source. §3.
- *Lantern of the Path*: attributed to Fudayl as lessons from Imam al-Sadiq (`light` p. 25); some, the Sheikh reports, say Shaqiq wrote it (L12 ¶10). §2 step 1a.
- Left for Ch 15: Tabatabaʾi's report that some masters hold the mystics' system was devised by them yet approved, as monasticism was (`light` p. 5).
