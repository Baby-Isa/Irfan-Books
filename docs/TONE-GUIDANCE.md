# Steering the tone: a note for the author

Your voice notes worked because they quoted the offending line and said what was wrong with it. Here is how to get that effect with less of your time, what would close the gap fastest, and some published books whose register is close to the one you describe.

## How to steer tone more effectively

Give a model, not only a diagnosis. One short passage you like, from any book, with a line saying why ("warm without chumminess", "explains a verse without lecturing"), does more than a page of corrections. A writer can imitate a passage but can only avoid a correction. Two or three passages from different books fix the target better than one.

Mark up a page rather than describe it. Margin comments on the PDF ("cut", "why?", "who says?", "too grand", "this is the point, start here") are faster for you and more precise for the writer than a summary. "Keep this" is as useful as "cut this"; at the moment the writer only knows what to avoid.

Name three adjectives and three anti-adjectives. For example: clear, warm, unhurried; not clever, not chatty, not preachy. A writer tests sentences against six words more reliably than against a paragraph of guidance.

Ask for two variants of one paragraph and pick one. When a paragraph is still wrong after a round, have it rewritten two ways, one plainer and one warmer, and choose. The choice teaches your ear faster than any instruction, and the chosen version becomes the model for the chapter.

Turn a fault into a rule only when it repeats. One bad sentence is a correction; the same fault three times goes into TONE.md.

## What would close the gap fastest

One page, from anywhere, that reads as you want this book to read, with a line on what you hear in it. One page of the current draft marked up line by line, including the lines that are right. And a few lines on the reader you picture: their age, what they already do, what they have tried before, and what would make them close the book. "A woman-friendly book that is not overly familiar" and "an intelligent, impatient friend" are a start; a sentence more would settle many small decisions at once.

## Published books in the register you describe

**Mohammad Ali Shomali, *Self-Knowledge*.** A held source. Short chapters, plain sentences, each quotation followed by its meaning in the next sentence. Borrow that habit.

**Murtada Mutahhari, "An Introduction to ʿIrfan", in *Light Within Me*.** Held. The clearest short account of what ʿirfan is and how it differs from philosophy and fiqh, written for students. Borrow the definitions and the side-by-side comparisons.

**ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi, *Shiʿite Islam* (translated by Seyyed Hossein Nasr).** A calm, orderly account of the faith for an intelligent outsider. Borrow the pace: one idea per section, stated once, then supported.

**Reza Shah-Kazemi, *Spiritual Quest: Reflections on Qurʾanic Prayer According to the Teachings of Imam ʿAli*.** Shia, devotional, serious and gentle. Borrow the tone used of the Imams: reverent, exact, never sentimental.

**William Chittick, *Sufism: A Short Introduction*.** A scholar writing for lay readers without jargon. Borrow the way a term is defined in the sentence where it first appears, and the short quotations.

**Rowan Williams, *Being Christian*.** Not Islamic, but the register is yours: short talks on practice, warm, direct, comfortable for any reader, free of in-group chat. Borrow the walk from an everyday act to what it means.

**C. S. Lewis, *Mere Christianity*.** The standard example of argument for lay readers: one case per chapter, in order, in plain words, treating the reader as capable. Borrow the paragraph linkage; each opening picks up the last close.

**Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*.** You know it. It is the book's structure, and a test for the prose: a reader should be able to read only first sentences and follow.
