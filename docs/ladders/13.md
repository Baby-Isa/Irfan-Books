## Opening (≤150 words; told story #32, D47)
1. *Hook.* Many of us know Abu Dharr al-Ghifari from the majalis. He spoke out against the favouritism and injustice of the rulers after the Prophet ﷺ, and "This led him to suffer torture and exile, and finally it was in exile, deserted and alone, that he passed away from this world." [`light` p. 20] (checked, p. 20). In the Sheikh's account the Prophet ﷺ had said that Abu Dharr would live alone, die alone and be raised alone, and he died alone in a faraway desert (L11 ¶35; paraphrased, no held text of the saying).
2. *Raises the chapter's question.* "A number of orientalists have raised the question of what motivated Abu Dharr to act as he did." [`light` p. 20] (checked, p. 20). They looked for something outside Islam to explain him (same page, paraphrased), and Western books ask the same of the whole road (L11 ¶3–4). Hand-off into §1, in the title's words: the reader has walked the road for twelve chapters; where does it come from? Mutahhari puts the choice plainly: is ʿirfan like jurisprudence and Qur'an commentary, grown from Islam's own material, or like medicine and mathematics, brought in from outside? [`light` p. 16, paraphrased]. The chapter's answer: it comes from the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as).

## §1 The Qur'an, the companions and the Imams
**Point: The road's material was in Islam from its first generation: in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him.**
1. *If the road grew inside Islam, its material should be there from the start. Some say it is not.* Their picture of Islam is a simple one: a Creator outside His world as a builder is outside his house, a piety of fear of hell and hope of paradise, and companions who were only pious men [`light` pp. 16–17, paraphrased]. Mutahhari: "In reality this view can in no way be endorsed." [`light` p. 17] (checked, p. 17). *(Reader told, naturally: the picture has three parts, and each has its answer.)* *(Quote raises the three questions that follow.)*
   - a. *Is the God of the Qur'an a builder outside His house?* "The Quran identifies God as the Creator of the world, stating at the same time that His Holy Essence is everywhere and with everything" [`light` p. 17] (checked, p. 17; the source sentence ends in a colon before its verses). Verse block Q 2:115 (Qaraʾi): "To Allah belong the east and the west: so whichever way you turn, there is the face of Allah!" (checked, `build/quran.py 2:115`), the first verse Mutahhari gives. *(Verse answers.)* Callback in a clause: the nearness Chapter Four and Chapter Eight built on. One more sentence, so that "in the Qur'an" rests on more than one verse: Mutahhari then lists the Qur'an's own words for the road the reader has walked, the meeting with God, the three selves, the purification of the soul, and love of God above every other love [`light` p. 18, paraphrased; callbacks in a clause to Ch 4–5, 9 and 11].
   - b. *Was the piety of the first generation only fear and hope?* Start with the Prophet ﷺ himself, whom the chapter point names: his life, words and prayers are "rich with spiritual enthusiasm and ecstasy", and the ʿurafaʾ rely on his supplications [`light` p. 19] (checked, p. 19; the phrase runs "The life, utterances and prayers of the Holy Prophet (S) are rich with spiritual enthusiasm and ecstasy"). Then the young companion whom he found after the dawn prayer, seeing paradise and hell, recalled in one sentence (Ch 3 tells and quotes it; not retold). Mutahhari: the lives of the early figures show "burning spiritual love" [`light` p. 19] (checked, p. 19; a phrase, not spliced).
   - c. *Were the companions only pious men?* The Sheikh: at the Prophet's own time there were mystics, and Sunni and Shia sources alike name three companions most often: Abu Dharr, Salman al-Farsi and Uways al-Qarani (L11 ¶34–35, ¶38, ¶44; ¶35 says "mentioned most", so not "above the rest"). *(Who's Who box: the three companions.)*
2. *Of all the companions, whose words hold this teaching most fully?* Imam ʿAli (as). Tabatabaʾi: "His sayings on this subject are a treasure of knowledge." [`light` p. 4] (checked, p. 4); the sayings of the other companions that have come down "do not contain enough material on this subject" [`light` pp. 4–5] (checked, the phrase runs across the page break). The Sheikh agrees: Nahj al-Balagha is full of the love of God, and companions not linked to Imam ʿAli do not speak this way (L11 ¶60–61). The mystics, Sunni and Shia, trace their chains to him through companions such as Salman, Uways and Kumayl [`light` p. 5]; one sentence, with a pointer to Chapter Fifteen, which owns the chains.
3. *And after Imam ʿAli?* The Imams after him taught the same. Annemarie Schimmel, a Western historian of Islamic mysticism, accepts, as the Sheikh reports her, that Imam al-Sadiq (as) was one of the greatest teachers of early mysticism (L11 ¶45–46; paraphrase, Schimmel not held, so not quoted); the Sheikh adds that it should be dated further back, to the earlier Imams and to Imam ʿAli (as) (L11 ¶59–60; his evidence there is the four-levels hadith, which is not held (D15), so give the dating as his view and leave the hadith out).
4. *Where would an ordinary believer meet that teaching?* In the prayers already in our hands (callback to Ch 3): "The Du'a' Kumayl, the Du'a' Abi Hamzah, the supplications of al-Sahifat al-Kamilah and the group of supplications called Sha'baniyyah, all contain the most sublime spiritual ideas." [`light` p. 20] (checked, p. 20). *(Quote answers; spelling of the duʿa names inside the quotation stays as printed.)*
5. Therefore: the road's material was in Islam from the first generation, in the Qur'an, in the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and most fully in Imam ʿAli (as) and the Imams after him.

## §2 The first seekers and the Imams above them
**Point: The first seekers after the companions learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and stood below them.**
1. *The companions had the teaching. Who carried it into the next century?* Men the people took for holy men, Ibrahim ibn Adham and Shaqiq of Balkh among them, who seemed only ascetics: "They did not talk openly of gnosis or mysticism, though they conceded that they were introduced to spiritualism by the first group and trained by it." [`light` p. 5] (checked, p. 5; Tabatabaʾi. His list names Ibrahim ibn Adham; its "Sharif Balkhi" is not relied on, so Shaqiq is named from L12 ¶9, ¶24 instead). **Say what "the first group" is:** on the same page it is Imam ʿAli's companions, Salman, Uways, Kumayl and the rest, so the chain Tabatabaʾi gives runs from Imam ʿAli through his companions to these men; steps 1a–1c then add what the lectures and `light` pp. 24–25 show, that the best known of them also learned from the Imams of their own day. Chapter Eleven met these seekers by their temperaments, fear, renunciation and love, each with all three (recalled in a clause, not retold). *(Reader told, naturally: one master of each temperament shows where each learned.)*
   - a. *Fear.* Fudayl ibn ʿIyad, the highwayman whose turning Chapter Nine told (recalled in a clause). New here: *Lantern of the Path*, quoted since Chapter One as the book attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as), is said to be the record of lessons an early seeker took from the Imam. Mutahhari names Fudayl: "The book Misbah al-Shariah is attributed to him and it is said to consist of a series of lessons that he took from al-'Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (A)." [`light` p. 25] (checked, p. 25); the Sheikh reports that some name Shaqiq instead (L12 ¶10, "Some people say that it was written by Shaqeeq Balkhi and it was attributed to Imam Jafar Sadiq"; paraphrase). Side by side in one sentence, D20; either way the book is a second-century seeker's lessons from the Imam, which is the step's point. Consistent with Ch 1 and Ch 7's "attributed to Imam al-Sadiq (as)". The Sheikh: Fudayl reported abundantly from Imam al-Sadiq (L12 ¶21).
   - b. *Renunciation (zuhd).* Ibrahim ibn Adham, a prince of Balkh, learned from Imam al-Sadiq (as) while still young, left his palace with no provision, and became a byword for poverty and trust in God (*tawakkul*) (L12 ¶11; L11 ¶78; `light` p. 24). He taught three levels of renunciation: of the world's pleasures; of the pleasure of having renounced; and a third where the world is too slight to mention (L12 ¶12–16). The Sheikh says these concepts came from the Imams (L12 ¶14) and explains the third level with a saying of Imam ʿAli (as) (L12 ¶15). Quote block Nahj 3.221: "By God, this world of yours is worth less in my eyes than the bones of a pig in the hand of a leper." [`nahj`, 3.221; PDF p. 760, printed p. 745] (checked; Arabic copied from `sources/extracts/nahj.md` ll. 26471–26472, "خنز ير" rejoined, fix pass 2026-11-01). *(Quote answers: Ibrahim's highest level is already in Imam ʿAli's words; say "is found in", not "comes from", which is more than the Sheikh says.)* The next sentence follows from it: we renounce only what has value, "You renounce something which is valuable, isn't it?" (L12 ¶15) (checked), so a world worth less than that bone is not worth the word.
   - c. *Love.* Maʿruf al-Karkhi "became a Muslim at the hands of al- 'Imam al-Rida (A), learning much from him" [`light` p. 25] (checked, p. 25; print "al-Rida" with the house (as) if paraphrased) and was among the first to teach that love is "divine gift and not an acquisition" (L12 ¶39) (checked; the point Chapter Eleven made, now given its early voice). Rabiʿa, whose torch Chapter Eleven told, recalled in a clause, with the one fact the reader will ask about: the Sheikh reports that she studied under no master (L12 ¶55), so she is the exception to "learned from the Imams", and step 3 measures her against the Imam's prayer.
2. *If they learned from the Imams, why are the famous names Sunni, and the Shia hardly named?* Tabatabaʾi grants that most mystic leaders in the biographies "belonged to the Sunni school of thought" [`light` p. 5] (checked, p. 5). Two answers, each concrete:
   - a. The Imams' names were dangerous to say. Hasan al-Basri, asked whom he heard his hadith from, said that when he names no companion the companion is Imam ʿAli, whose name was not safe to speak under the Umayyads (L11 ¶67–68); Shaqiq reported from the Ahl al-Bayt without naming them, because in his town naming them was not done (L12 ¶24; the transcript's "abandoned" may be "banned", so word it to be true on either reading).
   - b. The Imams' own followers spoke of nothing but the Imams. The Sheikh: the Shia "never spoke of themselves" and said only what Imam al-Sadiq or Imam al-Baqir had said (L11 ¶57) (checked), as Salman fell silent beside the Prophet ﷺ (L11 ¶56); seekers raised among Sunnis repeated what they heard in their own words, and people were drawn to them (L11 ¶45–46, ¶57). Reworded per the outline: the Sheikh's "complete Shia influence" (L11 ¶51) is not used; say they "drew on the Imams' teaching", with Tabatabaʾi's finding that what has come down of the early gnostics' visions mostly holds what the sayings of Imam ʿAli and the other Imams hold [`light` p. 5, paraphrased; not spliced].
3. *Did they then stand level with the Imams, as fellow travellers?* No. What they said was "an echo" of Imam al-Baqir and Imam al-Sadiq (L11 ¶58) (checked); Rabiʿa's verses do not compare with Imam Zayn al-ʿAbidin's Whispered Prayer of the Lovers (L11 ¶58–59; recalls Ch 3 and Ch 11). Love was missing among the early mystics before Rabiʿa, the Sheikh says, and he exempts the Imams' teaching in the same breath (L12 ¶56; paraphrase, since the transcript's "I'm not talking about teaching of immah" is garbled). The proof that the exemption is earned: the definition of love that later mystics repeat, love as a divine fire that consumes the whole man, the Sheikh gives as the Imam's, from *Lantern of the Path* (L12 ¶38; L11 ¶51), and Chapter Eleven quoted it (recalled in words, not re-quoted). *(Dropped: the dating "spoken long before Rabiʿa" from Imam ʿAli's death date. The Lantern gives the saying to "the Commander of the Faithful" (p. 88), the Sheikh to Imam al-Sadiq (L12 ¶38), the book's authorship is itself contested (step 1a), and Rabiʿa's dates overlap Imam al-Sadiq's; the inference was the book's own and did not hold.)*
4. *Told story (prose, D47 #43), the proof by example.* Shaqiq al-Balkhi, Ibrahim's pupil [`light` p. 24], a master of renunciation, set out for hajj in 149 AH, the first year after Imam al-Sadiq's death (L12 ¶31). At al-Qadisiyya he saw a handsome young man in Sufi wool, sitting apart, and took him for one of the idlers who live off the caravan (L12 ¶30–31). He went to rebuke him; before he spoke, the young man said, "Shaqiq," and told him to avoid much suspicion, for some suspicions are sins (L12 ¶32, "keep away from lots of suspicions because some suspicions are seen"). **Not a verse block.** The Sheikh gives the words without naming a verse; that they are the Qur'an's (Q 49:12, Qaraʾi: "Avoid much suspicion; indeed some suspicions are sins.") is the book's identification, resting on the exact match of wording (`reviews/story-sources.md` #43 finds the verse in the classical versions, but those are not held, so they are not cited). Print the words inline in the Qaraʾi wording with the reference in brackets, "the Qur'an's own words (49:12)", so the reader sees the identification is ours and the Arabic is not set. *(The words raise: how did he know?)* At the next stop, Waqisa, Shaqiq found him praying, shaking and in tears, and came to apologise; the young man finished and recited, verse block Q 20:82, "Indeed I forgive those who repent, become faithful, act righteously, and follow guidance." (checked, Qaraʾi; L12 ¶33). *(Verse answers Shaqiq's unspoken apology.)* Twice he had answered what was in Shaqiq's heart (L12 ¶34). The well episode is trimmed (outline). In Mecca Shaqiq saw him by the spout of the Kaʿba, weeping and praying until dawn; after the dawn prayer and the tawaf he followed him to a great house full of relatives, slaves, money and property (the Sheikh's own list, ¶36; keep "slaves", do not soften to "servants"), where people crowded to take his blessing; it was Musa ibn Jaʿfar, Imam al-Kazim (as) (L12 ¶35–37). The Sheikh notes the story is reported by Ibn al-Jawzi, a Sunni scholar (L12 ¶30); `light` p. 24 confirms that Shaqiq met the Imam and told of his station (cite).
5. *What does the story show?* The Sheikh: the mystics were "completely different in terms of their calibre of understanding and how they behave" from the Imams (L12 ¶37) (checked). One sentence, his; the story has already shown it (a master of renunciation judged by the wool and was answered from the Qur'an twice). *(Dropped: the book's reading that the Imam in the rich house "lived Ibrahim's third level". It is the book's theology, not the Sheikh's or a held source's; if anything the Sheikh's own parallel is Imam al-Sadiq's hidden coarse garment under the fine one, L12 ¶13–14, the "conceal your zuhd" saying, which is Sufyan's story, screened to Ch 16. Leave the reader to see it.)*
6. Therefore: the first seekers learned from the Imams, directly or through Imam ʿAli's companions, and the Imams stood above them.

## §3 Not brought in from outside
**Point: So the road was not brought in from outside; at most, some techniques were borrowed, and the later theory drew on outside ideas.**
1. *Then why do Western books look elsewhere?* Because, the Sheikh says, they "cannot believe that Islam as a religion of law and jurisprudence can produce mystics" (L11 ¶3) (checked). *(Callback: Chapter Six's law and path are one road; the premise is the opposite of the book's.)* Their candidates: Christian monks, Neoplatonism, and Buddhism (L11 ¶4, ¶12, ¶21; `light` p. 16 adds a Persian reaction against the Arabs, which the lectures do not take up; leave it out). *(Reader told: three candidates, each answered in a paragraph.)*
   - a. *Monks.* The claim rests on the Sufis' coarse wool, "Sufi" coming from *suf*, wool [`light` p. 22; L11 ¶5]. The Qur'an, which §1 showed is the road's source, had already named the monks' path as their own invention: verse block Q 57:27, "But as for monasticism, they innovated it—We had not prescribed it for them—only seeking Allah's pleasure." (checked, Qaraʾi). *(Verse answers: a road grown from this book had no warrant to copy monasticism, so the wool proves nothing.)* In the Sheikh's reading they wanted more than ordinary worship, left the world and made a method God had not written for them; he keeps the verse's balance, that those who did it in faith had their reward (¶11, with the verse's own clause), and then: "I'm telling you, this is not the way" (L11 ¶10–11) (checked). Keep that balance in the prose (TONE 47, both sides; Tabatabaʾi reads the verse more kindly, `light` p. 5, left for Ch 15). Celibacy, honoured in Christian monasticism, did not go well with the practice of Muslim mystics (L11 ¶6; worded to be true on either reading of the transcript's "several words"), and their lodges are "completely different" from monasteries, so the claim is "very difficult to prove" (L11 ¶11–12; quote only "very difficult to prove", checked, ¶12).
   - b. *Neoplatonism.* It entered late, when the mystics set out to systematise theoretical ʿirfan; the seekers of the second, third and fourth centuries did not have it (L11 ¶20–21). Mutahhari records a third view, that ʿirfan took its material from Islam and, in giving it structure, was influenced by outside currents, especially philosophy; how far, he says, needs separate study [`light` p. 16, paraphrased]. He does not endorse the influence; he is certain only of the source (step 2).
   - c. *Buddhism.* The strongest case: many early renunciants came from Khurasan, which bordered India and China, and Ibrahim ibn Adham, the prince who left his palace, is often compared with the Buddha (L11 ¶21; L12 ¶11; `light` p. 24). Yet the concepts differ too much for the doctrine to have come from there (L11 ¶22; the nirvana–fanaʾ difference is Ch 12's, recalled in a clause); the attitude of renunciation is in the Qur'an (L11 ¶74); and the prince of §2 learned from Imam al-Sadiq (as). At most some techniques were borrowed, not the doctrine, which is also Julian Baldick's view as the Sheikh reports it (L11 ¶22–23; the transcript's "Julian Balwick" is Baldick: the Sheikh names "Julian Baldick… a book Mystical Islam… King's College" in L07 ¶51, so the name is his, not the book's guess). *(Worded around, per the outline: the Sheikh's list of borrowed techniques (¶22: seclusion, not speaking, long meditation, "not found in our hadith") clashes with Ch 10, which roots silence and seclusion in the Qur'an and hadith. Say only "some techniques, not the doctrine".)*
2. *So what is certain?* Mutahhari: "But that which is certain is that 'irfan has derived its basic sources of inspiration from Islam itself and from nowhere else." [`light` p. 16] (checked, p. 16). *(Quote answers.)*
3. *Did Western scholarship come round?* Its best did: R. A. Nicholson and Louis Massignon "have expressly admitted that the principal sources of 'irfan are the Quran and the Prophet's Sunnah" [`light` pp. 20–21] (checked, runs across the page break), and the Sheikh says Schimmel too accepts that it came from the Qur'an and hadith (L11 ¶4). After setting out the Qur'an's verses of light, nearness and the Face, Nicholson writes: "Surely the seeds of mysticism are here." [`light` p. 21] (checked, p. 21). *(Quote answers; Nicholson's sentence on the Prophet's statements being "formally inconsistent", same page, is not quoted: theological care, TONE 33.)*
4. *Return to the opener (told in words).* George Jurdaq, a Lebanese Christian, answered the orientalists over Abu Dharr: they are like people who see a man standing by a sea with a full pitcher and go searching for the pond he filled it from [`light` p. 20, paraphrased]. Mutahhari draws the same line for ʿirfan: "The orientalists are in search of a non-Islamic source of inspiration of 'irfan, while they completely overlook the great ocean of Islam." [`light` p. 20] (checked, p. 20).
5. Therefore (section and chapter): the road began in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams (as); its first seekers learned from the Imams and stood below them; nothing from outside gave it more than some techniques and, later, ideas its theorists drew on.

## Try this
Self-accounting (*muhasaba*), the practice al-Harith al-Muhasibi was named for and that the Sheikh calls "still persistent in our mysticism" (L12 ¶4, checked). ## Where scholars differ (D20, side by side, gently)
- Hasan al-Basri: the history of ʿirfan begins with him and orders trace through him to Imam ʿAli [`light` p. 23; listed among Imam ʿAli's companions, p. 5]; the Sheikh calls him ambivalent (L11 ¶64). In the §2 box.
