**Title:** Drunk and sober

**Chapter point:** Of those who reached annihilation, some stayed lost in it and others came back sober, and the sober went further; the theory of what they saw, Ibn ʿArabi's unity of being, is contested, but it is neither pantheism nor licence.
*(Book chain line 14, sharpened: "the end" becomes "annihilation" so it joins Ch 12; "the great theory" is named; "contested" is added because §3 must leave one question, Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties, open.)*

**Section chain:**
1. Of those who reached annihilation, the drunk stayed lost in it and spoke of it, while the sober came back, kept its secret, and went further.
2. Ibn ʿArabi gave what they saw a theory, the unity of being: there is one existence, and creation is its real manifestation, neither God nor apart from Him, known by experience rather than argument.

## Opening (two paragraphs, ~200 words)
1. *Hook (familiar ground: a duʿa in Shaʿban).* In the nights of Shaʿban the Munajat asks God to make us among those who answer when He calls, and at whom He glances so that they swoon before His Majesty (Munajat §109–110, the Part Four divider line, recalled in words, not re-quoted). Some travellers did reach that swoon, and Chapter Twelve followed one into annihilation and back, keeping to principles rather than names. What came after the swoon divided the travellers who really reached it. *(Callback: Ch 12, one sentence.)*
2. *Hand-in: who were they, and what does "drunk and sober" mean?* The mystics borrowed two words for the two outcomes: intoxication (*sukr*), losing oneself in God until nothing else is seen, and sobriety (*sahw*), coming back to oneself (L13 ¶13–14; Ch 12 glossed both, so one clause each). The Sheikh's caveat on the image: the forgetting is "for Allah" ("but of course, here for Allah", L13 ¶14, checked). Reader told what is coming, in the title's words: who stayed drunk and who came back sober; the theory the greatest of their heirs gave to what they saw; and the concerns of the scholars who have been wary of it all, with the answers given to them (the prologue's own wording, `00-prologue.md` "What you can expect").

## §1 Lost in God, or back with God
**Point: Of those who reached annihilation, the drunk stayed lost in it and spoke of it, while the sober came back, kept its secret, and went further.**

1. *Who reached annihilation first, and how did he describe it?* Bayazid of Bistam (d. 261/874 or 264/877), "it is said", was the first to speak openly of annihilation in God and of subsistence through Him (`light` p. 25, paraphrased; L13 ¶16). His own image of the self he left: "I came forth from Bayazid-ness as a snake from its skin." (`light` p. 25, checked). *(Quote answers: the self he shed is the false self Ch 12 described, recalled in a clause.)*
2. *How did he get there?* **Told story (D47, #46), prose, as the Sheikh's account (L13 ¶16–18).** Asked what renunciation (*zuhd*) is, he said he had been three days in it and on the fourth he was done: the first day he renounced this world, the second the next world, the third everything but God. On the fourth day "nothing was left to me but God" (L13 ¶18, checked). A voice said, "you are not strong enough to endure with me alone"; he answered, "that is exactly what I want"; the voice said, "you have found" (L13 ¶18, checked; the transcript repeats "you have found"). The Sheikh's gloss: renouncing the next world means "not desiring anything but Allah" (L13 ¶17, checked, "they are not desiring anything but Allah"), a loneliness "not everyone can" endure (L13 ¶18; transcript spells "indure", so paraphrase).
   - a. Side by side, one sentence: *Kernel of the Kernels* records the fourth day differently: he was asked "What do you want?" and answered, "I want not to want anything!" (`kernel` p. 73, checked), and places the saying at the point Ch 11 reached, where the traveller "tries to forget love and the beloved" (`kernel` p. 73, checked). *(Callback: Ch 11, "even love left at the door", in words. Both versions end in wanting nothing but God, so they agree on the point.)*
3. *What happens when a man so emptied speaks?* In that intoxication some spoke ecstatic sayings (*shath*), words that make sense only to the one in the state; the Sheikh calls them "not right… unhealthy" in themselves, though they come from ecstasy (L13 ¶19, paraphrased: the transcript's "theo pathetic" wording is garbled). **Told story (D47, #47), prose, part 1.** Bayazid's most famous: "Praise is to me, how great my majesty is" (L13 ¶19, checked). *(Quote raises the worry, in the Sheikh's own words: "what this means is that I am God, isn't it?", L13 ¶19, paraphrased as the reader's question.)*
4. *Can a believer say that and remain a believer?* **#47, part 2.** The Sheikh gives two readings; reader told "two readings" first.
   - a. God spoke from his tongue as He spoke to Musa from the tree. Verse block (answers the worry): Q 28:30 `words="11-"` "from the tree: 'Moses! Indeed I am Allah, the Lord of all the worlds!'" (Qaraʾi, `quran.py 28:30`, checked; Arabic held). Next sentence follows from the verse: "the tree was not Allah. Allah was speaking from the tree" (L13 ¶20, checked); this reading, in the Sheikh's words, "exonerates these Sufis" (L13 ¶20, checked).
   - b. Many who had the same experiences preferred another: he could not see himself, only God, and spoke when God's glory had removed him from his own sight: "there is no you anymore" (L13 ¶21, checked; ¶21's "loves himself" is not used, D39).
   - c. On either reading no man became God: the tree was not God, and what went from sight was a self (Ch 12 §1, recalled in a clause: the soul remains and God remains God). Later Sufis rejected any reading that makes the lover and the Beloved one (L13 ¶23, paraphrased).
5. *Were all who spoke like this excused?* No. Hallaj spoke openly; the jurists pronounced him an apostate and he was executed (L13 ¶15; `light` p. 27: "The jurisprudents pronounced him an apostate", checked; executed 309/913). The Sheikh calls his statements "very unacceptable" (L13 ¶15, checked). The ʿurafaʾ themselves blamed him for one thing: "The 'urafa' themselves accuse him of disclosing spiritual secrets." (`light` p. 27, checked), and Hafiz: "He said, that friend, who was raised high on the cross, / His crime was that he used to reveal secrets." (`light` p. 27, checked; Persian n.a.). *(Verse raises the next question: what secret, and why keep it? Side by side, one clause: the ʿurafaʾ remember him as a martyr, `light` p. 27, "Al-Hallaj is remembered by the 'urafa' as a martyr.", checked.)*
6. *Why should something true be kept secret?* Because those who have not seen cannot bear it: "If they reveal their experiences ordinary people cannot tolerate it." (L13 ¶30, checked). Even among the companions, "Salman enjoyed a degree of faith that Abu Dharr could not have withstood" (`light` p. 23, checked), and Mutahhari gives the tradition: "If Abu Dharr knew what was in Salman's heart, he would (considering him a heretic) have killed him." (`light` p. 23, checked; quoted inline, not displayed, so no Arabic; if displayed, Arabic [VERIFY]). *(Quote answers. Callback: Abu Dharr, whose lonely death opened Ch 13, in a clause.)* So the sober spoke in allusion (*ishara*), a language outsiders could not follow, which "became a custom" among the early mystics (L13 ¶30, paraphrased).
7. *Who kept the secret, and how did he differ?* Junayd of Baghdad (d. 298/910, `light` p. 27), who outlived Bayazid by more than thirty years (from the dates on `light` pp. 25, 27), pupil and nephew of Sari al-Saqati and pupil of al-Muhasibi (`light` pp. 26–27; L13 ¶27–28). The ʿurafaʾ call him Sayyid al-Taʾifa, as "the Shi'ah jurisprudents call al-Shaykh al-Tusi Shaykh al-Ta'ifah" (`light` p. 26, checked). Mutahhari: "Junayd is counted as one of the moderate mystics. The kind of ecstatic ejaculations uttered by others were never heard from his lips." (`light` p. 27, checked); he "dressed like scholars and jurisprudents" (`light` p. 27, checked). He criticised Hallaj for speaking openly (L13 ¶30, ¶32).
8. *Why did the tradition rank him higher?* For Junayd, intoxication is a passing experience at the first steps of the road (L13 ¶14–15, paraphrased); annihilation is not the goal, and remaining (*baqaʾ*) is "a new life in God" (L13 ¶29, checked), in which "man becomes more aware of himself in the life in God" (L13 ¶28, checked; the garbled tail of ¶28 is cut). **The Sheikh's image (L13 ¶29):** explorers who reach a river after thousands of kilometres think it the end of the world; better-equipped travellers cross it and find "many, many other things" beyond. Bayazid stopped at the river; Junayd was "probably" the first to cross (L13 ¶29, "Junayd was the first one probably", checked). *(Callback: this is the sobriety Ch 12 found "loftier" than effacement, in words; `light` p. 57 not re-quoted.)*
9. *What does the sober traveller do with what he saw?* He keeps it in allusion and goes on praying. Sayyid Haydar Amuli records Junayd: "Uncertainties have vanished and all signs have disappeared; the only thing of benefit to us is the prayer we perform in the depth of the night." (`inner_secrets` p. 162, checked). *(Quote answers: sobriety ends in prayer, which §3 step 4 will need.)*
10. Therefore: of those who reached annihilation, the drunk stayed lost in it and spoke; the sober came back, kept its secret in allusion, kept the night prayer, and went further.
- Hand-on question (worked into the last sentence or the next section's first): they came back with something seen; could it be put into words?

## §2 A theory of what they saw
**Point: Ibn ʿArabi gave what they saw a theory, the unity of being: there is one existence, and creation is its real manifestation, neither God nor apart from Him, known by experience rather than argument.**

1. *Who put it into words?* Of the early leanings Ch 13 traced (fear, renunciation, love), a fourth put knowledge (*maʿrifa*) first, and its line of theory ran from al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi to Ibn ʿArabi (L13 ¶3, ¶12; Tirmidhi in the §3 box). Ibn ʿArabi (d. 638/1240, Damascus, `light` p. 35, checked), whom Mutahhari calls "certainly the greatest mystic of Islam" (`light` p. 34, checked; the source reads "is certainly the greatest mystic of Islam"); "the mystics who came after him ate the crumbs from his table" (`light` p. 35, checked). For Shia and Sunni mystics alike he is the Greatest Shaykh (L13 ¶33). *(Callback: his childhood vision of Surah Yasin, told in Ch 5, in a clause.)*
2. *What did he say they saw?* The unity of being (*wahdat al-wujud*): there is only one existence, and all existing things are its manifestations, states and specifications (L13 ¶60, paraphrased; the transcript's wording is garbled). Others had alluded to it; he made it a theory (L13 ¶55). Reader told: the theory is clearest set beside the others (box).
   - **Word Study box: four views of existence** (L13 ¶56–62). (1) The Peripatetics: many existences, each thing with its own, God's beside them. (2) The Illuminationists: one existence, God's; other beings are essences that take existence from it. (3) Mulla Sadra and the old Persian sages: one existence in grades, like sunlight and a small lamp, "all of them are light" (¶59, checked: "but all of them are light"). (4) Ibn ʿArabi: one existence, and everything else its manifestation. Sadra came closest to reasoning it out, through gradation (*tashkik*) (¶62). *(Callback: Ch 2's box named the first two schools; one clause.)*
3. *If there is one existence, where is creation?* The Sheikh's own question: when God created, is creation outside Him, a new existence beside Him, or a manifestation of Him (L13 ¶60–61)? His answer: "you cannot say that there's anything besides god" (L13 ¶61, paraphrase: the transcript reads "You cannot say that there's anything besides god", checked). Verse block (answers): Q 57:3 `words="1-5"` "He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden" (Qaraʾi, `quran.py 57:3`, checked; Arabic held). Next sentence follows from the verse: "You see His manifestation but he's not him." (L13 ¶65, checked).
4. *Is this a new teaching, or was it there before?* Imam ʿAli (as) said it in the oration Ch 2 quoted for "soaring thoughts": "He is with all things but not by association. He is other than all things but not by detachment." (`nahj` 1.1.1, p. 122, checked; Arabic [VERIFY]: the extract's p. 121 Arabic is garbled). The verse and the oration held it as statements; Ibn ʿArabi made them a theory, and after him readers saw it there (L13 ¶65–66, paraphrased). *(Quote answers; the Sheikh's own loose wording of the saying, ¶64, is replaced by the held text.)*
5. *Then am I God, or am I nothing?* Neither; reader told "neither" first.
   - a. Not God: "certainly I'm not god or a part of god" (L13 ¶63, checked; curly apostrophe in the transcript); God gave existence to beings whose limits He planned, so "I have gained existence from him" (L13 ¶64, checked). *(Ch 12 tasted this in "no power except through God"; recalled in a clause only, not re-explained.)*
   - b. Not nothing: "It is not illusion. It is more real than you think." (L13 ¶66, checked).
   - c. Khomeini's image holds both: "the waves of the sea are not separate from the sea itself, but still the sea is not the waves, although the waves are the sea." (`light` p. 144, checked; Part 3 of *Light* is Ayatullah Khomeini's), with his own caution: "even this similitude is not perfect, for no similitude can properly illustrate the relation between Allah and His creation." (`light` p. 144, checked). *(Theological care, TONE 33: the image is offered as imperfect.)*
6. *Why can't it simply be proved?* It is "above the rank of reason": "you can never rationalise this theory" (L13 ¶67, checked); the mind "is not set to understand", only "hard experience" (L13 ¶66, checked). *(Callback: Ch 2, knowing about God is not knowing Him; one clause.)*
7. *Then who can check it?* **Told story (D47, #53), prose, the Sheikh's account (L13 ¶42–43).** He read a book of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's on the names of God and was amazed at the analysis; then, reading Ibn ʿArabi's *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*, he found it all there. Tabatabaʾi never claimed it as his own: he loved Ibn ʿArabi and, as Ayatullah Hasanzadeh Amuli reports, said he had learned it from him; his own experiences "matched very much" Ibn ʿArabi's (L13 ¶43, checked). The Sheikh's conclusion: "it's not for us to judge because it needs someone who has the experience" (L13 ¶43, checked). The book is not named (the Sheikh does not name it). *(Callback: Tabatabaʾi's line of teachers, Ch 7, in a clause.)*
8. *Why do Shia masters still use a Sunni mystic's language?* His theory matched many mystics' experiences, and they "found no better language" for them (L13 ¶50, checked: "they found no better language than the language of Ibne Arabi"); his influence runs through Mulla Sadra, Ayatullah Khomeini and the Shia masters of Iran today (L13 ¶45–47, ¶51). *(This step hands to §3: the reliance itself drew critics.)*
9. Therefore: Ibn ʿArabi gave what the sober saw a theory: one existence, creation its real manifestation, neither God nor apart from Him, known by those who have the experience rather than by argument.

## §3 The critics' concerns, and the answers
**Point: The critics feared a path outside Islam, a God merged with the world, and the law set aside; the masters' own words and lives answer each fear, and only Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties stay open.**
*(Fulfils the prologue: "Chapter Fourteen sets out their wider concerns and the answers given to them." Scoped per FRAMEWORK §9 (D41): the concerns as the held defenders report them; no critical source is held.)*

1. *Who has been wary of all this, and why?* Murtada Mutahhari sorts the critics. One group of traditionists and jurists held that the ʿurafaʾ "are not practically bound to Islam", and that their citing of the Qur'an and Sunna "are merely a ruse to deceive the simple-minded people" (`light` p. 13, both checked); another, modernist group welcomed ʿirfan as a flight from Islam and its law (`light` p. 13, paraphrased; its "brought from outside" claim belongs to Ch 13, recalled in a clause). *(Callback: Ch 1's jurists who hold the obligations are the whole religion, and the prologue's fear that mysticism sets the law aside; one clause each.)* The concerns come down to three: sincerity, God and the world, and the law, with a fourth that a Shia reader would raise about Ibn ʿArabi himself (counts said naturally, TONE 46).
2. *Were the ʿurafaʾ sincere?* Mutahhari takes a third, neutral view: they may have made mistakes and innovations, more in practice than in theory, as theologians, philosophers and jurists have; "It is quite possible that they have made mistakes" (`light` p. 14, checked), but never from bad faith: "I do not believe that the 'urafa' have had evil intentions towards Islam." (`light` p. 14, checked). He asks for careful study by those who know both ʿirfan and Islam's teachings (`light` p. 14, paraphrased).
3. *Does the unity of being make the world God, or put God inside a man?* §2 has answered the first half: creation is not God or a part of Him. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's teaching, as the *Kernel* records it, answers the second: the traveller's imagining that God has united with him is "hulul and ittihad… which are kufr and shirk", and of the unity of being: "And how far is this from the notions of hulul and ittihad." (`kernel` p. 84, both checked; the quotation stops before "imaginary", see Where scholars differ). Amuli: the gnostics are "far removed from such false assumptions and lying fantasies" as "belief in incarnation, union with the divine and the ascription of human characteristics to Allah" (`inner_secrets` p. 163, both checked). *(Ch 12 quoted the same Tabatabaʾi warning from `light` p. 102; recalled in a clause; the new point here is the distinction from the unity of being.)*
   - a. Khomeini's rebuke to accusers (`forty_hadith` p. 345): God may ask, of one who never studied the unity of being, "Then why did you blindly accuse them of unbelief and insult them?" (checked). Context in words: he saw Mulla Sadra called a heretic and "a Sufi through and through", though Sadra wrote a book refuting the Sufis (`forty_hadith` p. 402, the quoted phrase checked). Even-handed: Khomeini puts the same question to a mystic who belittles the jurists, since "None of the scholars of any one of these sciences has a right to find fault with the others." (`forty_hadith` p. 345, checked; the question to the mystic runs on to p. 346). *(D20: both sides, gently; keeps faith with the prologue's respect for the jurists and the marjaʿ.)*
4. *Did the ecstatics set the law aside? (the prologue's chief fear)* The tradition judged its own ecstatics: the ʿurafaʾ hold that Bayazid "uttered these words when he was beside himself in ecstasy" (`light` p. 25, checked), and they absolve Hallaj and Bayazid on the same ground (`light` p. 27, checked: "the statements of al-Hallaj and Bayazid that gave the impression of unbelief were made when they were beside themselves in the state of 'intoxication'"). Words spoken out of one's mind are no rule for a sober believer (recalls §1 and Ch 12's "unacceptable" claim that duties are lifted, in a clause). The masters lived by the law: Junayd's night prayer (§1 step 9, recalled), and Ibn ʿArabi "would pray two cycles of prayer for every prophet and saint, after performing all the obligatory cycles of prayers" (`inner_secrets` p. 162, checked). *(Callback: Ch 6's "to the last breath", one clause; its quotations are not repeated.)*
5. *But was Ibn ʿArabi one of us?* The Sheikh: "It is difficult to say that he was a Shia"; he respected the Imams (as) and also the first two caliphs (L13 ¶47, paraphrased), and some of his passages and visions go against Shia belief (L13 ¶48–49, ¶52, generalised; the Rajabiyyun passage and the vision of the caliphs are not described). Friends called him the Perfect Saint, enemies "the Killer of the Faith" (`light` p. 35, checked: "Mumit al-Din (the Killer of the Faith)"). Reader told: the Sheikh answers in three ways.
   - a. Experience cannot settle which school is right: "This Shia Sunni business should only be solved by rational arguments rather than by experiences." (L13 ¶48, checked).
   - b. Visions take the shape of the theology a person already holds (L13 ¶53, paraphrased).
   - c. "you cannot 100% accept someone or reject" (L13 ¶52, checked); "Certainly he had something valuable in what he wrote." (L13 ¶53, checked).
   - d. Side by side (D20), one paragraph: the chain of his order runs, "Through one intermediary link", to ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Gilani (`light` p. 34, checked); Amuli reads Ibn ʿArabi's own words on the Seal of the Saints as pointing to Imam ʿAli (as) (`inner_secrets` p. 99, paraphrased; the extract reads "the absolute Seal of the Saints, is the Commander of the Faithful", checked); the Sheikh, weighing a book that argues he was Shia against one that argues he was a Sunni, finds the Sunni case "much much more powerful", because "we shouldn't be unjust" (L13 ¶54–55, both checked). So a Shia reader takes the theory, which the masters tested by experience, without taking its author's loyalties.
   - **Who's Who box: al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi and the Seal of the Saints** (L13 ¶4–12; `inner_secrets` pp. 98–99). A third-century mystic-philosopher, not the hadith compiler (¶4); as there is a Seal of the Prophets, he spoke of a Seal of the Saints (¶5), with a hierarchy of saints who keep the world in order (¶6–8, no numbers). The Sheikh: the same ideas Shia hold of the Imams, taken "to very extreme type of concept which is not even acceptable by Shia" (¶9, checked: "not even acceptable by Shia"). Ibn ʿArabi took up the Seal at length; side by side: the Sheikh reads him as meaning himself, adding "We cannot judge what he meant by this" (¶5–6, checked), whereas Amuli reads the absolute Seal as Imam ʿAli (as) (`inner_secrets` p. 99). *(Ties to step 5's question about his loyalties; the pole, *qutb*, is left to Ch 15.)*
6. Therefore (section and chapter): the critics' fears have answers in the masters' own words and lives: the ʿurafaʾ were sincere though fallible; the unity of being is neither pantheism nor God dwelling in a man; ecstatic words gave no licence, and the sober kept the law; and Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties stay open, so his theory is taken without them. With §1 and §2: of those who reached annihilation the sober went furthest, and the theory of what they saw, though contested, is neither pantheism nor licence.
- **Hand-off (from §3 step 5d):** Ibn ʿArabi's own chain ran through an order founded on ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Gilani; by his time the road was walked in orders, each with its chain of shaykhs. "How did the road become orders, and where do Shia stand?" (FRAMEWORK §3).

## Where scholars differ (D20, side by side, gently)
- Hallaj: the jurists pronounced him an apostate; the ʿurafaʾ remember him as a martyr and blame him only for revealing secrets (`light` p. 27); the Sheikh calls his statements "very unacceptable" (L13 ¶15). §1 step 5.
- Bayazid's fourth day: the Sheikh's voice ("you are not strong enough…") and the *Kernel*'s question ("What do you want?"). Both end in wanting only God. §1 step 2a.
- Bayazid and remaining: Mutahhari says Bayazid was first to speak of both annihilation and subsistence (`light` p. 25); the Sheikh says he stopped at intoxication (L13 ¶26, ¶28–29). Worded as preference: Bayazid preferred intoxication because it "obliterates the human attributes" (¶28), Junayd held the sober state higher; "stopped at the river" stays the Sheikh's image.
- Creation's reality: the Sheikh, "not illusion… more real than you think" (L13 ¶66); Tabatabaʾi calls every existence beside God's "a mere shadow" in comparison with His (`light` p. 103; `kernel` p. 84, "imaginary… shadows"); Khomeini: the waves "do not exist independently" (`light` p. 144). All deny that anything stands on its own beside God; they differ in emphasis. Prose uses Khomeini's image and the Sheikh's line; the *Kernel* is quoted only on hulul and ittihad, so the difference does not have to be argued in the body.
