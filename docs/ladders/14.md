**Title:** Drunk and sober

**Chapter point:** Of those who reached annihilation, some stayed lost in it and others came back sober, and the sober went further; the theory of what they saw, Ibn ʿArabi's unity of being, is contested, but it is neither pantheism nor licence.
*(Book chain line 14, sharpened: "the end" becomes "annihilation" so it joins Ch 12, where annihilation is a beginning, not the end; "the great theory" is named; "contested" is added because §3 must leave one question, Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties, open.)*

**Section chain:**
1. Of those who reached annihilation, the drunk stayed lost in it and spoke of it, while the sober came back, kept its secret, and went further.
2. Ibn ʿArabi gave what they saw a theory, the unity of being: there is one existence, and creation is its real manifestation, neither God nor apart from Him, known by experience rather than argument.

## Opening (two paragraphs, ~200 words)
1. *Hook (familiar ground: a duʿa in Shaʿban).* In the nights of Shaʿban the Munajat asks God to make us among those who answer when He calls, and at whom He glances so that they swoon before His Majesty (Munajat §109–110, the Part Four divider line, recalled in words, not re-quoted). Some travellers did reach that swoon, and Chapter Twelve followed one into annihilation and back, keeping to principles rather than names. What came after the swoon divided the travellers who really reached it. *(Callback: Ch 12, one sentence.)*
2. *Hand-in: who were they, and what does "drunk and sober" mean?* The mystics borrowed two words for the two outcomes: intoxication (*sukr*), losing oneself in God until nothing else is seen, and sobriety (*sahw*), coming back to oneself (L13 ¶13–14; Ch 12 glossed both, so one clause each). The Sheikh's caveat on the image: the forgetting is "for Allah" ("but of course, here for Allah", L13 ¶14, checked). Reader told what is coming, in the title's words: who stayed drunk and who came back sober; the theory the greatest of their heirs gave to what they saw; and the concerns of the scholars who have been wary of it all, with the answers given to them (the prologue's own wording, `00-prologue.md` "What you can expect").

## §1 Lost in God, or back with God
**Point: Of those who reached annihilation, the drunk stayed lost in it and spoke of it, while the sober came back, kept its secret, and went further.**

1. *Who reached annihilation first, and how did he describe it?* Bayazid of Bistam (d. 261/874 or 264/877), "it is said", was the first to speak openly of annihilation in God (`light` p. 25, paraphrased; L13 ¶16). His own image of the self he left: "I came forth from Bayazid-ness as a snake from its skin." (`light` p. 25, checked). *(Quote answers: the self he shed is the false self Ch 12 described, recalled in a clause.)* *(Mutahhari's sentence credits him with "subsistence through God" as well; the prose does not say he never spoke of remaining. It says, with the Sheikh, that he preferred intoxication (step 8). See Where scholars differ.)*
2. *How did he get there?* One short paragraph, not a told story (Fable review, change 1). Asked what renunciation (*zuhd*) is (L13 ¶17), he answered with his days, given here in the held version: "I renounced the world on the first day, and the Hereafter on the second; the third day I renounced everything other than God, and on the fourth I was asked, 'What do you want?' I said, 'I want not to want anything!'" (`kernel` p. 73, checked; the Arabic of the question and answer is on the same page but garbled in the extract, so it is `[VERIFY]` unless the printed page is checked). The Sheikh's gloss: renouncing the next world means "not desiring anything but Allah" (L13 ¶17, checked, "they are not desiring anything but Allah"). *Kernel of the Kernels* places the saying at the point Chapter Eleven reached, where the traveller "tries to forget love and the beloved" (`kernel` p. 73, checked). *(Callback: Ch 11, "even love left at the door", in words. The Sheikh's fuller version with the voice, "you are not strong enough to endure with me alone", L13 ¶18, is not told: it is in no held source, and the held version makes the same point in two lines.)*
3. *What happens when a man so emptied speaks?* In that intoxication some lost control of their tongues and spoke ecstatic sayings (*shath*; Ch 12 defined them, so one clause). The Sheikh's judgement, paraphrased (the transcript's wording is garbled, "theo pathetic"): as statements about God they are not right in themselves, though they come out of ecstasy (L13 ¶19). The ʿurafaʾ say the same of Bayazid: "he uttered these words when he was beside himself in ecstasy" (`light` p. 25, checked; moved here from §3). **Told story (D47, #47), prose, part 1.** Bayazid's most famous: "Praise is to me, how great my majesty is" (L13 ¶19, checked). *(Quote raises the worry, in the Sheikh's own words: "what this means is that I am God, isn't it?", L13 ¶19, paraphrased as the reader's question.)*
4. *Can a believer say that and remain a believer?* **#47, part 2.** The Sheikh gives two readings; reader told "two readings" first, and neither is ranked (the transcript calls each "the best" in turn, ¶20 and ¶22, so the prose does not choose, D39).
   - a. God spoke from his tongue as He spoke to Musa from the tree. Verse block (answers the worry): Q 28:30 `words="11-"` "from the tree: 'Moses! Indeed I am Allah, the Lord of all the worlds!'" (Qaraʾi, `quran.py 28:30`, checked; Arabic held; words 11–19 of the verse are "من الشجرة أن يا موسى إني أنا الله رب العالمين", verified against the Arabic). Next sentence follows from the verse: "the tree was not Allah. Allah was speaking from the tree" (L13 ¶20, checked); this reading, in the Sheikh's words, "exonerates these Sufis" (L13 ¶20, checked).
   - b. Many who had the same experiences preferred another: he could not see himself, only God, and spoke when God's glory had removed him from his own sight: "there is no you anymore" (L13 ¶21, checked; ¶21's "loves himself" and "saw himself unified with God" are not used, D39 and ¶23).
   - c. On either reading no man became God: the tree was not God, and what went from sight was a self (Ch 12 §1, recalled in a clause: the soul remains and God remains God). Later Sufis rejected any reading that makes the lover and the Beloved one (L13 ¶23, paraphrased).
5. *Were all who spoke like this excused?* Not by the jurists. Hallaj spoke openly; "The jurisprudents pronounced him an apostate" (`light` p. 27, checked) and he was executed in 309/913 (`light` p. 27; L13 ¶15). The Sheikh calls his statements "very unacceptable" (L13 ¶15, checked). The ʿurafaʾ excused the words, as they excused Bayazid's, and blamed him for one thing only: "The 'urafa' themselves accuse him of disclosing spiritual secrets." (`light` p. 27, checked), and Hafiz: "He said, that friend, who was raised high on the cross, / His crime was that he used to reveal secrets." (`light` p. 27, checked; Persian n.a.). *(Verse raises the next question: what secret, and why keep it? Side by side, one clause: the ʿurafaʾ remember him as a martyr, `light` p. 27, "Al-Hallaj is remembered by the 'urafa' as a martyr.", checked.)*
6. *Why should something true be kept secret?* Because those who have not seen cannot bear it: "If they reveal their experiences ordinary people cannot tolerate it." (L13 ¶30, checked). Even among the companions, "Salman enjoyed a degree of faith that Abu Dharr could not have withstood" (`light` p. 23, checked), and the tradition the Sheikh and Mutahhari both give is told in words: had Abu Dharr known what was in Salman's heart, he would have taken him for an unbeliever (L13 ¶30; `light` p. 23, whose wording runs on to "killed him" with a parenthesis inside the sentence, so it is paraphrased rather than quoted). *(Answers. Callback: Abu Dharr, whose lonely death opened Ch 13, in a clause.)* So the sober spoke in allusion (*ishara*), a language outsiders could not follow, which "became a custom" among the early mystics (L13 ¶30, paraphrased; Mutahhari credits Dhu'l-Nun with beginning it, `light` p. 27, so the prose does not say who was first).
7. *Who kept the secret, and how did he differ?* Junayd of Baghdad (d. 298/910, `light` p. 27), who outlived Bayazid by more than thirty years (from the dates on `light` pp. 25, 27), pupil and nephew of Sari al-Saqati and pupil of al-Muhasibi (`light` pp. 26–27; L13 ¶27–28). The ʿurafaʾ call him Sayyid al-Taʾifa, as "the Shi'ah jurisprudents call al-Shaykh al-Tusi Shaykh al-Ta'ifah" (`light` p. 26, checked). Mutahhari: "Junayd is counted as one of the moderate mystics. The kind of ecstatic ejaculations uttered by others were never heard from his lips." (`light` p. 27, checked); he "dressed like scholars and jurisprudents" (`light` p. 27, checked). He criticised Hallaj for speaking openly (L13 ¶30, ¶32).
8. *Why did the tradition rank him higher?* Not for his silence but for where he went. For Junayd, intoxication is a passing experience at the first steps of the road (L13 ¶14–15, paraphrased); annihilation is not the goal, and remaining (*baqaʾ*) is "a new life in God" (L13 ¶29, checked), in which "man becomes more aware of himself in the life in God" (L13 ¶28, checked; the garbled tail of ¶28 is cut). **The Sheikh's image (L13 ¶29), given as his account:** explorers who reach a river after thousands of kilometres think it the end of the world; better-equipped travellers cross it and find "many, many other things" beyond. In the Sheikh's account Bayazid stopped at the river, preferring intoxication because it "obliterates the human attributes" (¶28, checked), and Junayd was "probably" the first to cross (L13 ¶29, "Junayd was the first one probably", checked). *(Callback: this is the sobriety Ch 12 found "loftier" than effacement, in words; `light` p. 57 not re-quoted.)*
9. *What does the sober traveller do with what he saw?* He keeps it in allusion and goes on praying. Sayyid Haydar Amuli records Junayd: "Uncertainties have vanished and all signs have disappeared; the only thing of benefit to us is the prayer we perform in the depth of the night." (`inner_secrets` p. 162, checked). *(Quote answers: sobriety ends in the night prayer, which §3 step 4 will need.)*
10. Therefore: of those who reached annihilation, the drunk stayed lost in it and spoke; the sober came back, kept its secret in allusion, kept the night prayer, and went further.
- Hand-on question (worked into the last sentence or the next section's first), stated as the tension it is (TONE 37): the sober kept what they saw a secret; yet the greatest of their heirs wrote it down as a theory. How can both be true? (§2 step 5 resolves it: the theory is written, but only those with the experience can read it.)

## §2 A theory of what they saw
**Point: Ibn ʿArabi gave what they saw a theory, the unity of being: there is one existence, and creation is its real manifestation, neither God nor apart from Him, known by experience rather than argument.**

1. *Who put it into words?* Ibn ʿArabi (d. 638/1240, Damascus, `light` p. 35, checked), whom Mutahhari calls "certainly the greatest mystic of Islam" (`light` p. 34, checked; the source reads "is certainly the greatest mystic of Islam"); "the mystics who came after him ate the crumbs from his table" (`light` p. 35, checked). For Shia and Sunni mystics alike he is the Greatest Shaykh (L13 ¶33). *(Callback: his childhood vision of Surah Yasin, told in Ch 5, in a clause. The lecture's line of theory from al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi, and the "mysticism of knowledge" it grew from, L13 ¶3, ¶12, go in the §3 box, not here: a fourth leaning named in passing would raise a question the chapter does not answer.)*
2. *What did he say they saw?* The unity of being (*wahdat al-wujud*): there is only one existence, and all existing things are its manifestations, states and specifications (L13 ¶60, paraphrased; the transcript's wording is garbled). Others had alluded to it; he made it a theory (L13 ¶55). Reader told: the theory is clearest set beside the others (box).
   - **History box, "Four views of existence"** (L13 ¶56–62; `.box-history`, since the box compares schools rather than a word's root). Its point, stated in its first or last line: each view sets God and creation closer than the last, and the unity of being is the last step, one past where reasoning stopped. (1) The Peripatetics: many existences, each thing with its own, God's beside them. (2) The Illuminationists: one existence, God's; other beings are essences that take existence from it. (3) Mulla Sadra and the old Persian sages: one existence in grades, like sunlight and a small lamp, "all of them are light" (¶59, checked: "but all of them are light"). (4) Ibn ʿArabi: one existence, and everything else its manifestation. Sadra came closest to reasoning it out, through gradation (*tashkik*) (¶62). *(Callback: Ch 2's box named the first two schools and Sadra's "rationalised mysticism"; one clause.)*
3. *If there is one existence, where is creation?* The Sheikh puts the question to his listeners: when God created, is creation outside Him, a new existence beside Him, or a manifestation of Him (L13 ¶60–61)? The theory's answer, as he reports it: "you cannot say that there's anything besides god" (L13 ¶61, checked; attributed to the theory, "It states that…", not to the Sheikh himself). Verse block (answers): Q 57:3 `words="1-5"` "He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden" (Qaraʾi, `quran.py 57:3`, checked; Arabic held; words 1–5 are "هو الأول والآخر والظاهر والباطن", verified). Next sentence follows from "the Manifest": what we see is His manifestation, and it is not He (L13 ¶65, paraphrased: the transcript's "You see His manifestation but he's not him" is garbled in its pronouns, LESSONS 33).
4. *Then am I God, or am I nothing?* Neither; reader told "neither" first. (Moved up from fifth place: this is the reader's urgent question after "nothing beside God", and the chapter must answer it before anything else.)
   - a. Not God: "certainly I'm not god or a part of god" (L13 ¶63, checked; curly apostrophe in the transcript); God gave existence to beings whose limits He planned, so "I have gained existence from him" (L13 ¶64, checked). *(Ch 12 tasted this in "no power except through God"; recalled in a clause only, not re-explained.)*
   - b. Not nothing: "It is not illusion. It is more real than you think." (L13 ¶66, checked).
   - c. Imam ʿAli (as) holds both at once, in the oration Ch 2 quoted for "soaring thoughts", and the Sheikh himself reaches for this saying here (¶64): "He is with all things but not by association. He is other than all things but not by detachment." (`nahj` 1.1.1, PDF p. 122, checked; Arabic [VERIFY]: the extract's p. 121 Arabic is garbled). The verse and the oration held it as statements; Ibn ʿArabi, the Sheikh says, made them a theory, and after him readers saw the theory in them (L13 ¶65–66, paraphrased; the Sheikh's own loose wording of the saying is replaced by the held text). *(Quote answers "neither", and shows the teaching is not new; the old separate step "Is this a new teaching?" is folded here.)*
   - d. Ayatullah Khomeini's image holds both: "the waves of the sea are not separate from the sea itself, but still the sea is not the waves, although the waves are the sea." (`light` p. 144, checked; Part 3 of *Light* is his, confirmed in the extract), with his own caution: "even this similitude is not perfect, for no similitude can properly illustrate the relation between Allah and His creation." (`light` p. 144, checked). *(Theological care, TONE 33: the image is offered as imperfect.)*
5. *Why can't it simply be proved?* It is "above the rank of reason": "you can never rationalise this theory" (L13 ¶67, checked); the mind "is not set to understand", only "hard experience" (L13 ¶66, checked). *(Callback: Ch 2, knowing about God is not knowing Him; one clause.)* This resolves §1's hand-on tension: Ibn ʿArabi wrote the theory down, but writing it did not give it away, since without the experience it cannot be understood (¶62: "you cannot understand this theory of Wahdat ul Wujud… except through experience", paraphrased, no ellipsis in prose; Ayatullah Khomeini says the same of its study, `forty_hadith` p. 345, used in §3). So the secret kept by the sober and the theory written by their heir are the same thing seen from two sides.
6. *Then who can check it?* **Told story (D47, #53), prose, the Sheikh's account (L13 ¶42–43).** He read a book of ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's on the names of God and was amazed at the analysis; then, reading Ibn ʿArabi's *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*, he found it all there. Tabatabaʾi never claimed it as his own: he loved Ibn ʿArabi and, as Ayatullah Hasanzadeh Amuli reports, said he had learned it from him; his own experiences "matched very much" Ibn ʿArabi's (L13 ¶43, checked). The Sheikh's conclusion: "it's not for us to judge because it needs someone who has the experience" (L13 ¶43, checked). The book is not named (the Sheikh does not name it). *(Callback: Tabatabaʾi's line of teachers, Ch 7, in a clause.)* **Two Amulis in one chapter:** write "Sayyid Haydar Amuli" and "Ayatullah Hasanzadeh Amuli" in full at every mention; never a bare "Amuli" (TONE 28).
7. *Why do Shia masters use his language?* His theory matched many mystics' experiences, and they "found no better language" for them (L13 ¶50, checked: "they found no better language than the language of Ibne Arabi"); his influence runs through Mulla Sadra, Ayatullah Khomeini and the Shia masters of Iran today (L13 ¶45–47, ¶51). *(This step hands to §3: the reliance itself drew critics, ¶50's "despite… an insult to some Shia idea".)*
8. Therefore: Ibn ʿArabi gave what the sober saw a theory: one existence, creation its real manifestation, neither God nor apart from Him, known by those who have the experience rather than by argument.

## §3 The critics' concerns, and the answers
**Point: The critics feared that the ʿurafaʾ were not sincere Muslims, that their God was merged with the world, and that the law was set aside; the masters' own words and lives answer each fear, and only Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties stay open.**
*(Fulfils the prologue: "Chapter Fourteen sets out their wider concerns and the answers given to them." Scoped per FRAMEWORK §9 (D41): the concerns as the held defenders report them; no critical source is held. "Not sincere Muslims" replaces "a path outside Islam": whether the road came from outside is Ch 13's question, answered there.)*

1. *Who has been wary of all this, and why?* Murtada Mutahhari sorts the critics. One group of traditionists and jurists held that the ʿurafaʾ "are not practically bound to Islam", and that their citing of the Qur'an and Sunna "are merely a ruse to deceive the simple-minded people" (`light` p. 13, both checked); another, modernist group welcomed ʿirfan as a flight from Islam and its law (`light` p. 13, paraphrased; its "brought from outside" claim belongs to Ch 13, recalled in a clause). *(Callback: Ch 1's jurists who hold the obligations are the whole religion, and the prologue's fear that mysticism sets the law aside; one clause each.)* The concerns come down to three: sincerity, God and the world, and the law, with a fourth that a Shia reader would raise about Ibn ʿArabi himself (counts said naturally, TONE 46).
2. *Were the ʿurafaʾ sincere?* Mutahhari takes a third, neutral view: they may have made mistakes and innovations, more in practice than in theory, as theologians, philosophers and jurists have; "It is quite possible that they have made mistakes" (`light` p. 14, checked), but never from bad faith: "I do not believe that the 'urafa' have had evil intentions towards Islam." (`light` p. 14, checked). He asks for careful study by those who know both ʿirfan and Islam's teachings (`light` p. 14, paraphrased).
3. *Does the unity of being make the world God, or put God inside a man?* §2 has answered the first half: creation is not God or a part of Him. ʿAllama Tabatabaʾi's teaching, as the *Kernel* records it, answers the second. In words: the traveller who imagines that God has united with his own ego has fallen into *hulul* and *ittihad*, "which are kufr and shirk" (`kernel` p. 84, checked; quoted as a whole clause, no ellipsis), whereas the unity of being denies that anything stands beside God at all: "And how far is this from the notions of hulul and ittihad." (`kernel` p. 84, checked; the sentence on existents as "imaginary… shadows" is not quoted, see Where scholars differ). Sayyid Haydar Amuli in one clause: the gnostics are "far removed from such false assumptions and lying fantasies" (`inner_secrets` p. 163, checked; the list of accusations on that page is not repeated, LESSONS 25). *(Ch 12 quoted the same Tabatabaʾi warning from `light` p. 102; recalled in a clause; the new point here is the distinction from the unity of being.)*
   - a. Ayatullah Khomeini's rebuke to accusers (`forty_hadith` p. 345): God may ask, of one who never studied the unity of being, "Then why did you blindly accuse them of unbelief and insult them?" (checked). Context in words: he saw Mulla Sadra called a heretic and "a Sufi through and through", though Sadra wrote a book refuting the Sufis (`forty_hadith` p. 402, the quoted phrase checked; the page says "we call him", a rebuke to his own community, so "he saw" is fair). Even-handed: he puts the same question to a mystic who belittles the jurists, since "None of the scholars of any one of these sciences has a right to find fault with the others." (`forty_hadith` p. 345, checked; the question to the mystic runs on to p. 346). *(D20: both sides, gently; keeps faith with the prologue's respect for the jurists and the marjaʿ.)*
4. *Did the ecstatics set the law aside? (the prologue's chief fear)* The tradition judged its own ecstatics, as §1 showed (recalled in a clause, with one quotation): the ʿurafaʾ "say that the statements of al-Hallaj and Bayazid that gave the impression of unbelief were made when they were beside themselves in the state of 'intoxication'" (`light` p. 27, checked; `light` p. 25's "beside himself in ecstasy" now sits in §1 step 3, so it is not repeated). Words spoken out of one's mind are no rule for a sober believer (recalls Ch 12's "unacceptable" claim that duties are lifted, in a clause). The masters lived by the law, the drunk as well as the sober: Junayd's night prayer (§1 step 9, recalled); Sayyid Haydar Amuli counts Bayazid among those who prayed many cycles each day and night (`inner_secrets` p. 162, paraphrased; the same page's claim that he served in Imam al-Sadiq's house is left out, since Mutahhari denies it on dates, `light` p. 25); and Ibn ʿArabi "would pray two cycles of prayer for every prophet and saint, after performing all the obligatory cycles of prayers" (`inner_secrets` p. 162, checked). *(Callback: Ch 6's "to the last breath", one clause; its quotations are not repeated.)*
5. *But was Ibn ʿArabi one of us?* The Sheikh: "It is difficult to say that he was a Shia"; he respected the Imams (as) and also the first two caliphs (L13 ¶47, paraphrased), and some of his passages and visions go against Shia belief (L13 ¶48–49, ¶52, generalised; the Rajabiyyun passage and the vision of the caliphs are not described). Friends called him the Perfect Saint, enemies "the Killer of the Faith" (`light` p. 35, checked: "Mumit al-Din (the Killer of the Faith)"). Reader told: the Sheikh answers in three ways, **in one paragraph** (folded from three, for length): experience cannot settle which school is right, "This Shia Sunni business should only be solved by rational arguments rather than by experiences." (L13 ¶48, checked); visions take the shape of the theology a person already holds (L13 ¶53, paraphrased); and "you cannot 100% accept someone or reject" (L13 ¶52, checked), for "Certainly he had something valuable in what he wrote." (L13 ¶53, checked).
   - a. Side by side (D20), one paragraph: the chain of his order runs, "Through one intermediary link", to ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Gilani (`light` p. 34, checked); Sayyid Haydar Amuli reads Ibn ʿArabi's own words on the Seal of the Saints as pointing to Imam ʿAli (as) (`inner_secrets` p. 99, paraphrased; the extract reads "the absolute Seal of the Saints, is the Commander of the Faithful", checked); the Sheikh, weighing a book that argues he was Shia against one that argues he was a Sunni, finds the Sunni case "much much more powerful", because "we shouldn't be unjust" (L13 ¶54–55, both checked). So a Shia reader takes the theory, which the masters tested by experience (§2 step 6), without taking its author's loyalties (the book's inference from the Sheikh's "cannot 100% accept or reject", marked as such if needed).
   - **Who's Who box: al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi and the Seal of the Saints** (L13 ¶3–12; `inner_secrets` pp. 98–99). Its point, stated: the mystics' theory grew close to what Shia hold of the Imams, then went past it. A third-century mystic-philosopher, not the hadith compiler (¶4), of the line that put knowledge (*maʿrifa*) before fear, renunciation and love (¶3; moved here from §2 step 1), and "a turning point" in bringing theory into mysticism, from whom many of Ibn ʿArabi's ideas came (¶12, checked: "a turning point"). As there is a Seal of the Prophets, he spoke of a Seal of the Saints (¶5), with a hierarchy of saints who keep the world in order (¶6–8, no numbers). The Sheikh: the same ideas Shia hold of the Imams, taken "to very extreme type of concept which is not even acceptable by Shia" (¶9, checked). Ibn ʿArabi took up the Seal at length; side by side: some read him, as the Sheikh reports, as "talking more about himself than others" (¶49, checked; ¶5's "he regarded himself" is ambiguous between Tirmidhi and Ibn ʿArabi, so ¶49 is used instead, D39), and the Sheikh adds, "We cannot judge what he meant by this" (¶6, checked), whereas Sayyid Haydar Amuli reads the absolute Seal as Imam ʿAli (as) (`inner_secrets` p. 99). *(Ties to step 5's question about his loyalties; the pole, *qutb*, is left to Ch 15.)*
6. Therefore (section and chapter): the critics' fears have answers in the masters' own words and lives: the ʿurafaʾ were sincere though fallible; the unity of being is neither pantheism nor God dwelling in a man; ecstatic words gave no licence, and drunk and sober alike kept the law; and Ibn ʿArabi's own loyalties stay open, so his theory is taken without them. With §1 and §2: of those who reached annihilation the sober went furthest, and the theory of what they saw, though contested, is neither pantheism nor licence.
- **Hand-off (from §3 step 5a):** the chain of Ibn ʿArabi's order ran back, through one link, to ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Gilani, whose order Chapter Fifteen meets; by his time the road was walked in orders, each with its chain of shaykhs. When you recite or hear Surah al-Hadid this week (one of the Musabbihat of Chapter Eight's Try this), stop at its third verse, "He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden". Then, once a day, when something in creation stops you (a face, a sky, an act of kindness), recall the Sheikh's reading: what you see is His manifestation, but it is not He (Q 57:3; L13 ¶64–65). Sourced act: the verse, with the lecture's meaning.
- Decided (Fable): keep this, not the night-prayer alternative. It echoes the chapter's point and the verse, and Junayd's night prayer already does its work in §1 step 9 and §3 step 4. The wording must keep the Sheikh's second half ("but it is not He") so that no reader takes a creature for God.
- The outline's Try this (*la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah*) is dropped: Ch 12 §2 already makes it the lived truth of annihilation in action. §2 step 4a recalls it in a clause.

## Where scholars differ (D20, side by side, gently)
- Hallaj: the jurists pronounced him an apostate; the ʿurafaʾ remember him as a martyr and blame him only for revealing secrets (`light` p. 27); the Sheikh calls his statements "very unacceptable" (L13 ¶15). §1 step 5.
- Bayazid's fourth day: the *Kernel*'s question ("What do you want?") is used; the Sheikh's voice ("you are not strong enough…", L13 ¶18) is not, since no held source has it. Both end in wanting only God. §1 step 2.
- Bayazid and remaining: Mutahhari says Bayazid was first to speak of both annihilation and subsistence (`light` p. 25); the Sheikh says he stopped at intoxication (L13 ¶26, ¶28–29). Worded as preference and as the Sheikh's account: Bayazid preferred intoxication because it "obliterates the human attributes" (¶28), Junayd held the sober state higher; "stopped at the river" stays the Sheikh's image, attributed. §1 steps 1 and 8.
- Creation's reality: the Sheikh, "not illusion… more real than you think" (L13 ¶66); Tabatabaʾi calls every existence beside God's "a mere shadow" in comparison with His (`light` p. 103; `kernel` p. 84, "imaginary… shadows"); Khomeini: the waves "do not exist independently" (`light` p. 144). All deny that anything stands on its own beside God; they differ in emphasis. **Decided (Fable): no parenthesis in the body.** The prose uses Khomeini's image and the Sheikh's line; the *Kernel* is quoted only on hulul and ittihad. A parenthesis would open a question the chapter cannot close for a newcomer (no new questions, CLAUDE.md).
- The Seal of the Saints and Ibn ʿArabi's loyalties: the Sheikh (reporting others) and Sayyid Haydar Amuli read him differently (box; §3 step 5a).
