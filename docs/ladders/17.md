**Title:** The return to God

**Chapter point (book chain line 17, sharpened; proposed):** Every soul returns to God by giving back what it was given, willingly or not; ʿirfan is making that return willingly, now.
*(Book chain: "Every soul returns to God, willingly or not; ʿirfan is making that return willingly, now." The added clause names what the return is, so that "willingly" has an object. If not approved, the book chain wording stands and nothing below changes.)*

**What the close must also land:** B5 ("the road is for God, and it brings the traveller back to people and to Him") and the book's thesis ("…and it ends not in withdrawal but in return, to people with God, and to God Himself"). The prologue promises: "Chapter Seventeen then follows every soul on its own return to God."

**Section chain (each depends only on the ones before; MECE: how to read → what the return is → the return made → the return refused → the return made now):**
1. **Reading from the inside** [17.1]: The Qur'an's images of the next world carry an inner meaning, read only where it agrees with the outer meaning and never cancels it.
2. **Given in order, returned in reverse** [17.2]: Every soul returns to God by the road it came, giving back in reverse what it was given.
3. **Will, power, knowledge, existence** [17.2]: Each gift given back becomes a station on the way to God, and God gives each back without limit.
4. **The return refused** [17.3]: Hell is the same return, undergone against one's will.
5. **Going back now** [conclusion]: ʿIrfan is making that return willingly, in this life.

(§2) → What does giving each thing back look like, and what is left of me? (§3) → What if I have not given them back by the time I die, and what of a soul that never does? The Mafatih recommends it: "If this surah is recited at the head of an ailing person…", and Imam al-Sadiq (as) calls it "the heart of Quran" (Mafatih p. 21, checked). ## §1 Reading from the inside [17.1]
**Point: The Qur'an's images of the next world carry an inner meaning, read only where it agrees with the outer meaning and never cancels it.**

1. *What does the surah at the bedside actually say?* Near its start is an image no one forgets. **[Q36:8, block: "Indeed We have put iron collars around their necks, which are up to the chins, so their heads are upturned."]** *(Raises: no one walks like this.)* No unbeliever walks with his chin forced up, and no one takes the verse literally: "No one would take literally" (L16 ¶10–11; the Sheikh's aside on the "very stringent mufaseroon" and the Throne verse is left out: it is a jab at other commentators and raises a question the chapter does not answer). Its meaning lies inside it: the light of God is covered for them, so they walk without seeing what is before them or behind (L16 ¶12; Q 36:9, "so they do not see", in a clause).
2. *If a verse can mean more than its words, can it mean anything at all?* No. Two readings are excluded: a meaning given "at the will of some mystic" that has no tie to the verse, and a scheme brought from outside and laid over every verse (L16 ¶3–5; Qadi al-Nuʿman not named, FRAMEWORK §8.11). Ayatullah Khomeini sets the same guard: "Islam does not allow anybody to interpret the Qur'an according to his personal opinion or private judgment", and he counts among such interpreters those who give the verses "some spiritual meaning" to suit their own wishes (`light` p. 110, checked). *(Answers the worry.)*
3. *Then what reading is allowed?* "a type of insightful look at the verses of the Quran" (L16 ¶5): the inner meaning must agree with the outer and never go against it, and the outer meaning stays; meaning has layers, each with a deeper one beneath (L16 ¶7–8, ¶15, ¶55; the rule given as the Sheikh's, with no Ghazali or Mizan attribution, per the outline). Its model is a parable (*mathal*) such as the Verse of Light, which Chapter Two read (L16 ¶6; Q 24:35 recalled in words, not re-quoted). Mirza Jawad Maliki Tabrizi guards the other side: treating the inner meanings as false "also closes the path of Qur'anic understanding" (`journey` p. 54, checked; paraphrase the head of his sentence, quote its end). *(Word Study box: taʾwil and tafsir.)*
4. *Does the rule hold for a verse about the next world?* The man led to hell is bound **[Q69:32, block, whole verse: "Then bind him in a chain, seventy cubits in length."]** *(Raises: a real chain, or the collar again?)* If the collar of Surah Yasin is not seen on unbelievers here, the chain there may also be "something else" (L16 ¶16): the long chain of ambitions and ignorance that keeps them from seeing God (L16 ¶15). The Sheikh keeps both: "we cannot deny that it might be the real material type of chain", and "we cannot deny the deeper meanings as well" (L16 ¶15; the two halves of his sentence, each quoted for its own side). His evidence that the inner reading carries over into the next world is the Qur'an's own pairing of the two: whoever is blind here is blind there (Q 17:72, which Chapter Four quoted, recalled in a clause), yet the people of hell are not sightless, since each group curses the one before it (Q 7:38, cited in words; L16 ¶16–17). So the blindness there is the blindness of here, and the chain there is read like the collar here.
5. Therefore: the Qur'an's images of the next world carry an inner meaning, read only where it agrees with the outer and never cancels it. *(Reader told: the chapter reads the return this way, keeping both.)*

## §2 Given in order, returned in reverse [17.2]
**Point: Every soul returns to God by the road it came, giving back in reverse what it was given.**

1. *Who has read the verses on the return this way, keeping both meanings?* The Sheikh's worked example is Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's reading of hell and paradise, which "doesn't deny the material aspect" and asks why people go to each (L16 ¶17). *(Who's Who box: Tusi.)* The book Tusi's reading comes from is not named (L16 ¶17's title is garbled).
2. *What is the return?* For Tusi, the return (*maʿad*) is going back the way we came: "it's a reverse type of process" (L16 ¶18). The verse at the head of the chapter says the same, "so will you return" (Q 7:29, recalled in words; L16 ¶19).
3. *How, then, did we come?* We were given four things, in the order a child receives them. *(Reader told: "four gifts, in the order a child receives them".)*
   - a. Existence first: from dust, a drop, a clinging mass, to an infant (Q 40:67 cited, not quoted; L16 ¶20).
   - b. Then knowledge: born knowing nothing, then given hearing, eyesight and hearts (Q 16:78 cited, "while you did not know anything" may be quoted inline; L16 ¶21).
   - c. Then power: a baby can do nothing, then shouts or crawls for what it wants, and its power grows (L16 ¶22).
   - d. Then will: knowing it has power, it wants something and goes and gets it (L16 ¶22–23).
4. *How do we go back?* By the same road in reverse: will first, then power, then knowledge, then existence (L16 ¶22–23). Not at the start of life: for years we grow in all four, and then God asks us to go the other way, gradually (L16 ¶23).
5. Therefore: every soul returns to God by the road it came, giving back in reverse order its will, its power, its knowledge and its existence.

## §3 Will, power, knowledge, existence [17.2]
**Point: Each gift given back becomes a station on the way to God, and God gives each back without limit.**

1. *What does giving back the will look like?* Contentment (*rida*): with no will of one's own, one is content with whatever comes from God (L16 ¶23–24). Ayatullah Khomeini explains the pivotal term: the one who has reached it "is one who has annihilated his will in the Divine Will, having no more a separate will of his own"; asked his wish, a mystic replied, "My wish is not to wish at all" (`forty_hadith` p. 207, checked). *(Pivotal term from a held source, TONE 47.)*
2. *Does a soul with no will of its own want nothing in paradise?* The opposite. **[Q50:35, block: "There they will have whatever they wish, and with Us there is yet more."]** *(Answers.)* When one's will is God's will, whatever one wishes one has; the will is given back, everlasting and joined to His (L16 ¶24–25). The Sheikh adds Tusi's observation that the keeper of paradise is named Ridwan, from the same root as *rida*, and that paradise is full of treasures, "And the key to it is this contentment" (L16 ¶25; the angel's name is the tradition's, not the Qur'an's, so gloss it as the Sheikh's). *(Image that echoes the point: the key. Q 9:72 dropped: a second verse for a step that already has one; the root link is carried by Ridwan here and by *radiya* at §5.6.)*
3. *What does giving back power look like?* Reliance (*tawakkul*): knowing that the power we use is God's. The Sheikh raises the misreading himself (so the "not" stays): it does not mean sitting and waiting for God to act; rather, "whatever you do you know that this is not you. This is him" (L16 ¶28). Held gloss: *tawakkul* literally means "admission of one's inability and reliance on one other than oneself" (`forty_hadith` p. 204, checked). Q 65:3 inline: "whoever puts his trust in Allah, He will suffice him" (L16 ¶28). Chapter Twelve's annihilation in action (*la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah*) recalled in one clause, with the difference kept so it is not a retelling: there the traveller at the summit *sees* that the power is God's; here *tawakkul* is the station on the way, where he acts on it before he sees it.
4. *What does giving back knowledge look like?* Submission (*taslim*): knowing that we know nothing of ourselves, that only He knows and He gives what knowledge we have (L16 ¶28–29). It is Ibrahim's (as) submission. **[Q2:131, block: "When his Lord said to him, 'Submit,' he said, 'I submit to the Lord of all the worlds.'"]** (L16 ¶30). Its voice in prayer: "O my God, I am ignorant despite my knowledge" (Duʿa ʿArafah §670, attributed "as given in the Mafatih", FRAMEWORK §8.2; checked in `duas-dua-arafah-imam-husain.md`; Arabic held).
5. *And existence?* Annihilation (*fanaʾ*): existence belongs to God alone (L16 ¶30). Chapter Twelve walked this in full; one sentence here.
6. *If all four are given back, what is left of the traveller?* All four are given back by God, new and without limit: full contentment; power without limit, because the soul is now "drowned" in God's power and joined to it; knowledge that "will shine in you"; and everlasting existence, "the day of immortality" (Q 50:34 inline) (L16 ¶31–32). *(Keep the Sheikh's "joined with that power", ¶31: the soul uses God's power, it does not become it.)*
7. *Is this the Sheikh's gloss, or Tusi's own teaching?* Tusi's own words, from his commentary on Ibn Sina's *Isharat*, as Ayatullah Khomeini quotes them: the gnostic "sees all powers as vanishing in Divine power and all sciences as drowned in Divine knowledge and all wills as vanishing in His will… At this time God, the Exalted, becomes his hearing and sight, power, knowledge and existence." (`forty_hadith` p. 517, checked; Khomeini's n. 27: *Sharh al-Isharat*, vol. 3, p. 389, namat 9.) Khomeini sets the passage down only after rejecting any incarnation or union of God and servant (p. 517, the lines before the citation): one clause of that guard in the prose, so the reader is not left with "becomes" alone. *(Split from step 6: two quotations and a verse in one paragraph was too much; this step is the held source's confirmation of the Sheikh's account. Moved here from §2 in the outline: the quotation describes the giving back and the re-giving, not the order.)*
8. Therefore: given back willingly, will, power, knowledge and existence become contentment, reliance, submission and annihilation, and God gives each back without limit, which is paradise (L16 ¶31).

## §4 The return refused [17.3]
**Point: The return happens whether or not we make it: a believer who left it undone finishes it after death, with difficulty; a will that never yields meets the same four gifts as their opposites, which is hell, and it is not God's revenge.**
*(Fable: the earlier point, "Hell is the same return, undergone against one's will", covered only steps 3–6 and mis-described steps 1–2, where the believer's involuntary completion ends in paradise, not hell. ARGUMENT 17.3 keeps its approved wording; this is the section's point as the steps actually prove it.)*

1. *What if I have not given these back by the time I die?* The return still happens, "either voluntarily or involuntarily" (L16 ¶39). A believer who has not made it here makes it in barzakh and on the Day of Resurrection, with "lots of fears, lots of difficulty" (L16 ¶39–40): stripped of the will, shown that there is no power and no life but God's (L16 ¶41). Chapter Five's barzakh recalled in a clause.
2. *Can a believer get through that alone?* No: the Sheikh holds that paradise is out of our reach without intercession (*shafaʿa*), a level that needs someone great to take us there (L16 ¶34, paraphrased). The believers' light is incomplete, and they pray **[Q66:8, block, words 34–38: "They will say, 'Our Lord! Perfect our light for us'"]** *(answers)*; the Prophet ﷺ completes it, since on that day God "will not let down the Prophet and the faithful who are with him" (Q 66:8 inline, words 21–28; L16 ¶37–39). *(The Sheikh's hinge is the verb he renders "humiliate": for the Prophet's own followers to go to hell would shame him, so their light is completed through him. Qaraʾi's "let down" is softer, so the prose must state his reading in a clause, as his; the verse alone does not carry it.)*
3. *And a will that never turns?* God's will prevails over it. **[Q23:71, block, words 1–9: "Had the truth followed their desires, the heavens and the earth would have surely fallen apart [along] with those who are in them."]** *(Answers.)* So that will is held against itself, "always in torment" (L16 ¶42–43). Here is §1's chain: it is seventy cubits long "because of the length of the will that they have" (L16 ¶43). *(Callback to §1; image echoes the point.)*
4. *And the other three gifts?* Each comes back as its opposite, the mirror of §3 (L16 ¶45–47, ¶54–55). *(Reader told: "the mirror of the four".)* In place of contentment, utter dissatisfaction; in place of power, helplessness, since they never learned that the power they used was God's (¶45–46); in place of submission, humiliation, finding themselves ignorant who thought they knew (¶46); in place of union, separation from God, and an existence that is neither life nor death (¶47, ¶55): **[Q87:13, block: "then he will neither live in it, nor die."]**
5. *Is hell, then, God's revenge?* "It is not a punishment which is brought to you by God. God doesn't want to revenge." (L16 ¶47–48, two sentences, quoted whole). There is only mercy; the soul brings its separation back with it (L16 ¶48). The Sheikh keeps the wrath real and deserved in the same breath, "they will deserve the wrath of God" (¶43), so the prose keeps both: the wrath is real, and it is not revenge. *(Both sides of the theology: God's will prevails, step 3; the separation is the soul's own, step 5; the wrath is the Qur'an's and the Sheikh's, not softened away. Nothing acts upon God. Gentle wording, as his view, "in the Sheikh's account"; no claim about hell beyond ¶42–48 and `forty_hadith` p. 137.)*
6. *Then are the fire and the boiling water only pictures?* "of course it's all true" (L16 ¶49). One reality takes a true form in each world: what is fire in this world is something else in the deeper worlds (L16 ¶51–52; Q 4:10, orphans' property eaten as fire, cited in words). Chapter Five's "some rewards are the deeds themselves made visible" recalled in a clause (its Ibn ʿArabi story is not retold). The Sheikh's model for two true forms of one thing is a dream and its meaning: "in dreams you see something while in reality it is something else. Both of them are right" (L16 ¶54, his general sentence on dreams; said in the course of a Ghazali example that is left out, see Cut). *(Story box, D47: Yusuf reads the king's dream, L04 ¶81–83; Q 12:43–49 cited. The box carries the step's point: the lean cows and the years of famine are one thing in two worlds, and the one who knows reads the one from the other.)*
7. *Do the scholars agree?* Side by side, as agreement (D20): Ayatullah Khomeini writes that "The Hereafterly form of our anger in this world is the fire of Divine Wrath in the next world", and keeps both fires: "The fire of the hell of deeds and the physical hell encompasses the without and travels towards the within" (`forty_hadith` p. 137, both checked).
8. *How should a believer carry this?* With fear and hope together, as the Munajat does. **[M120, quote block: "My Lord, if Your severe punishment calls me to Hell, the abundance of Your reward invites me to Paradise." `light` p. 166; Arabic `duas-munajat-shabaniyah.md` §120–121, held.]**
9. Therefore: hell is the same return, undergone against one's will; a believer who has not finished is helped through it by mercy.

## §5 Going back now [conclusion]
**Point: ʿIrfan is making that return willingly, in this life.**

1. *If every soul makes the return anyway, why make it now?* Najm al-Din Kubra, whose ship and pearl Chapter Six used, gives the Sheikh's answer: God has set two journeys for the children of Adam, one involuntary (*qahri*) and one voluntary (*ikhtiyari*) (L08 ¶6). Everyone meets the Lord, through death, barzakh and the resurrection, on a journey "out of your control"; the mystic's path, *suluk*, is the same meeting made by choice, "a shortcut" (L08 ¶7). (The Sheikh's flagged slip in Kubra's quotation, ¶5–6, is left out; no verse cited, D39.)
2. *What does the shortcut gain?* Chapter Four gave one reason in a sentence: the involuntary journey is longer and harder (L08 ¶8, recalled in words; Ch 4 already carries Kubra's pair unnamed, so §5.1–2 names it and adds only what Ch 4 did not). Kubra adds that while others are still on it, those who took the voluntary road have already arrived and live beside God (L08 ¶8, paraphrased; the garbled middle left out). The Sheikh reads a verse of Surah al-Anbiyaʾ of those who finished here (L16 ¶40; his application, stated as his): **[Q21:103, block, words 1–4: "The Great Terror will not upset them"]** *(answers; numbering corrected from the lecture's "57:103")*.
3. *What, then, is ʿirfan, at the end of the book?* The return of §3 made now and by choice: the will given back as contentment, power as reliance, knowledge as submission, and existence as annihilation, inside the law and beside a guide. *(Chapter point stated once.)*
4. *What words already belong to it?* The words said at the bedside, "to Him do we indeed return" (Q 2:156, recalled from the opening). We say them of another, at a death; in the verse itself they are the words of the living, "those who, when an affliction visits them, say" them of themselves (Q 2:156, words 1–5, cited in words or quoted inline from the build). The Qur'an has the living say the return of themselves, now; the traveller means it. *(No "book's reading" needed: the verse's own context does the work.)*
5. *So is there more to the religion than the obligations, and can a believer reach it safely?* (The book's opening question, `00-book-chain.md`, answered in one paragraph of at most 90 words; this is the thesis, TONE 44's closing recap, and the only place the Parts are gathered.) Yes: ʿirfan is that more, worth every believer's pursuit; it travels through the self and never leaves the shariʿa; it is walked in a known order, beside a tested guide; it is rooted in the Qur'an, the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams of his Ahl al-Bayt; and it ends in return, to people with God, as Chapter Sixteen showed, and to God Himself, the road every soul travels. ʿIrfan travels it now. *(Written as the answer to a question the reader brought to the book, not as a tour; no chapter numbers except Sixteen; no sermon, no closing remark after the verse that follows.)*
6. *Where does the willing return end?* Surah al-Fajr calls such a soul home, and its word for "pleased" (*radiya*) is the very word for one who has reached contentment: Ayatullah Khomeini writes of "the radi (one who has attained the station of rida)" (`forty_hadith` p. 207, checked; Arabic of the verse held, 89:28 word 4 رَاضِيَةً), the first gift given back; this is the self at peace of Chapter Five. **[Q89:27–28, block: "O soul at peace! Return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing!"]** *(Last line of the chapter body; nothing after the verse but the one-line hand-off.)*

## Boxes (three)
1. **§1 Word Study: *taʾwil* and *tafsir*.** *Tafsir* is commentary on the plain meaning; *taʾwil* draws out the inner meaning; Maliki Tabrizi names them together, "their commentary (tafsir) and esoteric meanings (tawil)" (`journey` p. 54, checked). Point of the box: an inner reading done well exposes one side of the Qur'an and never claims the whole. Ayatullah Khomeini says of the gnostics' commentaries: "what they have written is not the exegesis of the Qur'an. At the most it can be said that they have exposed some aspects of it" (`light` p. 109, checked), and "The Qur'an is not a book all aspects of which may be exposed by us or by anybody else"; what lies beyond us is "to be explained by the Imams" (`light` p. 110, checked).
2. **§2 Who's Who: Khwaja Nasir al-Din al-Tusi** (d. 672/1273, `light` p. 36, checked: "the year in which both Rumi and Khwajah Nasir al-Din died"). Shia philosopher and theologian; his commentary on Ibn Sina's *Isharat* is the one Chapter Eight met (`light` p. 50, recalled); Ayatullah Khomeini calls him "the best of the later scholars and the most perfect among the predecessors" (`forty_hadith` p. 517, checked). The Sheikh adds that Mulla Sadra copied long passages of Tusi's book on the return into the *Asfar* (L16 ¶18). Point: this reading of hell and paradise comes from one of the Shia's most trusted scholars.
3. **§4 Story (D47): Yusuf reads the king's dream** (#6 in `reviews/stories-audit.md`, L04 ¶81–83). The Sheikh's account, told as his: when the soul meets a meaning it has no picture for, it clothes it in one. Knowledge, for instance, purifies, so the sleeper sees an ocean and finds himself swimming in it; an interpreter tells him what the picture answers to, and that, the Sheikh says, is the whole idea of reading dreams. The king of Egypt dreamed of seven lean cows eating seven fat ones (Q 12:43, cited; the Sheikh says "Firaun", the Qur'an says "the king", so write "the king", true on either reading), and the Prophet Yusuf (as) read them as seven years of plenty and seven of famine (Q 12:47–49, cited). The king's own interpreters could not read it (Q 12:44, cited), because, in the Sheikh's words, a careless soul takes "an image which is very weird"; one who knows, like Yusuf, reads the one from the other. Point of the box, in the Sheikh's own sentence from this lecture: "in dreams you see something while in reality it is something else. Both of them are right" (L16 ¶54). The cows and the famine are one thing in two worlds, and so, he says, are the fire here and what it is there. *(Chosen over the Ibn ʿAwf dream: Qur'anic, no weak report, no companion shown unfavourably, clear in the transcript; see Cut and open problem 1.)*

## Try this
Each day this week, read a few verses slowly and take each command and warning "as though all of them have been revealed especially for him" (`journey` p. 46, checked), turning one verse over from several sides, as Maliki Tabrizi does with Surah al-Waqiʿa's "Have you considered the water that you drink?" (Q 56:68, Qaraʾi from the build, not Maliki's translation; his method `journey` pp. 49–50: the plants and animals made from it, the human being made from it, the water as a sign of God's blessing). Framed by the Sheikh's report of a hadith that in paradise the reader is told to recite and go up, read so that one rises by the depth to which the verses are taken in, not by their number (L05 ¶29–30, paraphrased; the hadith not held, [VERIFY] if ever quoted). This ties §1's inner reading to the ascent.

## Where scholars differ (D20)
- **Hell, inner and outer:** agreement, not difference. The Sheikh keeps the material fire ("of course it's all true") beside the inner reading; Ayatullah Khomeini keeps both fires (`forty_hadith` p. 137).
- **Gone around, not raised in prose:** Ayatullah Khomeini ranks the station of *rida* "higher and more luminous" than *tawakkul* (`forty_hadith` p. 207), whereas Tusi's order, as the Sheikh reads it, gives the will back before power. The two need not conflict (one is the order of giving back, the other the height of the station), but the prose makes no ranking: it uses p. 207 only for the definition of *rida*.
